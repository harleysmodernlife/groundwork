## Heating Systems: Furnaces, Boilers, and Heat Pumps

Heating is one of the largest energy expenses in most homes, and HVAC is one of the most in-demand trades. Understanding the different types of heating systems — how they work, their strengths and weaknesses, and how to identify what you're looking at — is the foundation of HVAC work.

---

## Forced Air Furnaces

The most common heating system in US homes. A furnace heats air and distributes it through ductwork to every room.

**How it works:**
1. The thermostat calls for heat
2. The furnace burner (gas, propane, or oil) fires, or electric resistance elements energize
3. Air from the return duct passes over the heat exchanger (gas/oil) or through the elements (electric)
4. The blower fan pushes the warmed air through the supply ducts to registers throughout the house
5. Once the thermostat is satisfied, the burner shuts off; the blower continues briefly to use residual heat

**Efficiency ratings:** Gas furnaces are rated by AFUE (Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency) — the percentage of fuel converted to heat. 80% AFUE means 20% of the fuel's energy escapes up the flue. Standard furnaces: 80% AFUE. High-efficiency (condensing) furnaces: 90-98% AFUE.

**Condensing furnaces:** High-efficiency furnaces extract so much heat from the exhaust gases that the gases condense into water vapor. These exhaust through white PVC pipes (not a metal flue), and have a condensate drain line.

**Components:**
- **Heat exchanger:** The metal chamber where combustion gases heat the air (gas/oil furnaces). A cracked heat exchanger is a carbon monoxide hazard — CO can leak into the airstream.
- **Blower motor:** Circulates air through the system. Also runs for air conditioning.
- **Burner assembly:** Where fuel combustion occurs
- **Inducer motor (high-efficiency):** Pulls combustion gases through the heat exchanger and exhausts them
- **Igniter:** Electronic ignition (hot surface igniter or spark igniter) replaces old pilot lights on modern furnaces

## Boilers (Hydronic Heating)

Boilers heat water (not steam in most modern systems) and circulate it through the house to radiators, baseboard heaters, or radiant floor systems.

**How it works:**
1. Thermostat calls for heat
2. Boiler fires (gas, oil, or electric) and heats water in the vessel
3. A circulator pump moves hot water through the distribution pipes
4. Water gives up heat at each radiator or baseboard unit, warming the space
5. Cooled water returns to the boiler for reheating

**Types of distribution:**
- **Cast iron radiators (steam or hot water):** Older homes. Steam systems use gravity; hot water systems use a pump.
- **Baseboard heaters (hydronic):** Long, low enclosures along walls with fins on a copper pipe. Common in homes from the 1950s-80s.
- **Radiant floor heat:** Tubing in the floor slab or below the subfloor. Very comfortable heat; slow to respond to thermostat changes; excellent for tile floors.

**Boiler pressure and expansion tanks:** Boilers operate at 12-25 PSI. An expansion tank absorbs the increased water volume when water heats up (water expands ~4% when heated from 50°F to 180°F). A failed expansion tank leads to the pressure relief valve constantly opening.

**Boiler vs. furnace advantages:** Hydronic heat is very comfortable (radiant warmth, no dry air, no air movement). Radiators heat evenly. No ductwork (eliminates duct heat losses). Boiler systems are quiet.

**Boiler vs. furnace disadvantages:** Cannot be used for central air conditioning (requires a separate system or ductless AC). More complex to zone. Higher maintenance requirements.

## Heat Pumps

A heat pump moves heat rather than generating it, making it 2-4x more efficient than resistance heating. The same system provides both heating and cooling.

**How it works (heating mode):** A refrigerant cycle moves heat from the outdoor air (even cold air contains heat) and delivers it inside.

1. Refrigerant in the outdoor unit absorbs heat from outside air (even at temperatures as low as -15°F in cold-climate models)
2. The compressor increases the refrigerant pressure, which raises its temperature significantly
3. The hot refrigerant flows to the indoor unit (air handler) and gives up its heat to the indoor air
4. The now-cooled refrigerant flows back outside to absorb more heat
5. The refrigeration cycle continues

In summer, the cycle reverses — the system moves heat from inside to outside. This is how air conditioning works.

**Coefficient of Performance (COP):** A heat pump in mild conditions might deliver 3 kWh of heat for every 1 kWh of electricity — a COP of 3. Compare this to electric resistance heat at COP 1.0 (100% of electricity becomes heat). The efficiency advantage is real and significant.

**Cold climate heat pumps:** Older heat pumps lost efficiency rapidly below 30°F. Modern cold-climate heat pumps (Mitsubishi Hyper Heat, Bosch Inverter, others) maintain high efficiency down to 0°F and can operate at -13°F or lower.

**Types:**
- **Air-source heat pump:** Moves heat between inside and outside air. Most common.
- **Ground-source (geothermal) heat pump:** Uses stable earth temperature (50-55°F) as the heat source/sink. More efficient but expensive to install (requires buried loop field).
- **Mini-split (ductless):** Air-source heat pump without ductwork. An outdoor compressor unit connects to one or more indoor air handlers via refrigerant lines through a small penetration in the wall. Flexible zoning, efficient, good for additions or rooms without ductwork.

## Electric Resistance Heating

Baseboard heaters, wall heaters, and electric furnaces convert electricity directly to heat. Simple and inexpensive to install. Expensive to operate — they're 100% efficient (all electricity becomes heat) but electricity costs more per BTU than gas in most markets.

Suitable for: small spaces, supplemental heating, areas without gas service, mild climates.

## Comparing Systems

| System | Fuel | Distribution | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas furnace | Natural gas / propane | Forced air ducts | Most US climates, existing ductwork |
| Boiler | Gas, oil, or electric | Hydronic (water) | Comfort, older homes with radiators |
| Heat pump | Electricity | Forced air or ductless | Efficiency, all-electric homes, mild-moderate climates |
| Electric resistance | Electricity | Baseboard / wall unit | Supplemental, small spaces |

---

## Fuel Considerations

**Natural gas:** Typically the cheapest heating fuel per BTU in the US. Widely available in urban and suburban areas.

**Propane:** More expensive than natural gas. Requires on-site storage tank. Common in rural areas.

**Heating oil:** Common in the Northeast US. Price volatile. Tank storage required.

**Electricity:** Most expensive per BTU for resistance heating; competitive or cheapest for heat pumps (where 1 kWh can deliver 3+ BTUs of heat effectively).

**The electrification trend:** Heat pumps with improved cold-climate performance are making all-electric heating practical in more climates. Combined with solar panels, they can dramatically reduce operating costs and carbon emissions.
