## Cooling Systems: How Air Conditioners Work

Air conditioning is refrigeration applied to a building. Understanding how the refrigeration cycle works demystifies every cooling system — window units, central AC, mini-splits, and heat pumps all use the same physics.

---

## The Refrigeration Cycle

All cooling systems work by moving heat, not by generating cold. Heat always flows from hot to cold — the refrigeration cycle uses refrigerant and a compressor to move heat from a cool space to a warm one, against the natural direction.

**The four-step cycle:**

1. **Evaporation (indoor unit — the evaporator):** Refrigerant enters the evaporator coil as a cold, low-pressure liquid. Indoor air is blown over the coil. The refrigerant absorbs heat from the indoor air and evaporates into a gas. The indoor air gets cooler and returns to the room.

2. **Compression (the compressor):** The cold low-pressure gas travels to the compressor (outside). The compressor squeezes the gas to high pressure, which also raises its temperature significantly — the gas is now hot and high-pressure.

3. **Condensation (outdoor unit — the condenser):** The hot high-pressure gas flows through the condenser coil (outside). The outdoor fan blows air across the coil. The refrigerant gives up its heat to the outside air and condenses back into a liquid.

4. **Expansion (the expansion valve):** The high-pressure liquid passes through an expansion device (metering valve or orifice) that drops its pressure suddenly. This causes rapid cooling as the refrigerant flashes partially to vapor. The cold refrigerant is ready to absorb indoor heat again.

**The result:** Heat is continuously moved from inside to outside. The indoor space gets cooler; the air outside gets a little warmer (that's why outdoor AC units blow hot air).

## Central Air Conditioning (Split System)

The most common residential cooling system in the US. Called a "split system" because the equipment is split between indoors and outdoors.

**Outdoor unit:** Contains the compressor, condenser coil, and condenser fan. This is the unit you see outside the house, often making a humming sound.

**Indoor unit (air handler):** Contains the evaporator coil and blower. Often installed in the same air handler as the furnace — the same ductwork and blower serve both heating and cooling.

**Refrigerant lines:** Two copper lines (suction line and liquid line) connect the outdoor and indoor units. Insulated with foam — the larger suction line carries cold refrigerant gas and must be insulated to prevent condensation and heat gain.

**The filter:** Located at the return air grille or in the air handler. Filters the recirculated air. Must be changed regularly — a clogged filter is the most common cause of reduced cooling performance and frozen evaporator coils.

## SEER Rating

Central AC units are rated by SEER (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio) — cooling output divided by energy input over a typical cooling season. Higher SEER = more efficient.

- **SEER 13-14:** Older or entry-level units (minimum federal standard has been rising)
- **SEER 16-18:** Mid-efficiency
- **SEER 20+:** High-efficiency

**SEER2:** A newer, more stringent test standard replacing SEER in recent years. SEER2 ratings are slightly lower than equivalent SEER ratings for the same equipment.

Higher efficiency units cost more upfront and save money over time in operating costs. The payback period depends on climate (how many cooling hours), electricity rate, and how long you own the home.

## Mini-Split Systems (Ductless)

A mini-split is a split-system heat pump or AC unit without ductwork. The outdoor compressor unit connects to one or more indoor air handlers (wall-mounted, ceiling cassette, floor-mounted) through a small hole in the wall carrying refrigerant lines and electrical connections.

**Advantages:**
- No ductwork — great for additions, older homes, or rooms that are hard to condition
- Each indoor unit operates independently — zone control without complex damper systems
- High efficiency (SEER 20-30+ for premium units)
- Heat pump models provide both heating and cooling
- Quiet — the compressor is outside, the indoor unit is whisper-quiet

**Disadvantages:**
- Higher upfront cost than window units
- Indoor units are visible on walls or ceilings
- Each indoor unit requires its own refrigerant connection to the outdoor unit
- Professional installation required for refrigerant line connections

**Multi-zone systems:** One outdoor unit can connect to multiple indoor air handlers (2-5 or more). Each indoor unit has its own temperature control.

## Window and Portable Units

**Window units:** Self-contained — the evaporator is inside, the condenser is outside, separated by the window. Single units cool a single room. Cheap and simple; less efficient than central systems; block window light and view.

**Portable units:** Stand on the floor inside the room with an exhaust hose going through a window. Less efficient than window units because they pull in room air to cool the condenser, then exhaust it outside — creating negative pressure that draws in warm outside air. Convenient but thermodynamically compromised.

## Common Cooling Problems

### AC Not Cooling Well

**Dirty filter:** Check and replace. This is the most common cause.

**Dirty outdoor unit:** The condenser coil needs to dissipate heat. If it's clogged with leaves, grass clippings, or debris, it can't. Clean the fins with a garden hose (gently, with the power off).

**Frozen evaporator coil:** A coil coated in ice can't absorb heat. Causes: dirty filter restricting airflow, low refrigerant charge, blower failure. Turn off cooling, let it thaw (blower on, heat off helps), then find the cause.

**Low refrigerant:** Refrigerant doesn't get used up — the system is a closed loop. If refrigerant is low, there's a leak. Requires a licensed HVAC technician with proper tools to locate the leak, repair it, and recharge the system.

### AC Won't Turn On

- Check the thermostat setting and batteries
- Check the circuit breaker (AC units are on dedicated breakers)
- Check the disconnect switch near the outdoor unit (can be pulled as a safety measure)
- Check the float switch on the condensate drain (some systems shut off if the drain overflows)

### AC Running But Outdoor Unit Not Running

The indoor blower runs but the outdoor compressor doesn't. Usually a failed capacitor (a very common failure on older units) or a tripped overload. Capacitors store electrical charge and help start/run the motors — they're inexpensive and replaceable, though they hold dangerous charge even when power is off.

## Refrigerant Types

Refrigerants in use:
- **R-22 (Freon):** The old standard for residential AC. Phased out in the US as of 2020 due to ozone depletion. Existing systems can still be recharged with recovered R-22 but at increasingly high cost.
- **R-410A (Puron):** Replaced R-22. Not compatible with R-22 equipment. Most systems installed since 2006.
- **R-32 and R-454B:** Newer refrigerants phasing in as R-410A is phased down due to high global warming potential.

Handling refrigerants requires an EPA Section 608 certification — it's not a DIY task.
