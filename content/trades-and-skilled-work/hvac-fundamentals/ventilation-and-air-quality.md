## Ventilation and Indoor Air Quality

Modern homes are built tighter than ever — which is great for energy efficiency and terrible for air quality if ventilation isn't addressed intentionally. Understanding how ventilation works and what pollutants matter is essential for anyone working on HVAC systems.

---

## Why Ventilation Matters

Older, leaky homes breathed through gaps, cracks, and poor insulation. Modern construction seals buildings tightly to reduce heating and cooling loads. The result: without intentional ventilation, indoor pollutants accumulate.

Indoor air is often significantly more polluted than outdoor air, containing:
- Carbon dioxide (from occupants breathing)
- Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from furniture, flooring, cleaning products, paint
- Moisture from cooking, bathing, breathing
- Combustion byproducts (if gas appliances are present)
- Dust, allergens, mold spores
- Radon (in some geographic areas)

The building science principle: "Build tight, ventilate right."

---

## Types of Ventilation

### Natural Ventilation

Air movement through windows, doors, and building envelope gaps. Works well in mild climates when the difference between indoor and outdoor conditions allows for comfortable natural airflow.

**Stack effect:** Warm air rises and exits the building at high points (attic, upper floors). Cooler air is drawn in at lower levels. This natural convection creates some ventilation even in closed buildings. In a very tight building, it's minimal.

### Exhaust-Only Ventilation

Fans exhaust stale indoor air out of the building. The resulting negative pressure causes outdoor air to infiltrate through whatever gaps exist.

Common systems: bathroom exhaust fans and kitchen range hoods. These are point-source ventilation — they exhaust air near the source of moisture and pollutants.

**The problem with exhaust-only in tight buildings:** Negative pressure can backdraft combustion appliances (pulling combustion gases back into the living space instead of up the flue), or draw radon and soil gases through foundation gaps.

### Supply-Only Ventilation

A fan draws fresh outdoor air into the building through a dedicated duct. Positive pressure causes indoor air to exfiltrate through gaps.

### Balanced Ventilation (HRV and ERV)

The best solution for tight buildings. A mechanical system provides equal amounts of supply (fresh outdoor air in) and exhaust (stale indoor air out).

**HRV (Heat Recovery Ventilator):** Transfers heat from the outgoing stale air to the incoming fresh air. In winter, the warm exhaust air preheats the cold incoming air, recovering 70-80% of the heat. In summer, transfers heat from incoming hot air to the outgoing cooler air.

**ERV (Energy Recovery Ventilator):** Like an HRV but also transfers moisture. Transfers humidity from outgoing air to incoming air (in winter, adds some humidity to dry incoming air). Better choice in hot-humid climates or very cold climates where you want to retain indoor moisture.

**When to use:**
- HRV: cold climates, homes where humidity control is not a concern
- ERV: hot-humid climates, or cold climates where you want to retain indoor moisture

## Exhaust Fans: Bathrooms and Kitchens

**Bathroom exhaust fans:** Remove moisture and odors. Should vent directly outside — not into the attic (a common but wrong installation that causes attic mold). Should be sized appropriately for the bathroom (measured in CFM — cubic feet per minute).

Sizing: For small bathrooms under 100 sq ft, 1 CFM per square foot as a minimum. For larger bathrooms, use point source calculation.

**Bathroom fan should run:** During and for at least 20 minutes after showering. A timer switch helps with this automatically.

**Kitchen range hood:** Removes grease, moisture, odors, and combustion byproducts from cooking. Recirculating (ductless) hoods filter grease but don't remove moisture or combustion gases — they don't substitute for vented hoods.

A vented range hood should have 100+ CFM for a typical residential range. High-BTU ranges need more ventilation.

## Filtration

Air filters in forced-air systems capture particulates from the recirculated air.

**MERV rating (Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value):** Scale from 1-20. Higher MERV = captures smaller particles.

| MERV | Captures | Typical Use |
|---|---|---|
| 1-4 | Pollen, dust | Fiberglass filters, basic protection |
| 8-11 | Mold spores, pet dander, dust mite debris | Good residential filter |
| 13-16 | Bacteria, smoke, finer particles | High-end residential, medical |
| 17-20 | Viruses, sub-micron particles | HEPA, hospital grade |

**HEPA filters:** True HEPA captures 99.97% of particles 0.3 microns and larger. Not commonly used in central HVAC because they restrict airflow significantly (the system must be designed for them).

**Filter maintenance:** Change filters on schedule — every 30 days for 1" filters, every 90 days for 4-5" media filters. A clogged filter reduces airflow, strains equipment, and defeats the purpose of having a filter.

**Higher MERV isn't always better:** MERV 13+ filters restrict airflow if the system wasn't designed for them. A system designed for MERV 8 with a MERV 13 filter installed may starve the evaporator coil of airflow, reducing efficiency and potentially causing the coil to freeze.

## Humidity Control

**Too little humidity (below 30% RH):** Dry skin, static electricity, wood floors and furniture shrinking and cracking.

**Too much humidity (above 60% RH):** Mold growth, dust mite proliferation, wood swelling, condensation on surfaces.

**Target: 40-60% RH.** Most HVAC systems don't control humidity directly — air conditioning naturally dehumidifies in summer (as air cools, moisture condenses on the evaporator coil), and heating in winter dries the air.

**Standalone dehumidifiers:** For basements or regions with high humidity.

**Whole-home humidifiers:** Installed in the air handler, add moisture to heated air in winter. Types: bypass humidifiers (drip water over a pad), drum humidifiers (rotating drum in a water tray), steam humidifiers (most accurate control). Require periodic cleaning and wick replacement.

## Carbon Monoxide

CO is odorless, colorless, and produced by incomplete combustion from gas appliances, oil furnaces, water heaters, fireplaces, and attached garages.

CO detectors are required in most jurisdictions near sleeping areas. Test them monthly. Replace every 5-7 years (CO sensors degrade).

Any CO alarm reading: evacuate immediately, call 911. Do not re-enter until cleared by the fire department.

The most common cause of CO in homes: cracked heat exchanger in a gas furnace. CO from the combustion side leaks into the air stream and distributes throughout the house.
