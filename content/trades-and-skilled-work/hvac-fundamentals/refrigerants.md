## Refrigerants: Types, Regulations, and Safety

Refrigerant is the working fluid of any air conditioning or heat pump system. It's what actually moves heat. Understanding refrigerant types, the regulatory landscape, and the safety requirements is essential for anyone working in HVAC — and a significant compliance issue in the field.

---

## What Refrigerant Does

Refrigerant circulates through a closed loop, alternately absorbing and releasing heat as it changes between liquid and vapor states. The specific properties of each refrigerant — the temperatures and pressures at which it changes states — determine what equipment it can be used in.

Refrigerant doesn't get "used up." A properly sealed system runs on the same charge for its entire life. Low refrigerant means a leak, period.

## Common Refrigerants

### R-22 (HCFC-22, "Freon")

The standard residential AC refrigerant from the 1960s through the 2000s.

**Phase-out:** R-22 was phased out in the US under the Clean Air Act due to its high ozone-depleting potential (ODP). As of January 1, 2020, production and import of new R-22 is banned in the US. Existing equipment can be serviced with recovered and recycled R-22 — but supply is limited and prices have risen dramatically.

**What this means for older systems:** An R-22 system that leaks is becoming increasingly expensive to service. Replacement with modern equipment using R-410A or newer refrigerants is often the economical choice when R-22 systems fail.

**Identifying R-22 systems:** Equipment manufactured before 2006 likely uses R-22. Check the data plate on the outdoor unit — the refrigerant type is listed.

### R-410A (HFC-410A, "Puron")

Replaced R-22 for residential equipment. Higher pressure refrigerant (roughly twice the operating pressure of R-22 — incompatible equipment, oils, and fittings). Zero ozone-depleting potential, but significant global warming potential (GWP of ~2088, compared to CO2 at 1).

**Phase-down:** R-410A is being phased down under the American Innovation and Manufacturing (AIM) Act and its implementing regulations. Production and import caps are reducing. It's still widely used and widely available, but new equipment is transitioning to lower-GWP alternatives.

### R-32 and R-454B (A2L Refrigerants)

The next generation of residential refrigerants. Lower GWP than R-410A. Mildly flammable (A2L classification) — requires specific handling protocols, detection in equipment rooms, and updated training.

R-32: GWP of 675 (vs 2088 for R-410A). Used widely in Asia and Europe; increasing in the US.

R-454B (Opteon XL41): GWP of 466. Major manufacturers' chosen R-410A replacement for residential equipment in the US.

**A2L flammability:** A2L refrigerants are "mildly flammable" — significantly less flammable than propane or natural gas, but different from the non-flammable R-410A. New service protocols address this.

### R-744 (CO2)

Carbon dioxide used as a refrigerant. Very low GWP (1). Used in commercial refrigeration and increasingly in heat pump water heaters. High operating pressures require specialized equipment.

## EPA Section 608 Certification

**The rule:** Federal law requires anyone who purchases refrigerant in quantities greater than 2 pounds to be EPA Section 608 certified. Purchasing refrigerants without certification is illegal.

**Certification types:**
- **Type I:** Small appliances (sealed systems with less than 5 lbs refrigerant — window AC, refrigerators)
- **Type II:** High-pressure refrigerants (R-22 and HFCs including R-410A)
- **Type III:** Low-pressure refrigerants (centrifugal chillers)
- **Universal:** All types

**Who needs it:** Anyone working professionally in HVAC. It's a prerequisite for buying refrigerant from supply houses.

**Getting certified:** Study guides and practice exams are widely available. Tests are administered at approved testing centers (Prometric, PSI, others). The exam covers refrigerant properties, regulatory requirements, leak detection, recovery, and safety. Typical cost: $20-50 for the exam.

---

## Recovery, Recycle, Reclaim

**The venting prohibition:** It is illegal to intentionally release refrigerant into the atmosphere, for any refrigerant that the EPA requires recovery. Fines can be substantial.

**Recovery:** Removing refrigerant from a system into a certified recovery cylinder. Required before opening a refrigerant circuit for service. Requires a recovery machine and approved cylinders.

**Recycling:** Cleaning recovered refrigerant on-site for reuse in the same or similar equipment. Limited decontamination.

**Reclamation:** Processing refrigerant off-site to ARI-700 purity standards (equivalent to new refrigerant). Only certified reclaimers can do this.

Recovery equipment is required for most refrigerant service work. Recovery machines and cylinders are standard parts of an HVAC technician's toolkit.

## Safety Hazards

**Frostbite:** Liquid refrigerant boils at very low temperatures (R-410A boils at -61°F at atmospheric pressure). Contact with liquid refrigerant causes frostbite. Always wear safety glasses and gloves when working with refrigerant.

**Pressure hazards:** R-410A operates at 400+ PSI on the high side. Refrigerant cylinders are under pressure. Never heat cylinders, never overfill them (fill by weight, not pressure). Use proper gauge manifolds.

**A2L flammability:** New mildly flammable refrigerants (R-32, R-454B) require leak detection equipment and awareness of ignition hazards. Don't use ignition sources in confined spaces with known refrigerant leaks.

**Asphyxiation:** In high concentrations, refrigerant displaces oxygen. Don't purge refrigerant in enclosed spaces.

**Phosgene from combustion:** When refrigerants contact open flames, they can form phosgene (a toxic gas). Don't use torches near refrigerant leaks.

## Identifying Refrigerant in the Field

Always check the equipment data plate before connecting gauges. Mixing refrigerants (charging a system with the wrong refrigerant) creates a contaminated system that must be fully recovered and recharged — expensive.

Data plates list the refrigerant type, original charge weight, and operating pressures. Charging to weight (using a digital scale) is more accurate than charging to pressure for most modern systems.
