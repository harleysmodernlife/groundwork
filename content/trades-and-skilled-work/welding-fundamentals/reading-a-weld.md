## Reading a Weld: What Good and Bad Welds Look Like

Every weld tells a story. The shape of the bead, the color of the metal, the pattern of the ripples, and the condition of the surrounding zone all reveal whether the weld is sound, what was done correctly, and what went wrong. A welder who can read their own welds can self-correct without a supervisor or inspector. A weld inspector reads the same indicators to accept or reject welds.

---

## What a Good Weld Looks Like

### Bead Profile

**Width:** Consistent throughout the length of the weld. Wider in some sections and narrower in others indicates inconsistent travel speed or technique.

**Height (crown):** A slight convex profile — roughly 1/8" above the plate surface for a flat butt weld. Too flat (or concave) suggests inadequate fill or excessive heat. Too convex (high, rounded) suggests insufficient heat or too-fast travel speed.

**Fillet weld profile:** For a T-joint fillet weld, the legs should be roughly equal (equal distance up the vertical member and across the horizontal member). The face of the weld should be flat or slightly concave — a concave face increases the throat of the weld (the distance across the weld face) and therefore the strength. A convex fillet weld has a reduced effective throat and may appear "fatty."

### Ripple Pattern

In MIG, TIG, and stick welds, the solidified weld bead shows a ripple pattern from the overlap of successive weld puddles. The pattern should be:
- **Even spacing:** Consistent ripple spacing indicates consistent travel speed and technique
- **Symmetrical:** Not leaning heavily to one side
- **In TIG:** A regular "stack of dimes" pattern — overlapping semicircles of consistent size — is the hallmark of controlled TIG technique

### Color

**On mild steel:** After the weld cools, the weld and heat-affected zone (HAZ) will be gray-black from oxidation. The bead itself may have a slightly different sheen from the surrounding plate. Heat tint (light gold to blue oxidation colors) around the weld is normal and indicates the metal was heated to a moderate temperature.

**On stainless steel:** Color is critical.
- Gold/straw: Acceptable (moderate heat input)
- Blue: Acceptable for some applications; the oxide layer is thicker
- Gray/black: Excessive heat — the oxide layer is very thick, indicating overheating. On stainless, heavy oxidation correlates with loss of corrosion resistance in the HAZ. Use lower heat, faster travel, and gas shielding on both sides of the joint.
- Silver/shiny: Ideal for the bead itself, indicating well-shielded, clean conditions

**On aluminum:** Good aluminum TIG welds appear bright silver-gray. A dull, matte gray weld indicates inadequate shielding or contamination. Black deposits in or around the weld indicate contamination.

## Common Visual Defects and Their Causes

### Porosity

**Appearance:** Small holes on the surface of the weld or visible within the weld cross-section. May appear as scattered pits, a group of holes (cluster porosity), or holes running along the weld (linear porosity).

**Causes:**
- Contaminated base metal (oil, rust, moisture, paint, galvanizing)
- Insufficient shielding gas coverage (low flow, damaged nozzle, drafts blowing gas away)
- Moisture in the flux (stick electrodes stored improperly — especially 7018)
- Excessive arc length in MIG or stick
- Wrong gas mixture for the process

**Significance:** Porosity reduces the effective cross-sectional area of the weld (the holes are voids with no strength). Moderate porosity fails welding codes. On structural welds, any visible surface porosity is usually cause for rejection.

**Fix:** Grind out and reweld the porous section. Identify and correct the root cause first.

### Undercut

**Appearance:** A groove or channel burned into the base metal along one or both edges of the weld bead. The weld bead has eaten into the adjacent metal without filling it back in.

**Causes:**
- Too high voltage or amperage
- Arc directed too much to one side
- Excessive weaving width
- Wrong travel angle — the arc is melting the base metal edge instead of the joint

**Significance:** Undercut creates a stress riser — a notch in the base metal at the weld toe. Under cyclic loading, cracks propagate from undercut. Most codes allow very minor undercut (maximum 1/32" depth); anything deeper requires repair.

**Fix:** For minor undercut, add a light pass of filler along the toe. For significant undercut, grind to clean metal and reweld.

### Overlap

**Appearance:** The weld bead has rolled over the edge of the joint or the base metal without fusing to it. Looks like the bead overflowed onto the surface without bonding.

**Causes:**
- Too slow travel speed (bead builds up and overflows)
- Excessive wire feed speed or amperage
- Wrong gun angle causing puddle to flow sideways
- Low voltage relative to wire feed speed (the arc isn't hot enough to maintain fusion)

**Significance:** Unfused overlap creates a notch similar to undercut and provides no strength — it's just metal sitting on the surface.

**Fix:** Grind off the overlap. Adjust settings and reweld.

### Incomplete Fusion (Cold Lap)

**Appearance:** The weld bead is present but not fused to the base metal or to the previous weld pass. May be visible at the edges of the bead, or only detectable by NDT (non-destructive testing). On cross-section, the bead appears to sit on top of the base metal rather than melting into it.

**Causes:**
- Travel speed too fast — the puddle didn't have time to fuse
- Heat too low — the base metal wasn't melted
- Contamination preventing fusion
- Welding over heavy scale or rust that insulates the base metal surface

**Significance:** Unfused welds provide little strength. Under load, the joint may appear intact until stressed. One of the most dangerous weld defects because it's often not visible from the outside.

**Fix:** Grind to clean metal and reweld with proper heat and speed.

### Incomplete Penetration

**Appearance:** On a butt joint, the root of the weld (the bottom of the joint) didn't fuse fully. Visible on the back side as a groove or incomplete fill.

**Causes:**
- Insufficient heat for the material thickness
- Too-tight joint fit-up (no root gap for the weld to penetrate)
- Travel speed too fast
- Wrong electrode/wire diameter for the thickness

**Significance:** The unfused root creates a stress concentration and a void in the weld cross-section. On structural and pressure welds, full penetration is required and specified.

**Fix:** Back-gouge (grind from the back side) to clean metal and weld the root pass from the opposite side. Alternatively, grind out the entire weld and start over with proper parameters.

### Cracks

**Types:**
- **Hot cracks:** Form in the weld pool while it's still solidifying. Caused by high restraint, wrong filler for the base metal, or contamination (sulfur in the base metal cracks the weld).
- **Cold cracks (hydrogen-induced cracking):** Form hours or days after welding. Caused by hydrogen from moisture in the flux, high-strength base metal, and residual stress. Primarily a concern with high-strength steel.
- **Crater cracks:** Small cracks in the end-of-pass crater. Caused by not filling the crater properly before ending the weld.

**Significance:** All cracks are rejectable on structural and pressure welds. Cracks grow under cyclic loading.

**Fix:** Remove the cracked area by grinding and reweld. For repeated hot cracking, address the root cause (preheat, change filler).

## Reading the Heat-Affected Zone

The heat-affected zone (HAZ) is the base metal adjacent to the weld that was heated (but not melted) enough to change its microstructure. The HAZ is not visible by color alone on mild steel, but you can assess the weld's heat input by the width of the discolored zone around the bead.

**On stainless steel:** The color of the HAZ indicates peak temperature:
- Gold/straw: ~600-900°F
- Purple: ~900-1100°F
- Blue: ~1000-1200°F
- Gray: above ~1200°F

**Excessive HAZ width** indicates high heat input — slow travel or too much amperage. High heat input increases distortion, can cause carbide precipitation in stainless, and alters mechanical properties.

## Destructive Testing Methods

Visual inspection is useful but limited. For critical welds, destructive testing verifies internal quality:

**Bend test:** Cut a strip from the weld and bend it over a mandrel. A sound weld will bend 180° without cracking. Porosity, lack of fusion, and cracks cause the specimen to fail by fracturing.

**Macro section:** Cut the weld perpendicular to its length, grind and polish the face, etch with acid (nital for steel — 2-10% nitric acid in methanol). The etch reveals weld bead, HAZ, fusion lines, and any defects. This is used in welder qualification testing and process validation.

**Tensile test:** Pull the weld apart in a tensile testing machine. The break should occur in the base metal, not the weld — a sound weld deposits metal stronger than the base metal it joins.

## Non-Destructive Testing (NDT)

For production welds that can't be cut apart:

**Visual inspection (VT):** The first and most economical method. All the defects listed above can be detected visually if you know what to look for.

**Liquid penetrant testing (PT/LPI):** A colored or fluorescent dye is applied to the weld surface, allowed to soak in, the excess removed, and a developer applied. The dye bleeds back from surface cracks and porosity, revealing their location. Finds only surface defects.

**Magnetic particle testing (MT):** A magnetic field is induced in the weld. Iron particles (dry or in suspension) are applied. At defects, the magnetic field leaks and draws the particles into patterns that reveal the defect. Only works on ferromagnetic metals (steel, not stainless or aluminum). Finds surface and near-surface defects.

**Ultrasonic testing (UT):** High-frequency sound waves are sent through the weld. Internal defects reflect sound waves back to the transducer. Can detect internal porosity, cracks, and incomplete fusion throughout the full weld thickness. The primary NDT method for thick structural welds.

**Radiographic testing (RT/X-ray):** X-ray or gamma radiation passes through the weld onto film or a digital detector. Voids appear as dark spots. Provides a permanent record. Used on pipe welds, pressure vessels, and other critical applications.
