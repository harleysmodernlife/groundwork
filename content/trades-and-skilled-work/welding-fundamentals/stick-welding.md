## Stick Welding (SMAW)

Stick welding — officially Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW) — is the oldest and most portable arc welding process. An electrode ("stick") consisting of a metal core coated with flux is clamped in an electrode holder. The arc forms between the electrode tip and the base metal. The flux coating generates shielding gas and slag that protect the molten weld pool.

No shielding gas cylinder needed. No wire feeder. Just the machine, some electrodes, and a power supply. That simplicity makes stick welding invaluable for field work, repair, and situations where MIG would be impractical.

---

## How Stick Welding Works

The electrode serves two purposes:
1. **Filler metal:** The steel core melts and deposits into the joint
2. **Shielding:** The flux coating burns and produces shielding gases that protect the weld pool from atmospheric contamination; it also melts and flows over the weld as slag, providing additional protection during cooling

After the weld cools, the slag must be chipped off and brushed clean. This is normal — the weld underneath is finished.

**The arc:** Maintain a short, consistent arc length — approximately equal to the electrode diameter. Too long: voltage spikes, arc goes out, excessive spatter and porosity. Too short: the electrode stubs and freezes to the base metal.

**Striking an arc:** Two methods:
- **Touch and lift:** Touch the electrode to the metal, then immediately lift slightly to establish arc length. Like striking a match.
- **Scratch start:** Scratch the electrode across the metal surface like a match, then lift to arc length.

If the electrode freezes (sticks to the metal), release the electrode holder immediately — don't force it free or the electrode will continue heating. Let go of the holder, let the electrode cool slightly, then free it.

## Electrodes

Stick electrodes are classified by the AWS (American Welding Society) system. The most common you'll encounter:

### E6010
**"Six-ten"** — DC+ only. Deep penetrating, fast-freeze. The electrode for structural welding, pipe welding, and the first pass (root pass) on full-penetration joints. Harder to run than 6013 but produces superior penetration and X-ray quality welds. Requires DC power.

**Use for:** Structural steel, pipeline root passes, welding through light surface contamination.

### E6011
The AC-compatible version of 6010. Same fast-freeze behavior and penetration but runs on AC or DC machines. Useful when only an AC "buzz box" is available.

**Use for:** Same as 6010, when AC power is the only option.

### E6013
**"Six-thirteen"** — AC or DC. Soft arc, easy to run, forgiving. The go-to electrode for beginners and for light sheet metal work. Less penetration than 6010/6011. Easy arc starts, minimal spatter. Produces smooth-looking beads even when technique is imperfect.

**Use for:** Sheet metal, light fabrication, general repairs, learning. Not for structural work requiring full penetration.

### E7018
**"Seventy-eighteen"** — DC+ (or AC on some machines). Low-hydrogen electrode producing high-strength, ductile deposits. The standard electrode for structural welding that must meet code (AWS D1.1 structural welding code calls for low-hydrogen electrodes on most applications). Requires dry storage — absorbed moisture causes porosity and hydrogen-induced cracking. Store in a rod oven at 250°F or in a sealed container.

**Use for:** Structural steel, heavy fabrication, high-strength steel, code-required work.

### Electrode Storage

7018 and other low-hydrogen electrodes absorb moisture from the air. Moisture in the coating causes porosity and hydrogen cracking in the completed weld. Store in a sealed container or a rod oven (a heated cabinet maintaining 250°F) when not in active use. Electrodes that have been exposed can be reconditioned by baking at 700-800°F per manufacturer recommendations.

6013 and 6010/6011 are less moisture-sensitive. Store in a dry location. Don't use wet or visibly damaged electrodes.

## Machine Settings

Stick machines are simpler than MIG — primarily just amperage control.

**Amperage by electrode diameter:**

| Electrode Diameter | Approximate Amperage Range |
|---|---|
| 3/32" (2.4mm) | 40-85 A |
| 1/8" (3.2mm) | 75-130 A |
| 5/32" (4.0mm) | 110-165 A |
| 3/16" (4.8mm) | 140-210 A |

**Material thickness guides electrode choice:** Thin material needs smaller electrodes. A 1/8" electrode on 14-gauge sheet will blow through it. A 3/32" or smaller electrode for sheet metal; 1/8" and larger for material 3/16" and up.

**Dialing in heat:** Start at the midpoint of the amperage range for your electrode. If the arc goes out easily and the electrode stubs frequently, increase amperage. If you're blowing through or the bead is excessively flat and spatter is heavy, decrease.

**DC+ vs. DC-:**
- DC+ (electrode positive, work negative): More heat into the electrode, deeper penetration. Used with 7018, 6010, 6011, and most electrodes.
- DC- (electrode negative, work positive): Less penetration, higher deposition rate. Used with some specific electrodes. Check the electrode box.

## Technique

**Electrode angles:** Similar to MIG.
- **Work angle:** 90° to the base metal for flat butt welds; 45° for fillet welds
- **Travel angle:** Drag 5-15° back from vertical (pointing backward in the direction you just welded)

**Travel speed:** Watch the width of the bead behind the arc. You want a consistent oval puddle, roughly 2.5 times the electrode core diameter wide. Moving too fast = narrow bead, incomplete fusion. Too slow = wide, convex bead, burn-through on thin metal.

**Arc length:** This is the hardest part of stick welding. As the electrode burns, it gets shorter — you must lower your hand to maintain consistent arc length. Inconsistent arc length causes inconsistent bead appearance and quality.

**Weave patterns:** For a wider bead or for vertical welding:
- **Whip and pause:** Move forward, pause, move forward again. Allows the puddle to fill and solidify slightly before the next addition.
- **Z-weave:** Move the electrode in a Z pattern across the joint width. Controls heat and builds a wider bead.
- **C-weave:** Move in crescent/C shapes across the joint.

**Restarting a pass:** When you need to replace an electrode or restart:
1. Chip and brush the previous slag back about 1/2" from where you stopped
2. Strike the arc 1/2" ahead of the crater
3. Move back to the crater and fill it before moving forward
4. The bead should blend seamlessly

## Slag Removal

After each pass cools slightly, chip the slag off with a chipping hammer. Hold the slag guard up (the flip-up cover on many helmets) or wear safety glasses — hot slag fragments fly unpredictably.

After chipping, wire brush the weld surface thoroughly. Any remaining slag under a subsequent pass will be trapped — a slag inclusion defect that weakens the weld.

For multipass welds (required on thick material), remove all slag between every pass.

## Stick Welding Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages:**
- No shielding gas required — works outdoors in wind that would blow away MIG shielding gas
- Welds through moderate rust, scale, and contamination better than MIG or TIG
- Equipment is simple and inexpensive
- Works with a generator (portable power)
- Electrode choice allows welding many different metals (different electrodes for cast iron, stainless, hardfacing)

**Limitations:**
- More skill-intensive than MIG — arc length, amperage, and electrode angle must all be manually managed
- Slower deposition rate than MIG
- Significant slag to clean between passes
- Not suitable for very thin material (below 3/16" is difficult)
- Position welding (vertical, overhead) requires more skill than MIG

## Common Defects

**Porosity:** Moisture in the electrode (especially 7018), contaminated metal, or arc too long. Recondition or replace electrodes, clean metal, shorten arc.

**Undercut:** Arc too long, too much amperage, or wrong angle. Reduce amperage, shorten arc, adjust electrode angle to direct heat into the joint.

**Overlap:** The weld metal flows over the base metal edge without fusing into it — looks like the bead rolled over the edge. Too slow travel speed, too much amperage.

**Slag inclusions:** Trapped slag within the weld deposit. Usually from incomplete slag removal between passes or from slag running ahead of the arc. Increase travel speed, ensure proper slag removal.

**Incomplete fusion (cold lap):** Weld metal not fused to base metal. Too fast travel speed, too low amperage, or surface contamination.

**Arc blow:** The arc wanders erratically, especially near the end of a weld. Caused by magnetic fields in the base metal deflecting the arc. Switch to AC if using DC, change your ground connection location, or change your welding direction.
