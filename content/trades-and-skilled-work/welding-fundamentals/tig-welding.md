## TIG Welding: Precision Work

TIG welding (Tungsten Inert Gas, officially GTAW — Gas Tungsten Arc Welding) is the most precise welding process. The arc forms between a non-consumable tungsten electrode and the base metal. Filler metal is added separately by hand, dipped into the leading edge of the puddle. The torch, filler rod, and foot pedal (controlling amperage) are all managed simultaneously.

TIG produces the highest quality, best-looking welds of any common process. It's used for stainless steel fabrication, aluminum welding, titanium (aerospace), root passes on pipe, and anywhere the weld must be both strong and aesthetically excellent.

---

## How TIG Welding Works

The tungsten electrode does not melt into the weld (it's non-consumable). It only conducts electricity to create the arc. The intense heat of the arc melts the base metal, forming a puddle. The welder then dips a filler rod into the puddle manually with the other hand to add fill material.

Shielding gas (100% argon for most applications) flows from a ceramic or glass cup around the electrode, protecting both the electrode and the weld puddle from oxidation.

**The foot pedal (ampcontrol):** The TIG torch is used with one hand. Filler rod is held in the other. The foot pedal controls amperage — pressing increases heat, releasing decreases it. This gives the welder precise control to start hot (for penetration), weld at moderate heat, and back down at the end of the joint to fill the crater without burn-through. Hands-free ampcontrol is essential for position welding and for avoiding burn-through when reaching the thin edge of a part.

Some TIG setups use a thumb control on the torch instead of a foot pedal — useful when working in positions where a floor pedal isn't practical.

## Equipment

### TIG Welder

TIG machines must produce both AC and DC output (or DC only, for steel and stainless only). AC is required for aluminum welding — it provides a "cleaning action" that breaks up the aluminum oxide layer.

**Entry-level:** Lincoln Square Wave 200, Everlast 200DV — both AC/DC, suitable for learning and light professional work.

**Professional:** Miller Dynasty series, Lincoln Invertec — precise AC frequency and balance control, sophisticated arc characteristics, inverter-based for efficiency and portability.

Machines are also specified by amperage. 200A covers most applications. Thicker material or larger jobs may need more.

### Tungsten Electrodes

Available in different types for different applications. The tip shape also matters:

**Ceriated (gray stripe):** DC welding on steel, stainless, and other metals. For DC, grind the tip to a sharp point — the point concentrates the arc.

**Pure tungsten (green stripe):** Traditional choice for AC aluminum welding. The tip forms a ball under AC operation — the balled tip is correct.

**Lanthanated (gold stripe):** Versatile — works on both AC and DC. Good choice if you want one tungsten type.

**Diameter:** Matched to amperage:
- 1/16" (1.6mm): Up to 70A (DC), up to 80A (AC)
- 3/32" (2.4mm): 70-150A (DC), 60-180A (AC)
- 1/8" (3.2mm): 150-250A (DC), 150-280A (AC)

### Filler Rods

TIG filler is sold in straight rods (typically 36" long) rather than spools.

**ER70S-2 / ER70S-6:** Carbon steel filler. ER70S-2 is cleaner (more deoxidizers), preferred for TIG.
**ER308L, ER316L:** Stainless steel fillers. Match the filler to the base metal alloy.
**ER4043:** Aluminum filler (softer, flows easily). For most general aluminum work.
**ER5356:** Stronger aluminum filler. For structural aluminum or where higher strength is needed.
**ERNiCrMo-3 (Inconel 625):** For nickel alloys and dissimilar metal welds.

Filler diameter: typically match to the base metal thickness. For thin material, smaller filler produces less heat. Common sizes: 1/16", 3/32", 1/8".

### Shielding Gas

**100% Argon:** Standard for all TIG welding — steel, stainless, and aluminum. Argon is heavier than air and settles over the weld puddle effectively at low flow rates.

**Argon/Helium mixes:** Helium increases heat input and speeds travel. Used for thicker aluminum and copper. More expensive.

Flow rate: 10-20 CFH. TIG uses less gas than MIG because the torch cup controls the flow precisely.

## Technique

TIG welding requires more coordination than any other welding process. The learning curve is steeper, but the skill is proportionally rewarding.

### Torch Hold and Position

Hold the torch like a pencil or pen — the torch body between thumb and index finger, with the other fingers providing stability. Some welders rest the ring or pinky finger on the workpiece for a steadying point.

**Electrode stick-out:** The tungsten should extend from the cup 1.5 to 2.5 times the electrode diameter. More stick-out reduces gas coverage and increases contamination risk. Less stick-out restricts visibility.

**Torch angle:** Similar to MIG — work angle perpendicular (or bisecting, for fillet welds), travel angle with a slight drag (5-15°).

### Starting the Arc

Modern TIG machines use HF (high-frequency) start — the arc initiates without touching the tungsten to the metal. Touch-start (lift-arc or scratch-start) is available on machines without HF, but it risks contaminating the tungsten.

With HF start: position the tungsten about 1/8" from the metal, depress the foot pedal, and the arc fires automatically.

### Welding Motion

Once the arc is established and the pedal is depressed:
1. Hold the arc at the start of the joint until a puddle forms — you'll see the metal go liquid and develop a characteristic bright, shiny appearance
2. Dip the filler rod into the leading edge of the puddle — a smooth, consistent dip-and-pull rhythm
3. Move the torch forward by a small increment
4. Dip again

The rhythm: dip, move, dip, move. The dip must go into the puddle, not into the arc — touching the filler to the arc contaminates the tungsten. The frequency of dipping determines bead appearance (more frequent dips = more pronounced ripple pattern).

**Contaminated tungsten:** If the tungsten touches the filler or base metal, it picks up contamination (turns black, develops a ball at the tip) and the arc becomes erratic. Stop, regrind or snap off the contaminated portion, re-tip the electrode.

### Foot Pedal Use

The foot pedal's power is in dynamic control:
- **Start hot:** Full pedal at the start to establish penetration quickly, then ease back to a working level
- **Approach the end:** Ease the pedal down over the last 1/2" to reduce heat and fill the crater without burn-through
- **Thin sections:** Back off when reaching thin edges; add heat when reaching thick sections

## Welding Aluminum with AC

Aluminum welding with TIG requires AC (alternating current). AC alternates between electrode positive (cleaning action, breaks up aluminum oxide) and electrode negative (penetration). The balance control on the machine adjusts the ratio.

**Oxide layer:** Aluminum immediately forms a tough, dull gray oxide layer (Al₂O₃) on its surface when exposed to air. This oxide melts at 3,700°F while the aluminum beneath it melts at 1,200°F. The electrode positive half-cycle of AC breaks up and disperses this oxide — you'll see a bright, shiny "cleaning zone" appear around the arc as it clears the oxide.

**Aluminum welding setup differences:**
- AC output (not DC)
- Higher amperage than an equivalent steel thickness (aluminum conducts heat away faster)
- Pure or lanthanated tungsten
- ER4043 or ER5356 filler
- Clean aluminum — wire brush with a dedicated stainless brush (contaminated with steel deposits steel into the weld)
- Preheat on thick sections (250-400°F) to prevent cracking

**The appearance of good AC aluminum welding:** A bright, shiny weld with a consistent ripple pattern, surrounded by a silver-gray cleaning zone on the base metal.

## Applications

**Stainless steel:** TIG on stainless produces clean, full-penetration welds with minimal distortion. The heat must be controlled carefully — excessive heat causes carbide precipitation (sensitization), reducing corrosion resistance. Use lower heat input, faster travel, and back-purge the backside of the weld with argon when welding pipe or tubing (prevents oxidation on the inside).

**Thin material:** TIG excels on thin material (16 gauge and thinner) where MIG risks burn-through. The foot pedal allows instant heat reduction.

**Root passes on pipe:** The first pass inside a pipe joint is often done with TIG for control and quality, with subsequent fill and cap passes done with MIG or stick.

**Exotic metals:** Titanium, chromoly, Inconel, and other high-value metals are usually welded with TIG. These require clean conditions — contamination causes embrittlement or porosity.

---

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages:**
- Highest weld quality of any arc process
- Best appearance — smooth, consistent, stackable dimes
- Precise control via foot pedal
- Suitable for all metals (with proper filler and setup)
- Minimal spatter

**Limitations:**
- Slowest deposition rate of common processes
- Steepest learning curve — requires hand/foot coordination
- Requires very clean metal — doesn't tolerate contamination
- More expensive equipment than MIG
- Slower than MIG and stick for thick material
