## MIG Welding: The Most Common Starting Point

MIG welding (Metal Inert Gas, officially GMAW — Gas Metal Arc Welding) is where most welders start. The process is relatively forgiving, produces decent welds quickly, and is the dominant process for structural and fabrication work in most shops. If you only learn one welding process, this is the one.

---

## How MIG Welding Works

A wire electrode (typically 0.023" to 0.035" diameter steel wire) feeds continuously from a spool through the welding gun. When you pull the trigger, three things happen simultaneously:
1. The wire feeds at a set speed
2. Electricity flows through the wire, creating an arc between the wire tip and the base metal
3. Shielding gas flows from the nozzle, blanketing the weld pool and arc from atmospheric oxygen and nitrogen

The arc melts both the wire and the base metal. The wire acts as filler, depositing metal into the joint as it's consumed. You move the gun along the joint, and the molten pool follows, solidifying into the weld bead.

**Why shielding gas:** Air contains oxygen and nitrogen. At welding temperatures, both contaminate the weld — oxygen causes porosity, nitrogen causes brittleness. The shielding gas (typically C25 — 75% argon, 25% CO2) pushes the air away from the weld zone.

## Equipment

### Welding Machine

MIG machines are rated by their output amperage and duty cycle:
- **Duty cycle:** The percentage of a 10-minute period the machine can weld at its rated amperage. A 30% duty cycle at 130A means you can weld 3 minutes out of 10 at 130A without overheating. Lower-cost machines have lower duty cycles.
- **Entry-level (Hobart Handler 140, Lincoln 140MP):** 140A output, suitable for up to 3/16" steel, 20-30% duty cycle. 120V plug. Good starting machines.
- **Professional (Lincoln 180, Miller 211, Hobart 210MVP):** Up to 210A, 1/4" steel, higher duty cycle. Most run on 240V for full output.

### Wire

**ER70S-6:** The standard MIG wire for mild steel. The "70" means the weld deposit has 70,000 PSI tensile strength. The "S" means solid wire. The "6" indicates the deoxidizer content (higher deoxidizer handles rustier, dirtier steel better).

**Diameter:**
- 0.023": Thin sheet metal (24-20 gauge)
- 0.030": General purpose, 18 gauge to 3/16"
- 0.035": Heavier material, 1/8" to 1/4"+

### Shielding Gas

**C25 (75% Ar / 25% CO2):** The standard for MIG welding mild steel. Produces a smooth, stable arc with good penetration and appearance.

**100% CO2:** Cheaper, deeper penetration, but more spatter. Used in production environments where cost matters more than spatter cleanup.

**Gas flow rate:** 15-25 CFH (cubic feet per hour). Too low leaves the weld unprotected; too high creates turbulence that can pull in outside air. Measure at the gun by opening the gas valve and checking flow.

**Gas cylinders:** Rent from a local welding supply (usually includes the regulator) or own your own. A 40 cf cylinder is a good starter. A 75-80 cf cylinder lasts longer and is more economical per cubic foot.

## Machine Settings

MIG machines have two primary controls:

**Voltage (heat):** Controls the heat input. Higher voltage = hotter arc = more penetration and wider bead. Too high burns through thin metal or produces a flat, wide bead with lack of fusion on the edges.

**Wire feed speed (WFS):** Controls how fast wire feeds, which also affects amperage. Higher WFS = more metal deposited = more amperage = hotter weld. Wire feed speed and voltage must be balanced — if the arc is too harsh and "blasting" through, reduce voltage. If the wire is stubbing into the base metal (machine gun sound), increase voltage.

**Starting settings:** Most machines have a chart inside the door or on the machine face showing recommended settings for different material thicknesses. Use those as a starting point, then adjust based on how the arc sounds and looks.

**The sound of a good MIG arc:** A steady, even crackle like frying bacon. Consistent. No stuttering, no hard pops.

## Preparing to Weld

**Clean the metal.** Mill scale (the blue-black oxide on hot-rolled steel), rust, paint, and oil all contaminate the weld. Clean with a grinder, flap disc, or wire brush down to shiny metal at least 1" on either side of the joint. MIG tolerates less-than-perfect metal better than TIG, but cleanliness always produces better welds.

**Joint fit-up.** The pieces should fit together with consistent gaps appropriate for the joint type:
- Butt joint: 0" to 1/16" gap for thin material; up to 1/8" for thicker material (allows full penetration)
- T-joint (fillet weld): Tight fit, minimal gap

**Ground clamp.** Attach the work lead (ground) directly to the workpiece or welding table, as close to the weld zone as practical. A poor ground connection causes arc instability and poor welds. Clip it to clean metal — paint and rust increase resistance.

## Technique: Gun Angle, Travel Direction, Travel Speed

### Gun Angle

**Work angle:** The angle of the gun relative to the joint. For a flat butt weld, hold the gun perpendicular to the joint (90° to the plate). For a fillet weld (T-joint), bisect the angle — point the gun at roughly 45° into the joint corner.

**Travel angle (torch angle or drag/push angle):** The angle of the gun along the direction of travel.
- **Drag (backhand):** Gun tilted 10-15° back from vertical, pointing into the puddle in the direction you came from. Produces deeper penetration.
- **Push (forehand):** Gun tilted 10-15° forward in the direction of travel. Shallower penetration, wider bead, better visibility of the puddle.

For structural work, slight drag or neutral is most common.

### Travel Speed

Travel speed determines bead width and penetration. Moving too slow: wide, convex bead, excess heat buildup, burn-through on thin material. Moving too fast: narrow, rope-like bead, incomplete fusion.

You want the arc to be at the leading edge of the puddle. Watch the puddle, not the arc — the arc will follow. When the puddle looks like a consistent oval teardrop behind the arc, you're at the right speed.

## Common Weld Defects

**Porosity (holes in the weld):** Almost always caused by contamination — oil, rust, paint, or shielding gas problems. Check that gas is flowing, nozzle isn't clogged with spatter, and metal is clean.

**Undercut:** A groove burned along the edge of the weld where the base metal is eaten away without being filled. Caused by too much voltage or wrong work angle. Reduce voltage or adjust the gun angle to direct more heat into the joint.

**Lack of fusion:** The weld bead sits on top of the base metal rather than fusing with it. Caused by too-low heat, too-high travel speed, or welding over rust/scale. Increase heat, slow down, clean the metal.

**Burn-through:** The arc blows a hole through the base metal. Common on thin material. Reduce voltage, increase travel speed, or use a thinner wire. Tack-weld the joint in several spots before running a continuous bead.

**Spatter:** Small balls of molten metal ejected from the arc and stuck to the surrounding metal. Some spatter is normal. Excessive spatter indicates voltage too low, wrong wire/gas combination, or contaminated wire. Apply anti-spatter spray to the nozzle and surrounding metal to make cleanup easier.

## Weld Positions

**1G/1F (flat):** Welding on the top of a horizontally positioned joint. The easiest position. Learn here.

**2F/2G (horizontal):** Welding on the side of a vertical surface. Gravity pulls the puddle down — adjust by angling the gun slightly upward and moving at a pace that keeps the puddle from sagging.

**3F/3G (vertical):** Welding vertically. Uphill welding (moving up) gives better penetration and is preferred for structural work. Downhill (moving down) is faster but shallower.

**4F/4G (overhead):** Welding on the underside of a surface. Gravity pulls the puddle down toward you. Reduce heat (the puddle sags more at high heat) and move faster. Full PPE — sparks fall on you.

## Safety

**Welding arc produces UV radiation.** Arc flash burns the eyes (like a severe sunburn on the eyeball) and skin. Wear a welding helmet with appropriate shade (typically shade 10 for MIG at normal amperage). Auto-darkening helmets trigger in milliseconds when the arc strikes. Side shields protect against reflected arc from nearby welders.

**Fumes.** Welding produces metal fumes that are harmful when inhaled — zinc fumes from galvanized metal cause metal fume fever. Manganese (present in most steel) causes neurological damage with chronic exposure. Weld in ventilated spaces, with exhaust extraction when working in enclosed areas. A respirator rated for metal fumes (P100) when ventilation is insufficient.

**Fire.** Sparks and spatter travel 10-30 feet from the weld. Clear combustible materials, cover with fire-resistant blankets. Keep a fire extinguisher accessible.

**Electrical.** The welding machine produces low-voltage, high-current electricity. Don't weld in wet conditions or with damaged leads. Don't rest the electrode against yourself.
