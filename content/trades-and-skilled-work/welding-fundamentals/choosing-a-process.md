## Choosing the Right Process for the Job

MIG, Stick, and TIG each have specific strengths and appropriate applications. Choosing the wrong process for a job produces welds that are harder to make, weaker, or take far longer than necessary. This lesson maps process capabilities to real-world situations.

---

## Quick Reference

| Factor | MIG | Stick | TIG |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skill to learn | Low-moderate | Moderate | High |
| Portability | Moderate (needs gas) | Excellent | Low-moderate |
| Thin material | Good | Poor | Excellent |
| Thick material | Good | Good | Moderate |
| Dirty/rusty metal | Moderate | Good | Poor |
| Outdoor use | Poor (wind disrupts gas) | Excellent | Poor |
| Aluminum | Yes (with spool gun) | No | Excellent |
| Stainless | Yes | Yes (limited) | Excellent |
| Speed/deposition | High | Moderate | Low |
| Weld quality ceiling | Good | Good | Excellent |
| Equipment cost | Moderate | Low | High |

---

## Choose MIG When:

**You need speed.** MIG has the highest deposition rate of the three processes. For production fabrication — building multiple units of the same weldment — MIG outpaces TIG significantly.

**Material is mild steel in a controlled environment.** MIG was designed for mild steel in shop conditions. It excels here.

**Operator skill is limited.** MIG is the most forgiving process to learn. The machine handles wire feeding; the operator mainly manages gun angle and travel speed.

**Material is 18 gauge to 1/2"+ in a single pass.** MIG handles a wide thickness range with appropriate wire diameter and settings changes.

**You need to weld thin to thick.** Bridging very different thicknesses is manageable with MIG — direct more heat at the thicker piece.

### MIG Limitations to Know:
- **Wind:** MIG shielding gas disperses in even light wind. Outdoor welding requires wind blocks or a different process.
- **Dirty metal:** MIG tolerates some contamination with ER70S-6 wire, but porosity risk increases on rusty or painted metal.
- **Aluminum out of position:** Welding aluminum with MIG (using a spool gun or push-pull system) works well on flat and horizontal joints but is harder on vertical and overhead — the soft aluminum puddle sags.

---

## Choose Stick When:

**You're working outdoors or in the field.** Stick requires no shielding gas cylinder. The flux coating on the electrode provides all necessary shielding. Wind doesn't affect it.

**The metal is rusty, dirty, or painted.** Stick tolerates surface contamination better than MIG or TIG. The electrode can burn through light rust and mill scale that would cause MIG porosity.

**You need portability.** A stick welder, a handful of electrodes, and an extension cord (or generator) is all you need. Many farm and field repair situations are solved with stick.

**You're welding heavy structural steel.** E7018 electrodes on thick structural steel is a proven combination backed by welding codes (AWS D1.1). The welder certification tests for structural welding are predominantly stick.

**The machine available is simple.** Stick machines are the least expensive and simplest. An old Lincoln "tombstone" buzz box can still produce code-quality welds with the right electrode and technique.

**You're welding cast iron, hardfacing, or exotic metals.** Specialized stick electrodes exist for cast iron (nickel-based electrodes), cutting and gouging (carbon arc), and hardfacing (manganese, chromium carbide deposits). MIG and TIG have fewer options here.

### Stick Limitations:
- **Not for thin material.** Below 1/8" (18 gauge) is very difficult with stick — the heat is hard to control.
- **Slower than MIG.** Deposition rate is lower; electrode changes are constant.
- **Slag cleaning required.** Every pass requires chipping and brushing.
- **Spatter.** More than MIG, though less than flux-core.

---

## Choose TIG When:

**Quality matters more than speed.** TIG consistently produces the highest quality welds with the best mechanical properties and appearance. When a weld will be inspected, x-rayed, or must meet stringent code requirements, TIG is often specified.

**You're welding stainless, aluminum, titanium, or exotic alloys.** TIG handles all of these with appropriate filler selection. MIG handles stainless and aluminum but with less control and quality.

**Material is thin.** For 22-gauge stainless kitchen fabrication, 16-gauge aluminum enclosures, or thin-wall tubing, TIG's foot pedal control allows precision that MIG can't match.

**Appearance is critical.** Automotive custom work, artistic fabrication, architectural metalwork — TIG welds can look like a stack of coins, a signature that's recognizable as skilled craftsmanship.

**You're welding tubing for a pressure or structural application.** Chromoly roll cages, hydraulic tubing, and pipe work where full penetration and excellent mechanical properties are required.

**You need to weld dissimilar metals.** TIG with appropriate filler can join stainless to mild steel, chromoly to stainless, and some other dissimilar combinations. This is difficult to do well with MIG and usually not attempted with stick.

### TIG Limitations:
- **Slow.** Significantly slower deposition than MIG. Not economical for production work that MIG can handle.
- **Requires clean metal.** TIG doesn't tolerate contamination — oil, rust, or oxide layers cause porosity and weld failures. Metal must be properly cleaned before TIG.
- **High skill requirement.** The coordination of torch, filler rod, and foot pedal takes considerable practice to develop.
- **Not for field use.** TIG requires a gas supply, clean conditions, and a stable working position. Not suited for outdoor or field repair.

## Process Combinations

Real-world welding often combines processes:

**Root pass TIG, fill and cap with MIG or Stick:** Common on pipe welding. TIG provides the precise, full-penetration root pass. MIG or stick fills the joint faster.

**MIG for structural, TIG for visible joints:** A steel fabrication might be MIG-welded throughout for structural integrity, with TIG used on joints that will be visible or polished.

**Tack with MIG, finish with TIG:** Quick tack welds to hold fit-up while you check alignment, then TIG for the final passes.

## Flux-Core Arc Welding (FCAW): An Additional Option

Flux-core welding is worth knowing about as a fourth option, closely related to MIG:

A wire with a flux-filled core replaces the solid MIG wire. Two variants:
- **Gas-shielded flux-core (FCAW-G):** Uses an external gas shield plus the flux core. Highest quality, used in structural and heavy fabrication.
- **Self-shielded flux-core (FCAW-S):** No external gas required — the flux core generates all shielding. Suitable for outdoor use in wind that would defeat MIG.

**Self-shielded flux-core is the outdoor/windy-conditions MIG alternative.** Deposition rate higher than stick, no gas cylinder, works in wind. Used heavily in construction, bridge work, and outdoor structural fabrication.

Drawbacks: More spatter than gas-shielded MIG, thick slag to clean (like stick), limited to carbon steel and some stainless. Not for thin material.

## The Economic Side

**Equipment cost:**
- Stick welder: $200-500 for a quality unit
- MIG welder: $400-1,000+ for a capable unit (plus gas cylinder)
- TIG welder: $600-1,500+ for AC/DC (aluminum capability included)
- Multiprocess units (MIG + Stick + TIG): Some machines do all three for $800-1,200

**Operating cost:**
- Stick: Electrode cost only. Moderate. 5-7 lb of electrodes lasts most DIY welders months.
- MIG: Wire + shielding gas. Wire is inexpensive; gas is the ongoing cost (cylinder rental + fills).
- TIG: Argon + filler rods + tungsten. More expensive per hour than MIG, but deposition is lower so less total gas per foot of weld on thin material.

**Learning path for most people:** Start with MIG for its forgiving nature and quick results. If you need field/outdoor capability, add stick. If you need aluminum, stainless, or precision work, add TIG.

Most professional welders can run all three processes — different jobs call for different tools, and versatility is directly correlated with employability and capability.
