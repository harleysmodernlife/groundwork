## Welding Safety: Fumes, Fire, and Protective Gear

Welding is inherently hazardous. The arc produces UV radiation intense enough to cause permanent eye damage in under a minute. The fumes contain heavy metals that cause neurological damage with repeated exposure. The heat and sparks ignite fires. Understanding these hazards and the controls that protect against them isn't optional background reading — it's the foundation of being a welder.

---

## Eye and Face Protection

### Arc Flash

The welding arc produces intense UV and infrared radiation across a broad spectrum. Unprotected exposure — even brief, indirect exposure — causes arc eye (photokeratitis): an extremely painful sunburn on the cornea. Symptoms appear 6-12 hours after exposure (often in the middle of the night). Treatment is supportive care; it heals in 24-48 hours but is temporarily debilitating.

Chronic unprotected exposure causes cataracts.

**Shade ratings:** The darkness of a welding filter is rated by shade number. Higher number = darker.

| Process | Recommended Shade |
|---|---|
| MIG (up to 160A) | Shade 10 |
| MIG (160-250A) | Shade 11-12 |
| Stick (up to 150A) | Shade 10 |
| Stick (150-300A) | Shade 12 |
| TIG (up to 50A) | Shade 8 |
| TIG (50-150A) | Shade 10 |
| TIG (150-500A) | Shade 12 |
| Plasma cutting | Shade 8-9 |
| Gas welding/cutting | Shade 4-6 |

### Auto-Darkening Helmets

Passive (fixed-shade) helmets are the old standard — flip the shade down, look at the arc, lift to check the weld. Auto-darkening helmets have a lens that reacts to the arc in milliseconds (typically 1/25,000 second), transitioning from a light state (shade 3-4) to the selected dark state.

**Advantages of auto-darkening:**
- You can see the joint clearly before striking the arc — better arc placement
- No need to flip the helmet repeatedly, reducing neck strain and lost time
- Essential for TIG welding where precise electrode positioning is critical

**What to look for:**
- Switching speed: 1/25,000 second or faster (some cheap helmets are much slower and allow harmful exposure during the transition)
- Sensitivity adjustability: for low-amperage TIG where the arc is less bright
- Solar + battery powered: solar-only helmets may not work indoors

### Side Shields and Flash Protection

The arc can reflect off walls and nearby surfaces. Other welders working nearby are a hazard. Wear side shields on safety glasses worn under the helmet, or use a welding helmet with side protection.

Never look at another person's arc directly, even briefly, without proper eye protection.

## Respiratory Protection

Welding fumes are a serious occupational health hazard. The composition depends on what's being welded, the process, and the consumables:

**Common fume components:**
- **Iron oxide:** From welding carbon steel. Main bulk of MIG and stick fumes. Causes siderosis with extreme exposure.
- **Manganese:** Present in most steel filler metals. Chronic manganese exposure causes manganism — a Parkinson's-like neurological disorder affecting movement, balance, and cognition. Manganism is irreversible.
- **Hexavalent chromium (Cr VI):** From welding stainless steel. A Group 1 human carcinogen. Causes lung cancer with repeated inhalation. Even low-level exposure requires controls.
- **Zinc oxide:** From welding galvanized (zinc-coated) steel. Causes metal fume fever — a flu-like illness (chills, fever, muscle aches) starting 4-8 hours after exposure. Unpleasant but usually resolves within 24 hours. Repeated exposure may have longer-term effects.
- **Lead:** From welding lead-painted surfaces. Neurotoxic, accumulates in the body.
- **Cadmium:** From cadmium-plated materials. Acute high-level exposure can be fatal. Chronic exposure causes kidney damage and lung cancer.
- **Nickel compounds:** From welding stainless and nickel alloys. Carcinogenic.

### Controls — Hierarchy of Priority

**Elimination/substitution:** Don't weld galvanized if alternatives exist. Use coated electrodes with lower fume generation when available.

**Engineering controls:**
- **Local exhaust ventilation (LEV):** Capture fumes at the source before they reach breathing zone. A fume extraction arm positioned 6-8" from the weld is significantly more effective than general ventilation. The ACGIH recommendation is to capture fumes within 12" of the arc.
- **Backdraft hoods:** For fixed stations, a hood behind the work captures rising fumes.
- **General ventilation:** Ensures the shop air is turned over — not sufficient alone for chronic heavy-production welding.

**Personal protective equipment:**
- An N95 respirator provides approximately 95% filtration of airborne particles, including metal fume. Adequate for occasional mild-steel MIG welding in a well-ventilated space.
- A P100 particulate respirator (half-face or full-face) provides 99.97% filtration. Use for stainless, galvanized, and heavy production welding.
- Supplied-air respirators for confined space welding or heavy stainless production.

**What not to rely on:**
- General building ventilation alone for heavy production
- Opening a door or window and "hoping the breeze helps"
- The idea that welding fumes are just "smoke" and no big deal — they're heavy metals

### Welding Galvanized Steel

If you must weld galvanized, grind off the zinc coating at least 2" from the weld zone on both sides. After welding, the zinc in the surrounding heat-affected zone will still fume as it burns off. Weld outdoors or with strong local exhaust, wear a respirator, and limit exposure time.

Never weld galvanized in a confined or poorly ventilated space.

## Fire and Burn Prevention

Welding sparks and spatter travel 30-35 feet in all directions. A single spark landing in combustible material can smolder for hours before igniting.

**Hot work permit system:** In industrial environments, welding and cutting in non-designated areas requires a hot work permit — a formal checklist that ensures the area is cleared of combustibles, a fire watch is posted, and a fire extinguisher is within reach. Even in your own shop, this discipline saves buildings.

**Before welding:**
- Clear or cover combustibles within 35 feet
- Cover floor drains, gaps, and open wall penetrations (sparks fall into them)
- Move compressed gas cylinders away from the work area (sparks on cylinder valves are dangerous; store upright, secured)
- Know where the fire extinguisher is — you should be able to reach it in 10 seconds

**Fire watch:** After welding, check the area for smoldering material. Stay in the area for 30 minutes after finishing; smoldering fires take time to develop into flames.

**Fire extinguisher:** Minimum ABC dry chemical extinguisher in every welding area. Know how to use it: pull the pin, aim at the base of the flame, squeeze, sweep side to side.

## Personal Protective Equipment Summary

**Welding helmet:** Auto-darkening, appropriate shade for the process and amperage.

**Safety glasses:** Worn under the helmet. Protect from chipping slag after the arc is off.

**Hearing protection:** Welding equipment, grinders, and hammering on metal produce sustained high noise levels. Earplugs or muffs.

**Welding gloves:** Thick leather for stick and MIG (handles significant spatter and heat). Thinner TIG gloves for hand sensitivity when feeding filler rod.

**Welding jacket or sleeves:** Leather or flame-resistant (FR) cotton. Protect arms from arc radiation, sparks, and spatter. Standard cotton or polyester is inappropriate — synthetic fabrics melt and burn on the skin.

**Leather boots:** Protect feet from spatter. Steel-toe optional for the welding itself, but if you're lifting material, they're important. Don't wear athletic shoes or low-cut footwear.

**No synthetic fabrics:** Polyester, nylon, and blended fabrics melt when sparks land on them and can stick to skin. Wear 100% cotton or FR fabrics under the welding jacket.

## Confined Space Welding

Welding in confined spaces (tanks, vessels, pipe trenches, pits) adds several life-threatening hazards:

**Oxygen deficiency:** Shielding gas (argon, CO2) is heavier than air and accumulates in low areas, displacing oxygen. A space with 16% O₂ causes impaired judgment. Below 14%, loss of consciousness. Below 10%, rapid loss of consciousness and death.

**Fume concentration:** Fumes have nowhere to go in a confined space — concentrations build rapidly to hazardous levels.

**Fire and explosion:** Residual flammable vapors or gases in old fuel tanks, chemical vessels, or piping can ignite from the arc or spatter.

**Confined space protocol:**
- Test the atmosphere before entry (oxygen level, flammable gas, toxic gases)
- Continuous ventilation with fresh air during welding
- An attendant outside the space at all times when a welder is inside
- Emergency rescue equipment ready (retrieval line, SCBA for rescuer if needed)
- Do not enter a confined space alone without a trained attendant and atmospheric testing

## Electrical Safety

**Touch potential:** The welding machine produces 20-100V open-circuit voltage (OCV). While lower than household current, it can still cause shock — especially if skin is wet or has cuts. Don't touch the electrode holder and the work clamp simultaneously.

**Electrode holder:** Use only fully insulated holders. Replace damaged holders. Never rest a live electrode holder on the workpiece.

**Wet conditions:** Never weld in rain or standing water. Moisture dramatically increases shock hazard.

**Welding leads:** Inspect cables before each use. Damaged insulation is a shock and fire hazard. Repair or replace.

**Pacemakers and other implants:** Strong electromagnetic fields from welding equipment can interfere with some cardiac devices. People with pacemakers should consult their physician before working around welding equipment.
