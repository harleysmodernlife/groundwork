## The Internal Combustion Engine

An internal combustion engine converts chemical energy (fuel) into mechanical energy (rotation). Understanding how it works explains why routine maintenance matters, what symptoms mean, and what a mechanic is talking about when they diagnose your car.

---

## The Four-Stroke Cycle

Most car engines run on the four-stroke Otto cycle. Each cylinder repeats this sequence continuously:

**1. Intake stroke:** The piston moves down. The intake valve opens. The piston's downward motion creates low pressure that draws an air-fuel mixture (or just air, in a direct-injection engine where fuel is added separately) into the cylinder.

**2. Compression stroke:** Both valves close. The piston moves up, compressing the air-fuel mixture into a small space. Compression ratio describes how much — a 10:1 compression ratio compresses the mixture to 1/10 of its original volume. Higher compression means more power and efficiency, but also requires higher-octane fuel.

**3. Power stroke:** Near the top of the compression stroke, the spark plug fires (or in a diesel, the highly compressed air is hot enough to ignite the fuel). The burning mixture expands rapidly, pushing the piston down with force. This is the only stroke that produces power.

**4. Exhaust stroke:** The exhaust valve opens. The piston moves back up, pushing the burned exhaust gases out of the cylinder and into the exhaust system.

This repeats hundreds of times per minute. At highway speed, each cylinder fires about 30-50 times per second.

## Engine Configuration

### Number of Cylinders

**4-cylinder:** Most common in passenger cars and smaller crossovers. Good fuel economy, adequate power for most uses. Common displacements: 1.5L–2.5L.

**6-cylinder (V6 or inline-6):** More power and smoothness than a 4-cylinder. Common in midsize cars, trucks, and SUVs. Inline-6s are inherently balanced (BMW, older Jeep/Toyota) and prized for smoothness.

**8-cylinder (V8):** Trucks, performance cars, larger SUVs. High power output. Less common in new vehicles due to fuel economy regulations — many trucks now use turbocharged V6s that match V8 performance at better economy.

### Engine Layout

**Inline (I):** Cylinders in a single row. Simple, less wide than a V. Inline-4 and inline-6 are the most common.

**V-type:** Cylinders in two banks at an angle (typically 60° or 90°). More compact than an inline for the same cylinder count. Most V6 and V8 engines.

**Flat (Boxer):** Cylinders horizontal, opposing each other. Subaru and Porsche. Very low center of gravity, good balance.

## Key Engine Components

### Cylinder Block and Head

The **block** is the main casting containing the cylinders, coolant passages, and oil passages. The **head** sits on top of the block and contains the combustion chambers, valves, and camshaft(s). They're sealed by the **head gasket** — if it fails, coolant and oil mix, which destroys the engine quickly.

### Pistons and Connecting Rods

**Pistons** are the moving part in each cylinder. They're connected to the **crankshaft** via **connecting rods** (con rods). The crankshaft converts the pistons' linear up-and-down motion into rotational motion that drives the transmission. The crankshaft is supported by main bearings — if these wear out, the engine makes a deep knock that worsens under load (a sign of serious engine wear).

### Camshaft and Valvetrain

The **camshaft** controls the opening and closing of intake and exhaust valves. It's driven off the crankshaft at half the crankshaft speed (one complete valve cycle per two piston strokes).

**Timing:** The camshaft must be synchronized precisely with the crankshaft — valves open at exactly the right point in each stroke. This synchronization is maintained by the timing chain (metal, inside the engine, bathed in oil, last for the engine's life) or timing belt (rubber, outside the engine, requires replacement at intervals — typically 60,000-100,000 miles).

**If a timing belt breaks while the engine is running:** In an "interference" engine, the open valves are struck by the pistons — catastrophic engine damage, repair cost typically exceeds the car's value. Know if your engine has a timing belt and when it was last replaced.

**OHV vs DOHC:** Older engines have the camshaft in the block (pushrod OHV engines — classic V8 design, simple and robust). Modern engines typically have twin overhead camshafts (DOHC) in the head — allows more precise valve timing, higher RPM capability, and better performance.

### Fuel System

**Port injection:** Fuel is sprayed into the intake port before the valve. Simple, good at cleaning carbon from valve stems. Common through the 2010s.

**Direct injection (GDI):** Fuel is injected directly into the cylinder under very high pressure. More efficient, higher power. Downside: no fuel washing the intake valves, so carbon builds up on valve stems over time — requires periodic walnut blasting or intake cleaning.

**Turbocharger:** A turbine driven by exhaust gases that compresses intake air, allowing more air (and therefore more fuel) into the cylinder. Significantly increases power from a small engine. Turbo engines run higher cylinder pressures and heat — oil quality and change intervals are more critical.

**Supercharger:** An air compressor driven by the engine via belt. Immediate response (no turbo lag), consistent power, but always takes some power from the engine to run.

## Lubrication System

The engine oil system circulates oil under pressure to every moving part — crankshaft bearings, camshaft bearings, valve lifters, timing chain. Without oil pressure, metal parts contact each other directly at high speed — engine seizes within seconds.

**Oil pump:** Driven by the crankshaft, maintains oil pressure throughout the engine. Low oil pressure warning light (not the oil level light — the pressure light) means the engine is being damaged in real time. Stop immediately.

**Oil viscosity:** The "5W-30" or "0W-20" designation. The first number (with W for winter) is the viscosity when cold — how easily it flows at startup. The second number is the viscosity at operating temperature. Thinner oils (0W-20) flow faster at startup, protecting cold engines better and improving fuel economy. Use the viscosity specified in the owner's manual — using the wrong viscosity can void warranty and cause damage.

**Oil change intervals:** Modern conventional oil: 5,000-7,500 miles. Synthetic oil: 7,500-10,000 miles, often 15,000 on manufacturer recommendation with synthetic in newer cars. Follow the manufacturer's interval, not the old "3,000 mile" rule — it's outdated and leads to over-servicing.

## Cooling System

The engine produces enormous heat. The cooling system keeps it within the operating range (typically 195-220°F) where efficiency is highest and components aren't damaged.

**Coolant (antifreeze):** A mixture of water and ethylene glycol (or propylene glycol) that has a higher boiling point and lower freezing point than water. Also contains corrosion inhibitors. Replace per manufacturer interval (typically 30,000-100,000 miles depending on type) — worn coolant becomes acidic and corrodes aluminum components.

**Thermostat:** A wax-pellet valve that keeps coolant circulating within the engine until it reaches operating temperature, then opens to allow flow through the radiator. A stuck-closed thermostat causes overheating. A stuck-open thermostat causes the engine to never reach operating temperature (poor fuel economy, excessive wear, heater doesn't work well).

**Radiator:** The heat exchanger where coolant releases heat to the air. Fins must be clean and unobstructed.

**Water pump:** Circulates coolant throughout the system. Often driven by the timing belt — if it fails, so does cooling. Frequently replaced together with timing belt service to avoid doing it twice.

## Ignition System

**Spark plugs:** Create the electrical arc that ignites the air-fuel mixture. Each cylinder has one (or two on some engines). They wear over time — the gap increases as the electrode erodes, requiring a higher voltage to fire. Replace at manufacturer's interval (copper plugs: 30,000 miles; iridium/platinum: 60,000-100,000 miles). Worn plugs cause misfires, rough idle, and poor fuel economy.

**Coil-on-plug (COP):** Modern engines have an individual ignition coil for each spark plug, mounted directly on the plug. No distributor, no plug wires. More reliable, allows more precise ignition timing.

**Knock sensor:** Detects engine knock (detonation) — uncontrolled combustion that damages pistons and bearings. The ECU retards timing when knock is detected. Running low-octane fuel in a high-compression engine stresses the knock sensor and may reduce power long-term.

## What Common Engine Sounds Mean

**Deep knock at idle that worsens under load:** Worn main or rod bearings. Serious — repair soon.

**Ticking at the top of the engine:** Often valve train noise. May be low oil, worn lifters, or a collapsed lifter. Check oil level first.

**Pinging or rattling under acceleration:** Detonation. Switch to higher-octane fuel. If it continues, get it diagnosed.

**Rattling at cold start that goes away:** Timing chain tensioner or cam phasers — common on some engines. May worsen over time.

**Rhythmic ticking from one spot:** Often an exhaust manifold leak. The hot exhaust gas escapes through a cracked manifold or failed gasket. Not immediately catastrophic but damages the catalytic converter over time.
