## Brakes and Suspension

Brakes stop the car. Suspension keeps the tires in contact with the road and isolates the passenger compartment from road imperfections. Both systems affect safety directly — worn brakes can't stop the car reliably, and worn suspension reduces your ability to steer and control the vehicle. Understanding how they work tells you what symptoms mean and what maintenance they need.

---

## Disc Brakes

Most modern cars use disc brakes on all four wheels. Some economy cars and light trucks still use drum brakes on the rear.

**Components:**
- **Rotor (disc):** A cast iron (or sometimes carbon-ceramic on performance cars) disc that rotates with the wheel
- **Caliper:** A hydraulic clamp that squeezes the brake pads against the rotor
- **Brake pads:** Friction material bonded to a steel backing plate that clamps against the rotor
- **Brake lines:** Carry hydraulic fluid from the master cylinder to the calipers

**How it works:** When you press the brake pedal, it pushes fluid through the hydraulic system. The fluid pressure acts on pistons in each caliper, squeezing the pads against the rotor. Friction between the pad and rotor converts the wheel's kinetic energy into heat, slowing the car.

**Pad wear:** The friction material wears down over time. New pads have ~10-12mm of material. Most pads have a wear indicator — a small metal tab that contacts the rotor when material is low (about 2-3mm), causing a squealing noise. Replace before the indicator wears away — metal-on-metal contact damages rotors and reduces braking effectiveness dramatically.

**Rotor condition:** Rotors develop grooves from pad material, and can warp from heat cycling. Check rotor thickness with a micrometer — each rotor has a minimum thickness specification cast into it. Below minimum, replace. Grooves deeper than 1.5mm require replacement or machining. Warped rotors cause pulsation through the brake pedal when braking — a characteristic feeling of the pedal pumping under your foot.

**Caliper service:** Calipers can seize (the piston doesn't retract fully), causing drag, uneven pad wear, and overheating. A seized caliper often causes the car to pull to one side when braking. The caliper must be rebuilt or replaced.

## Drum Brakes

Still used on rear wheels of many economy cars and trucks. The drum rotates with the wheel. Inside, curved shoes lined with friction material press outward against the inside of the drum when the brakes are applied.

**Self-energizing:** In drum brakes, the rotation of the drum pulls the leading shoe tighter into the drum (self-servo action), amplifying the braking force. This makes drums efficient at moderate braking but prone to fade under heavy use (heat causes vapor and gas between shoe and drum).

**Adjustment:** Drum brakes have a self-adjuster that maintains shoe-to-drum clearance, but it can stick. Symptoms of unadjusted drums: excessive pedal travel before the brakes engage, and a pulling sensation as one side drags more than the other.

**Inspection:** Remove the drum to inspect shoe thickness. Replace when lining is below 1/16" at the thinnest point.

## The Hydraulic System

**Master cylinder:** Converts pedal force into hydraulic pressure. Located in the engine compartment, usually with a clear plastic reservoir on top where you can see the fluid level without opening anything. The master cylinder has two separate hydraulic circuits (dual-circuit system) — if one line fails, the other still provides some braking.

**Brake fluid:** DOT-rated hydraulic fluid. Brake fluid is hygroscopic — it absorbs moisture from the air, which lowers its boiling point over time. Moisture-contaminated fluid causes "brake fade" under heavy use as the fluid boils and creates vapor (vapor is compressible; hydraulic fluid is not). Replace brake fluid every 2 years or per manufacturer recommendation.

**DOT ratings:** DOT 3, DOT 4, DOT 5.1 are glycol-based and miscible (can be mixed). DOT 5 is silicone-based and must NOT be mixed with the others. Most cars use DOT 3 or DOT 4. DOT 4 has a higher dry and wet boiling point.

**Brake fluid level:** Check in the reservoir. Low fluid can mean:
1. Pads are worn (as pads wear, more fluid fills the caliper pistons, lowering the reservoir level) — if it's low but not empty and your pads are worn, refill after pad replacement; the level will rise as pistons retract
2. There's a leak — find the source before adding fluid

**Brake bleeding:** Air in the hydraulic lines causes a spongy pedal. Bleed from the furthest wheel (usually rear right) to the closest (front left) to push air through the system.

## ABS (Anti-Lock Braking System)

ABS prevents wheels from locking under hard braking. A locked wheel loses directional control and skids — a rotating wheel at the threshold of locking provides maximum traction. ABS modulates brake pressure automatically, releasing and reapplying many times per second.

**ABS feel:** When ABS activates, you feel a pulsing vibration through the brake pedal. This is normal. Many people feel it and reduce pedal pressure — don't. Press the pedal firmly and steer to avoid the obstacle. ABS lets you steer while braking at maximum deceleration.

**ABS warning light:** A failed wheel speed sensor or ABS module disables ABS. The standard brakes still work, but you've lost the anti-lock function. Diagnose soon — wheel speed sensor replacement is usually straightforward.

## The Parking Brake (Emergency Brake)

A mechanical cable system that operates the rear brakes independently of the hydraulic system. On disc brake cars, the parking brake either uses a small drum mechanism inside the rear rotor hat, or a secondary caliper mechanism.

**Keep it adjusted:** A parking brake that travels too far before engaging (many clicks) needs cable adjustment. It should be firm within 3-5 clicks.

**Use it regularly:** Parking brake cables corrode and seize if never used. On manual cars, engage it every time you park. On automatics, engage it at least occasionally to keep the mechanism free.

## Suspension Fundamentals

The suspension connects the wheels to the vehicle structure. It serves two purposes:
1. **Wheel control:** Keep the tires in contact with the road surface (not bouncing in the air on rough roads)
2. **Isolation:** Absorb road shocks before they reach the passenger compartment

### Springs

Springs support the vehicle weight and absorb large bumps. Types:
- **Coil springs:** Spiral steel wire, most common in modern cars
- **Leaf springs:** Stacked steel leaves, used on truck rear axles
- **Torsion bars:** Twisted steel bars, used on some trucks and SUVs
- **Air springs:** Pneumatic airbags, used on luxury vehicles for adjustable height and self-leveling

Broken springs cause the vehicle to sit lower on one corner and may produce a clunking sound over bumps.

### Shock Absorbers and Struts

Springs control large movements but don't damp oscillation — a car on springs alone would bounce continuously. Shock absorbers damp the spring's oscillation using hydraulic resistance.

**Shock absorber:** A separate component that works alongside the spring. Common on rear suspensions and older vehicles.

**Strut (MacPherson strut):** Combines the shock absorber and spring into a single structural unit that also serves as the upper pivot point for the wheel. Common on front suspensions.

**Worn shocks/struts:** Cause excessive bouncing after bumps (the "bounce test" — push down on a corner, release; it should settle within one to two bounces). Worn struts also affect handling: the car nose-dives under braking, leans excessively in corners, and tires bounce over rough pavement (reducing contact patch). Replace in pairs (both fronts or both rears).

### Control Arms and Ball Joints

**Control arms** connect the wheel hub to the chassis, controlling wheel position during suspension travel. They pivot on bushings at the chassis end and a ball joint at the wheel end.

**Ball joints** allow the control arm to pivot in all directions while supporting the wheel. They're grease-filled and wear over time. A worn ball joint causes a clunking sound over bumps and imprecise steering feel. A completely failed ball joint causes the wheel to separate from the suspension — catastrophic and dangerous.

**Bushings:** Rubber (or polyurethane) sleeves in the pivot points of control arms, sway bar links, and other suspension components. They wear and crack over time, causing knocking or clunking noises and vague handling. Bushing replacement is often cheaper than replacing the entire control arm.

### Tie Rods

Connect the steering rack to the wheel hub. When you turn the steering wheel, the rack pushes/pulls the tie rods, which pivot the wheels.

**Inner tie rod:** Connects to the steering rack. Failure is uncommon.
**Outer tie rod end:** Connects the inner rod to the wheel hub. Has a ball-and-socket design that wears with use. A loose or worn outer tie rod causes imprecise steering, vague feel, and wandering. Test: grab the tire at 9 and 3 o'clock and try to wiggle — movement indicates a worn tie rod (or wheel bearing). Worn tie rods also cause rapid tire wear.

### Sway Bar (Anti-Roll Bar)

A torsional spring connecting the left and right suspension on each axle. It resists body roll in corners by transmitting force from the compressed (outside) suspension to the extended (inside) suspension.

**Sway bar end links:** Short links connecting the bar to the control arms. They wear and develop play, causing a knocking sound over bumps, especially at low speed. Inexpensive to replace.

**Sway bar bushings:** Mount the bar to the chassis. Wear causes knocking or creaking, especially in slow parking lot maneuvers.

## Steering Systems

**Power steering (hydraulic):** An engine-driven pump pressurizes power steering fluid, which assists the rack in turning the wheels. Requires periodic fluid checks and eventual fluid replacement. A whining noise that changes with steering input indicates a low fluid level or failing pump.

**Electric power steering (EPS):** An electric motor assists the rack, eliminating the hydraulic pump. No fluid to maintain. Power steering failure warning light means the assist has failed — steering still works but requires much more effort.

## Signs of Brake and Suspension Problems

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Squealing when braking | Pad wear indicator — replace pads soon |
| Grinding when braking | Metal-on-metal — pads gone, rotor damage |
| Pulsating pedal | Warped rotors |
| Pulling when braking | Seized caliper, uneven pad wear |
| Spongy pedal | Air in brake fluid |
| Pedal goes to floor | Major leak or master cylinder failure |
| Bouncing after bumps | Worn shocks/struts |
| Clunking over bumps | Worn ball joints, tie rod ends, sway bar links, or bushings |
| Car pulls to one side | Wheel alignment, tire pressure, or brake drag |
| Steering wheel shakes | Unbalanced tires, worn tie rods, or loose hub |
| Uneven tire wear | Alignment problem, worn suspension, improper inflation |
