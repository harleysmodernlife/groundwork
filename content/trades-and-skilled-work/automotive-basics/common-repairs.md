## Common Repairs You Can Do Yourself

A lot of car repair is straightforward if you have basic mechanical ability, the right tools, and the willingness to look up your specific vehicle. The labor rate at a shop is $100-200/hour. A brake job that takes a shop 1.5 hours costs you $150-300 in labor plus parts — the same job takes you 2-3 hours the first time, costs the price of parts only, and teaches you the car. Here are the repairs that give the best return on learning.

---

## Prerequisites for DIY Repair

**Get the correct information for your vehicle.** A repair procedure for a 2018 Honda Accord is different from the same-named repair on a 2018 Toyota Camry. Sources:

- **Factory service manual (FSM):** The most complete and accurate. Available as PDFs online for most vehicles.
- **Alldata DIY, Mitchell1:** Subscription services providing factory procedures and specifications.
- **YouTube:** Invaluable for visual confirmation of how something comes apart. Search your exact year/make/model + repair.
- **Model-specific forums:** Other owners of your vehicle have probably done the same repair and discussed it at length.

**Tools you'll use on almost every repair:**
- Combination wrench set (metric and standard)
- Socket set (1/4", 3/8", and 1/2" drive)
- Ratchets and extensions
- Torque wrench (crucial — overtorqued fasteners cause as much damage as undertorqued)
- Floor jack and jack stands (never work under a vehicle supported only by a hydraulic jack)
- Breaker bar for stuck fasteners
- Penetrating oil (PB Blaster, Kroil) for corroded fasteners

## Brake Pad Replacement

**Difficulty:** Moderate (beginner with guidance)
**Tools:** Floor jack, jack stands, lug wrench, socket set, C-clamp or brake caliper piston tool
**Time:** 1-2 hours for all four corners

Brake pads are one of the most common DIY repairs, and the skills transfer to many other repairs. Disc brake pads on all four corners is straightforward on most vehicles.

**Process:**
1. Loosen the lug nuts before jacking the car.
2. Jack the car and support on jack stands. Remove the wheel.
3. Inspect the rotor face for scoring or thickness issues before proceeding.
4. Remove the caliper bolts (usually two slide pins with 12-17mm bolts). The caliper slides off.
5. Hang the caliper with a wire hook to a spring or nearby bracket. Don't let it hang by the brake line.
6. Slide out the old pads from the caliper bracket.
7. Retract the caliper piston with a C-clamp (one-piston caliper) or a brake piston tool (multi-piston calipers). Put a rag over the reservoir cap — as the piston goes in, fluid is pushed back to the reservoir. Open the cap to prevent overflow.
8. Apply brake caliper grease to the pad ears (where the pad contacts the caliper bracket) — not to the friction surface.
9. Install new pads, reinstall caliper, torque bolts to specification.
10. Before driving: pump the brake pedal until it feels firm. The piston retracted during step 7 — pumping re-extends it to contact the new pads.

**When to replace rotors:** If thickness is below minimum (stamped on the rotor), if the rotor has deep grooves (more than 1.5mm), or if the rotor is under minimum thickness including material that would be removed by machining. On high-mileage cars, replacing rotors with new pads is cheap insurance.

**Parts cost:** Pads typically $20-60 per axle. Rotors $30-60 each. Total DIY cost for both axles: $100-200 vs $400-600+ at a shop.

## Oil Change

**Difficulty:** Easy
**Tools:** Oil drain pan, oil filter wrench, socket for drain plug
**Time:** 30-45 minutes

Probably the most valuable DIY skill — every 5,000-10,000 miles for the life of the vehicle.

**Process:**
1. Warm the engine briefly (3-5 minutes) — warm oil drains faster. Don't run hot or you'll burn yourself.
2. Position your drain pan under the oil drain plug (underside of the engine, not the transmission).
3. Remove the drain plug with the correct socket. Have the pan ready — oil comes out fast.
4. Remove the oil filter (usually accessible from above or through the wheel well). Oil will drip out when the filter breaks loose. Some oil will remain in the filter — tilt it carefully.
5. Install the new filter. Apply a thin film of new oil to the rubber gasket. Hand-tighten until the gasket contacts the surface, then 3/4 turn more. Don't overtighten — it makes the next change harder and can damage the gasket.
6. Reinstall the drain plug. Most have a new crush washer each time — reusing a worn washer leaks. Torque to spec (typically 25-30 ft-lb depending on thread size — use a torque wrench).
7. Add new oil through the fill cap on top of the engine. Add the specified quantity (usually 4-6 quarts), start and run for 30 seconds, shut off, wait 2 minutes, check the dipstick.
8. Dispose of old oil at any auto parts store (free).

**The most common mistake:** Not checking for leaks. After the first oil change, look under the car the next day. A slow drip at the drain plug or filter is common if the gasket is wrong or the torque is off.

## Air Filter Replacement

**Difficulty:** Very easy
**Tools:** None or screwdriver
**Time:** 5-10 minutes

Most air filters live in a plastic box (airbox) with a metal clip or screws holding the lid. Open the lid, note the orientation of the old filter, put the new one in the same orientation, close the lid. Done. Inspect the air intake tube for cracks while you have the lid open.

**Cabin filter:** Similar simplicity, usually accessed by removing a panel behind or under the glove box. YouTube search your model for the exact location.

## Spark Plug Replacement

**Difficulty:** Easy to moderate (depends on engine access)
**Tools:** Spark plug socket (5/8" or 13/16"), ratchet with long extension, torque wrench
**Time:** 30-60 minutes for most 4-cylinders; longer for rear-bank cylinders on V6/V8

**Process:**
1. Work one plug at a time — remove the coil-on-plug (usually one bolt), disconnect the electrical connector, and pull the coil.
2. Use a magnetic spark plug socket on a long extension. Blow out any debris from the plug well with compressed air before removing (prevents debris from falling into the cylinder).
3. Remove the plug. Note the condition — a tan/light brown color is normal. Black/sooty indicates rich running. White/burned indicates lean. Oily plugs indicate oil burning.
4. Check gap on new plug with a feeler gauge. Iridium plugs are pre-gapped and should not need adjustment.
5. Install by hand first — spark plug threads are easy to cross-thread. Turn by hand at least two full turns before using the socket.
6. Torque to spec (typically 10-20 ft-lb depending on thread size). In aluminum heads, overtorquing strips the threads — a much worse problem than undercutting by 2 ft-lb.

## Battery Replacement

**Difficulty:** Easy
**Tools:** Combination wrench or socket (typically 10mm)
**Time:** 15-20 minutes

**Process:**
1. Disconnect the negative (black) terminal first, then the positive (red). Removing the negative first prevents shorting the positive to the chassis.
2. Remove the battery hold-down clamp (a bracket or strap).
3. Lift out the battery. Batteries are heavy — 30-50 lbs.
4. Clean the terminal clamps with a wire brush and baking soda solution if corroded.
5. Install the new battery in the same orientation.
6. Connect the positive terminal first, then the negative.
7. Apply terminal grease or felt terminal pads to prevent future corrosion.

**After replacement:** The car's computer may have lost its memory (radio presets, window auto-up/down calibration, idle adaptation). Most modules re-learn within a few drive cycles. Some features (like one-touch window operation) need manual recalibration per the owner's manual.

## Serpentine Belt Replacement

**Difficulty:** Moderate
**Tools:** Belt routing diagram (on sticker in the engine compartment), breaker bar or serpentine belt tool, possibly a 3/8" ratchet for spring-loaded tensioners
**Time:** 30-45 minutes

**Critical:** Photo or copy the belt routing diagram before removing the old belt. The diagram shows exactly how the belt wraps around each pulley — getting it wrong wastes a new belt and can damage the alternator.

**Process:**
1. Locate the tensioner — a spring-loaded pulley that keeps the belt tight.
2. Insert a breaker bar or serpentine belt tool into the tensioner's square drive hole and rotate against the spring tension to relieve belt tension.
3. Slide the belt off one of the pulleys while holding the tensioner.
4. Route the new belt per the diagram, leaving the tensioner for last.
5. Tension the new belt by the same method.

## Wiper Blades

**Difficulty:** Trivial
**Tools:** None
**Time:** 5 minutes

Check your vehicle for the blade style (hook attachment, pin attachment, or bayonet). Replacement blades have instructions on the package. Lift the blade arm, pinch the release tab, slide the old blade off, slide the new blade on until it clicks. Repeat for the other side. Front blades typically take 1 minute each.

## Wheel Bearing Replacement

**Difficulty:** Moderate to hard
**Tools:** Depends significantly on vehicle — may need a hub puller, bearing press, or large sockets
**Time:** 1-3 hours

Modern FWD vehicles often use a sealed hub assembly (hub + bearing as one unit) that bolts to the steering knuckle. These are often DIY-accessible without a press.

Older vehicles may have separate tapered roller bearings that require a bearing press to install properly.

A growling or humming noise that changes pitch with speed (and may change when the steering wheel is turned) indicates a wheel bearing. Testing: drive at moderate speed and swerve left and right gently. If the noise changes loudly when weight shifts onto one side, that's the bearing — it's failing when loaded.

## When a Job Goes Wrong

**Fastener strips:** Apply penetrating oil, wait 15-30 minutes, try again with a proper-fitting socket (not a worn one). Impact driver helps. Last resort: bolt extractors.

**Fastener rounds off:** Caused by using the wrong size tool or a worn tool. A bolt extractor or left-hand drill bit removes rounded fasteners. Prevent by using only metric on metric, standard on standard, and replacing worn sockets.

**Broke something:** Stop and assess. Look up what you broke — it may be a known fragile clip or plastic fastener that's a $5 part. Proceeding in frustration makes it worse.

**Can't complete the job:** Don't leave a car disassembled if you need it. Document where you are, re-secure anything unsafe, and pick it up fresh. Or load it onto a flatbed for the shop to complete.
