## Routine Maintenance: What, When, and Why

The single biggest factor in how long a car lasts and how much it costs you over time is whether you maintain it. A well-maintained car with 200,000 miles is more reliable than a neglected car with 80,000. Most routine maintenance is simple, predictable, and cheap — the only reason people skip it is that they don't know what it is or why it matters.

---

## Oil Changes

Engine oil lubricates, cools, and cleans internal engine components. Over time, it degrades: the base oil breaks down from heat and oxidation, additives deplete, and the oil accumulates combustion byproducts, metal particles, and sludge. Old oil can't do its job.

**Change interval:**
- Conventional oil: 5,000-7,500 miles
- Synthetic oil: 7,500-15,000 miles (follow manufacturer specification)
- Check your owner's manual — many modern cars specify 10,000 miles with synthetic

The old "3,000 mile" rule is outdated and was largely a marketing creation. Modern engines with modern synthetic oil go longer. Follow the manufacturer, not the quick-lube shop's reminder sticker.

**Oil type:** Viscosity (e.g., 5W-30, 0W-20) and specification (GF-6A, SP). Use exactly what the manufacturer specifies. Wrong viscosity causes reduced protection. Using 5W-30 in an engine that calls for 0W-20 can void powertrain warranty and reduce fuel economy.

**Checking oil level:** Pull the dipstick, wipe clean, reinsert fully, pull again. Oil level should be between the min and max marks. Check when cold for the most accurate reading. Check every 1,000-2,000 miles between changes — some engines consume a quart or more between changes, especially older engines or performance engines.

**Oil color:** New oil is amber. Dark brown/black after use is normal — the oil is doing its job carrying away combustion deposits. Milky/gray oil indicates coolant contamination (blown head gasket). Metallic glitter in the oil means internal engine wear — see a mechanic.

## Air Filter

The engine air filter prevents dirt and debris from entering the engine. A clogged filter restricts airflow, reducing power and fuel economy.

**Change interval:** 15,000-30,000 miles, or inspect annually. Dusty environments (rural areas, dirt roads) require more frequent replacement. A dirty filter is visibly gray or brown compared to a new white/yellow filter.

**Cabin air filter:** Filters the air entering the HVAC system. Clogged cabin filters reduce airflow from vents and may cause musty smell. Change every 15,000-25,000 miles. Easy to DIY — usually accessible behind the glove box.

## Spark Plugs

Ignite the air-fuel mixture. Worn plugs have a larger electrode gap (the electrode erodes over time), requiring higher voltage to fire. This leads to misfires, rough idle, and poor fuel economy.

**Change interval:**
- Copper/nickel plugs: 30,000 miles
- Platinum plugs: 60,000 miles
- Iridium plugs: 60,000-100,000+ miles

**Symptoms of worn plugs:** Rough idle, hesitation on acceleration, poor fuel economy, Check Engine Light with misfire codes (P0301-P0308 indicate which cylinder is misfiring).

**DIY difficulty:** Straightforward on many engines — pull the coil-on-plug, remove the plug, install new plug to spec torque, reinstall. Some V6 and V8 engines have rear bank plugs that are difficult to access — worth paying for that labor.

## Coolant (Antifreeze)

Maintains operating temperature and prevents corrosion in the cooling system. Degrades over time — the additives deplete and the pH drops, becoming acidic and corroding aluminum components.

**Change interval:**
- Green coolant (IAT — Inorganic Additive Technology): 30,000 miles or 2 years
- Orange/red/pink coolant (OAT or HOAT): 50,000-150,000 miles or 5 years

**Never mix types.** Mixing green and orange coolant creates a gel that clogs the system. If you don't know what's in there, flush completely before refilling.

**Coolant concentration:** Usually 50/50 mix with distilled water (not tap water — minerals in tap water cause scale buildup). 50/50 provides protection to -34°F and boiling protection to 265°F. In extreme cold climates, 70/30 coolant-to-water is used, but overly high coolant concentration actually reduces heat transfer.

## Transmission Fluid

Lubricates gears and clutches, provides hydraulic pressure for automatic transmission operation. Degrades from heat over time.

**Change interval:**
- Automatic transmission: 30,000-60,000 miles (despite "lifetime fluid" claims)
- Manual transmission: 30,000-60,000 miles
- CVT: 30,000-60,000 miles (use only specified CVT fluid)
- DCT: per manufacturer specification

**Signs of degraded ATF:** Dark red or brown color (new fluid is bright red), burnt smell, rough shifts, slipping.

---

## Brake Fluid

Hygroscopic — absorbs moisture from the air through the reservoir cap and brake line micro-permeability. Moisture in brake fluid lowers its boiling point, causing vapor lock and "fade" under heavy braking.

**Change interval:** Every 2 years or per manufacturer recommendation, regardless of mileage.

**Test:** Brake fluid test strips are available at auto parts stores. They measure copper content (a proxy for moisture and corrosion) and indicate when replacement is due.

---

## Power Steering Fluid

**Hydraulic power steering:** Check the reservoir level monthly. Low fluid causes whining. Change interval varies — some manufacturers say "inspect and change as needed," others specify 50,000 miles. If it looks dark or smells burnt, change it.

**Electric power steering:** No fluid to maintain. Done.

---

## Differential and Transfer Case Fluid

**Rear differential (RWD, AWD vehicles):** Heavy gear oil. Change every 30,000-60,000 miles. More frequently for towing or off-road use (heat and contamination from water crossings accelerate degradation).

**Transfer case (4WD vehicles):** Separate fluid, similar interval. Neglecting this is common and expensive when the transfer case fails.

---

## Drive Belt (Serpentine Belt)

A single belt that drives the alternator, power steering pump, A/C compressor, and water pump. When it breaks, everything stops immediately — the engine overheats within minutes without the water pump, the battery discharges without the alternator.

**Change interval:** 60,000-100,000 miles, or when cracking, glazing, or fraying appears.

**Tensioner:** A spring-loaded pulley that keeps the belt at correct tension. Replace together with the belt — a failed tensioner quickly destroys a new belt.

---

## Timing Belt

Synchronizes the crankshaft and camshaft(s). If it breaks, the engine stops — and in interference engines, the valves strike the pistons, causing catastrophic engine damage.

**Change interval:** 60,000-105,000 miles (check your specific vehicle — this is non-negotiable and model-specific).

**Water pump:** Usually driven by the timing belt. Replace it at the same time — the labor cost of accessing the water pump is already paid for.

**Timing chains:** Some engines use a metal chain instead of a rubber belt. Chains can last the engine's life but require oil changes to be kept clean. A rattling from the timing area on cold start is a warning sign.

---

## Tire Maintenance

**Check pressure monthly** when tires are cold. Use the door jamb specification, not the sidewall maximum. Low pressure causes rapid edge wear, poor fuel economy, and overheating at highway speeds.

**Rotate every 5,000-7,500 miles.** Ensures even wear across all four tires, maximizing tire life.

**Check tread depth.** Replace at 2/32" minimum. For year-round wet driving, 4/32" is safer. Snow performance degrades significantly below 5/32".

**Inspect for damage.** Look for bulges in the sidewall (impact damage — replace immediately), embedded nails or screws (may be holding the air in — repair or replace before removing), and uneven wear patterns (indicates alignment or suspension issues).

## Maintenance Tracking

**Keep records.** A log (paper or app) of every service, mileage, and cost gives you a service history to reference, proves maintenance to future buyers, and lets you notice patterns (if you're replacing the same part repeatedly, something else is wrong).

**Understand the difference:**
- **Scheduled maintenance:** Done based on time or mileage whether or not anything is wrong (oil, filters, fluids)
- **Condition-based maintenance:** Done when inspection shows need (brake pads, tires, belts)
- **Symptom-driven repair:** Done because something isn't working right

Most people do the last two and skip the first. Scheduled maintenance prevents expensive repairs by catching wear before it cascades.

## What You Can DIY vs. What to Pay For

**DIY friendly:**
- Oil and filter change
- Air filter and cabin filter
- Wiper blades
- Battery replacement
- Spark plugs (on accessible engines)
- Fluid top-offs (brake, power steering, washer fluid)
- Fuse replacement

**Reasonable DIY with care:**
- Brake pad replacement (disk brakes)
- Serpentine belt
- Cooling system flush
- Spark plugs on V6/V8 engines (harder access, more cylinders)
- Battery terminal cleaning and cable replacement

**Leave to a shop:**
- Timing belt (critical — no margin for error)
- Transmission fluid on sealed units
- Anything requiring specialized tools (alignment, ABS bleeding)
- Airbag-related work (explosive inflators — not for DIY)
