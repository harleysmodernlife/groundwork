## Drivetrain: Transmission, Axles, and Wheels

The engine produces rotational power. The drivetrain's job is to get that power to the wheels efficiently, allowing the car to move at different speeds, reverse, and transfer power appropriately between wheels. Understanding the drivetrain explains why your car behaves the way it does and what's happening when something goes wrong.

---

## The Transmission

The transmission connects the engine to the drive wheels. Because the engine produces power only within a certain RPM range, the transmission provides different gear ratios — lower gears multiply torque for acceleration from a stop, higher gears reduce RPM for efficient highway cruising.

### Manual Transmission

A set of gear pairs in mesh inside the gearbox. The driver selects which pair transmits power by engaging a gear via the shift lever and clutch.

**Clutch:** A friction disc sandwiched between the flywheel (which rotates with the engine) and the pressure plate. When the clutch pedal is depressed, a release bearing pushes against the pressure plate, disengaging the clutch disc from the flywheel — the engine can then spin freely while the driver shifts gears. Releasing the pedal re-engages the clutch, gradually connecting engine power to the transmission.

**Clutch wear:** The friction disc wears over time. Symptoms of a worn clutch: slipping (RPMs rise without proportional acceleration), difficulty fully engaging in higher gears, burning smell under hard acceleration. Clutch replacement is labor-intensive because the transmission must be removed.

**Synchromesh:** Internal rings in the gearbox that match the speeds of the input and output shafts before the gear engages. This makes smooth shifting without grinding. When synchros wear out, gears grind on engagement — a sign the transmission needs rebuilding.

### Automatic Transmission (Traditional Hydraulic)

Uses a torque converter (a fluid coupling) instead of a clutch, and shifts gears automatically based on speed and throttle position. More convenient, though historically slightly less efficient than manual. Modern automatics with 8-10 speeds often out-fuel-economy manuals.

**Torque converter:** A fluid-filled housing connected to the engine flywheel on one side and the transmission input shaft on the other. At idle, the fluid barely transfers power (the car doesn't lurch forward when in gear). As RPMs rise, the fluid coupling becomes more efficient. Most modern torque converters include a lock-up clutch that mechanically locks at highway speed, eliminating slip for fuel economy.

**Automatic transmission fluid (ATF):** The lifeblood of the automatic transmission. It provides hydraulic pressure to actuate clutch packs, lubricates moving parts, and cools the transmission. Degraded ATF causes hard shifts, slipping, and eventual failure. Many manufacturers say "lifetime fluid" — but lifetime often means the transmission's lifetime (which ends prematurely). Changing ATF every 30,000-60,000 miles is good practice.

**Common failure symptom:** Slipping between gears, shuddering on light acceleration, or delayed engagement when shifting from Park to Drive. Early diagnosis (usually just a fluid service) is far cheaper than rebuilding the transmission.

### CVT (Continuously Variable Transmission)

Uses a belt or chain running between two variable-width pulleys, providing an infinite range of gear ratios rather than fixed steps. The engine can hold at its most efficient RPM while vehicle speed changes.

**Characteristic feel:** CVT-equipped cars often feel like the engine "revs then catches" under acceleration — the engine RPM rises quickly, then the CVT adjusts the ratio to allow acceleration without further RPM rise. Some people find this unnatural.

**CVT fluid:** Must be the specific fluid for the make/model. Using generic ATF in a CVT causes failure. Change per manufacturer interval — CVTs are expensive to replace.

### Dual-Clutch Transmission (DCT)

Two clutch packs — one for odd gears, one for even. While one gear is engaged, the next gear is pre-selected on the other clutch pack. Shifts are extremely fast (faster than a manual). Common on sporty cars and many European vehicles.

**Low-speed behavior:** DCTs can feel jerky in stop-and-go traffic because they're not as smooth as a traditional torque converter at very low speeds.

## Drive Configurations

### FWD (Front-Wheel Drive)

Engine power goes to the front wheels, which also steer. The most common configuration for passenger cars. Packaging advantage — engine, transmission, and drive wheels are all in the same compact front package, maximizing interior space.

**FWD limitations:** Front wheels doing both steering and driving — under hard acceleration, torque steer (pulling to one side) can be an issue. Less suitable for towing than RWD. Handling is generally safe (understeer) but less dynamic.

**CV (constant velocity) joints:** FWD axles need to transmit power while the wheels turn and articulate. CV joints allow this. When a CV joint fails, you hear a clicking noise in turns that gets louder — classic CV joint failure. Failing boots (rubber covers protecting the joint with grease) accelerate failure.

### RWD (Rear-Wheel Drive)

Engine power goes to the rear wheels. The front wheels only steer. Common in trucks, sports cars, performance sedans, and most larger vehicles.

**Advantages:** Better weight distribution (engine front, driving axle rear), better handling balance, greater towing capacity, easier to service front suspension.

**Limitations:** In snow and ice, rear-wheel drive can spin the rear tires and cause oversteer (rear stepping out). Good winter tires significantly mitigate this.

**Driveshaft:** In RWD and 4WD vehicles, a shaft runs from the transmission (front of car) to the rear differential. Universal joints (U-joints) at each end allow the driveshaft to operate through angle changes. Worn U-joints vibrate or clunk — feel it in the drivetrain at low speed or under load.

### AWD (All-Wheel Drive)

All four wheels receive power. The center differential or coupling unit distributes power between front and rear axles, often varying the split electronically.

**Full-time AWD:** Permanently engaged. Common in performance AWD systems. Uses more fuel than 2WD.

**Part-time AWD (clutch-based):** Normally sends most power to one axle, transfers power to the other when slip is detected. Common in crossover SUVs.

**AWD vs 4WD:** True 4WD (found on trucks and body-on-frame SUVs) has a separate transfer case with High and Low range. Low range provides massive mechanical advantage for off-road crawling. AWD systems are optimized for on-road traction in variable conditions, not extreme off-road.

## The Differential

The differential allows the driven wheels to spin at different speeds — essential for cornering, because the outside wheel must travel further than the inside wheel.

**Open differential:** Power takes the path of least resistance. If one wheel is on ice, all power goes to that wheel (which spins freely) and none to the wheel with traction. This is why getting one driven wheel on a slippery surface stalls you.

**Limited-slip differential (LSD):** Uses clutch packs or gears to limit how much speed differential is allowed between the two wheels, sending more torque to the wheel with traction. Common on performance vehicles and trucks.

**Electronic stability control (ESC):** Uses the ABS system to apply individual brake pressure to spinning wheels, effectively mimicking an LSD. Standard equipment on all US vehicles since 2012.

**Differential fluid:** The rear differential (and front differential in AWD) contains gear oil that must be changed at intervals (typically 30,000-60,000 miles). Neglected differential fluid causes whining and eventually bearing and gear failure.

## The Axle and Hub Assembly

**Axle shafts:** Connect the differential to the wheels. On FWD vehicles, these are the CV axles. On solid-axle RWD trucks, the axle shaft is inside a housing and simpler in design.

**Hub and wheel bearing:** The wheel attaches to the hub, which rides on the wheel bearing. Worn wheel bearings make a humming or growling noise that changes pitch with speed and may change when the car is steered left or right (loading one bearing more than the other). Left alone, a failed wheel bearing can cause the wheel to loosen — a safety issue.

**Hub-centric vs. lug-centric wheels:** Factory wheels center on the hub (hub-centric). Aftermarket wheels may center only on the lug nuts (lug-centric), which requires precise torquing. Always torque lug nuts to spec with a torque wrench — both under- and over-torquing cause problems.

**Lug nut torque:** Typically 80-120 lb-ft depending on the vehicle (check the owner's manual). Undertorqued lugs can loosen and allow the wheel to separate. Overtorqued lugs can warp brake rotors and strip the studs.

## Tires and Their Role

Tires are the only contact between the vehicle and the road. Everything the drivetrain does — acceleration, braking, steering — ultimately happens through the tire contact patch, which is roughly the size of your palm per tire.

**Tire rotation:** Move tires between positions to equalize wear. Recommended every 5,000-7,500 miles (often done with oil changes). Front and rear tires wear at different rates — front tires on FWD cars wear faster.

**Tire pressure:** Check monthly when cold (tires add 4-6 PSI when warm). Correct pressure is on the door jamb sticker, not the max pressure on the tire sidewall. Low pressure causes poor fuel economy, uneven wear, and poor handling. High pressure reduces traction and causes center tread wear.

**Tread depth:** New tires have ~10/32" tread. Replace at 2/32" (the legal minimum). Easy check: insert a penny (Lincoln head down) into the tread groove. If you can see Lincoln's head, the tread is below 2/32". A quarter gives you a 4/32" warning before you hit the minimum.

**Balancing:** Tires are balanced at installation by adding small weights to the rim. Imbalance causes vibration at certain speeds. Rebalance if vibration develops or after hitting a significant pothole.

**Alignment:** The geometry of how the wheels point. Misalignment causes uneven wear (tires wearing on one edge) and pulling to one side. Check alignment annually and after any significant impact (curb hit, pothole, suspension work).
