## Lumber, Plywood, and Engineered Wood Products

Every carpentry project starts with material selection. Wood comes in dozens of species, cuts, and manufactured forms — each with specific strengths, weaknesses, costs, and appropriate uses. Knowing what you're working with before you buy prevents expensive mistakes.

---

## Solid Lumber

Solid lumber is sawn directly from logs and dried. It's the traditional material for structural framing, furniture, and finish work.

### Softwoods (Framing and Construction)

Softwoods come from coniferous trees (pine, fir, spruce, cedar) and make up most construction lumber. Despite the name, not all softwoods are actually soft.

**Douglas Fir:** Strong, relatively hard for a softwood. The primary structural lumber in the western US. Excellent stiffness, holds fasteners well.

**Southern Yellow Pine (SYP):** Dominant structural lumber in the Southeast US. Very dense and hard for a softwood. Strong, good for decking and structural applications.

**Spruce-Pine-Fir (SPF):** A combined grade designation covering several similar species. Common framing lumber throughout the US. "2x4" at the lumber yard is usually SPF.

**Western Red Cedar:** Naturally rot-resistant and insect-resistant. Used for siding, decking, fencing, outdoor structures.

**Pressure-Treated Lumber (PT):** Any species (usually SYP) treated with preservative chemicals (ACQ, copper azole) under pressure. Required for ground contact, concrete contact, and exterior applications where decay is a concern. Look for the label specifying the treatment grade (UC3B for above-ground; UC4A/4B for ground contact; UC4C for below the ground/water contact).

### Hardwoods (Furniture and Finish Work)

Hardwoods come from deciduous trees (oak, maple, cherry, walnut). Used for furniture, cabinetry, flooring, and trim where appearance matters.

**Oak (Red and White):** Very hard, strong, prominent grain. Common for furniture and flooring. White oak is more rot-resistant and popular for outdoor furniture. Red oak is more common for interior work.

**Hard Maple:** Very hard and dense. Excellent for cutting boards, butcher block counters, workbench tops. Takes finish well.

**Cherry:** Moderate hardness, beautiful reddish-brown color that deepens with age. A premium furniture wood.

**Walnut:** Rich dark brown, moderately hard, easy to work, takes finish beautifully. Expensive.

**Poplar:** A hardwood that's actually quite soft. Easy to work, accepts paint well, inexpensive. Common for painted furniture and cabinetry where grain won't show.

## Lumber Grades and Sizing

**Nominal vs. actual dimensions:** The lumber industry's famous quirk. A "2x4" is actually 1.5" x 3.5". A "1x6" is 3/4" x 5.5". The nominal size is what the lumber measured when green; it shrinks when dried. Always use actual dimensions in calculations.

**Common dimensional lumber:**
- 1x (three-quarters thick) — boards for shelving, casing, trim
- 2x (1.5" thick) — framing lumber
- 4x (3.5" thick) — posts and beams
- 6x and larger — heavy timbers

**Grade marks:** Structural lumber is graded by stiffness (Modulus of Elasticity, E) and bending strength (Fb). Common grades: Select Structural, No.1, No.2, No.3. No.2 is the standard minimum for most framing.

**For appearance:** Hardwoods are graded by the percentage of clear (defect-free) material on the board surface. FAS (Firsts and Seconds) is the highest grade; Common grades have more knots.

## Plywood

Plywood is layers of wood veneer glued with alternating grain directions, creating a panel that's stable, strong in multiple directions, and doesn't shrink and swell the way solid wood does.

**Construction plywood (CDX, OSB):**
- Grades A through D for each face (appearance)
- CDX: C face, D back, X glue (exterior-grade adhesive) — the most common structural sheathing
- OSB (Oriented Strand Board): Engineered wood made from compressed wood strands. Cheaper than plywood. Standard sheathing for walls and roof decking.

**Hardwood plywood:** Better face veneers for visible applications — cabinet boxes, furniture backs, interior paneling.

**Marine plywood:** No interior voids, waterproof glue. For boats and outdoor applications with water exposure.

**Standard sizes:** 4x8 foot sheets (48" x 96"). Also available in 5x5 and 4x10. Thicknesses: 1/4", 3/8", 1/2", 5/8", 3/4", 1".

## Engineered Wood Products

**LVL (Laminated Veneer Lumber):** Thin wood veneers laminated with adhesive, with grain all running the same direction. Very strong and stable. Used for beams, headers, and long spans where solid wood would be inconsistent or need to be very large.

**Glulam (Glued Laminated Timber):** Layers of solid dimension lumber laminated together. For large beams in timber framing, exposed beam structures, and long spans.

**TJI (Engineered I-Joists):** An I-beam shape with LVL flanges and an OSB web. Long spans with less material than solid lumber, consistent dimensions, no crown or warp. Common for floor framing.

**LDL/PSL (Parallel Strand Lumber):** Strands of wood all running parallel, compressed and glued. Extremely dense and strong. For heavy posts and columns.

## Moisture Content and Movement

Wood moves with moisture. As humidity increases, wood swells; as it decreases, wood shrinks. This movement occurs across the grain, not along it — a board gets wider and narrower with humidity, not longer or shorter.

**Moisture content (MC):** Percentage of water weight relative to dry wood weight. Green lumber can be 50%+ MC. Kiln-dried (KD) lumber is 19% or less for framing, 6-8% for interior finish work.

**Why it matters:**
- Interior woodwork (cabinets, furniture, trim) must be installed at near-final moisture content, or joints will open and materials will warp as the wood adjusts
- Leave boards to acclimate in the space where they'll be installed for several days before milling or installation
- Framing lumber at 19% MC will shrink slightly as the building dries out — expect some settling and nail pops in new construction

**Quartersawn vs. flatsawn:** Quartersawn boards (grain rings roughly perpendicular to the face) move less in width and show a straighter grain figure. Flatsawn (grain rings more parallel to the face) are more common and show wider cathedrals but move more. Matters most for flooring and tabletops.
