## Finish Carpentry: Trim, Doors, and Cabinets

Rough framing builds the structure. Finish carpentry is what people see. It's the trim around windows and doors, the baseboards at the floor, the crown at the ceiling, the cabinets in the kitchen. The tolerances are tight and there's no hiding mistakes under paint — any gap, any out-of-square joint, any waviness is visible. Finish carpentry requires better technique, sharper tools, and more patience than framing.

---

## The Challenge of Finish Work

Houses are not square or perfectly flat. Walls bow. Floors aren't level. Corners aren't exactly 90°. Finish carpentry is the art of making straight, precise pieces look perfect in an imperfect structure.

The tools that make this possible:
- **Miter saw** for precise angled cuts
- **Finish nailer** (15g or 16g for casing; 18g or 23g for smaller trim)
- **Caulk gun** for filling gaps between trim and wall
- **Nail set** for setting nail heads below the surface
- **Scribing tools** for fitting to irregular surfaces

---

## Trim Types and Their Purpose

**Baseboard:** Runs along the wall at the floor, hiding the gap between drywall and flooring and protecting the wall from kicks and vacuums. Typically 3-4" tall in standard homes, 5-6" in higher-end work.

**Door casing:** The trim surrounding door openings on both sides. Hides the rough opening and provides the finished face between the door jamb and the wall. Typically 2-1/4" to 3-1/2" wide.

**Window casing and stool:** Same as door casing for the sides and top of a window, plus the horizontal "stool" at the bottom (the windowsill cap) and the "apron" below the stool.

**Crown molding:** Decorative trim at the wall-ceiling junction. The most complex to install because it sits at an angle between two planes, requiring compound angle cuts on inside and outside corners.

**Chair rail:** A horizontal band of molding at roughly 32-36" (the height where chair backs would contact the wall). Decorative and functional.

**Picture rail:** A narrow molding near the ceiling, historically used to hang artwork from hooks.

## Cutting Angles: Miters and Bevels

**Miter angle:** The angle of the cut in the horizontal plane (left and right). A butt joint is 0° miter. An inside corner needs 45° miters on both pieces.

**Bevel angle:** The angle of the cut in the vertical plane (through the thickness). A standard miter saw cut is 0° bevel (straight down). Crown molding needs both miter and bevel angles simultaneously (a compound cut).

### Inside and Outside Corners

For most trim, corners are either 90° inside corners (where two walls meet inward) or outside corners (where two walls meet outward, like a peninsula wall).

**Outside corners:** Cut both pieces at 45° — right piece at 45° left, left piece at 45° right. The miters meet at the corner's point.

**Inside corners:** Two options — mitered or coped.

Mitered inside corners cut both pieces at 45° meeting in the corner. This works if the corner is exactly 90°. If it's not, the joint will gap. Most corners in real buildings aren't exactly 90°.

Coped joints are the professional approach to inside corners:
1. Cut one piece square (90°) and run it into the corner, full length
2. On the second piece, make a 45° inside miter — this reveals the profile of the molding on the mitered end
3. With a coping saw or jigsaw, cut along the profile revealed by the miter, undercutting the back slightly so only the front face touches the first piece
4. The coped end of the second piece fits over the face of the first piece like a glove

Coped joints look better and survive seasonal movement better than mitered inside corners. The technique takes practice but is worth learning.

## Installing Baseboard

1. **Find the high point of the floor** with a 4-foot level. The baseboard should follow the floor — if you set it perfectly level, you'll have a gap at the high spots.

2. **Start with inside corners** and work toward the outside corners. Run one piece square into the inside corner. Cope the second piece to fit over it.

3. **Outside corners** get 45° miters. Cut both pieces slightly long, dry-fit, then nail. Glue the miter joint with wood glue before nailing.

4. **Nail into studs** with 16g or 15g finish nails. The bottom of the baseboard nails into the sole plate (no stud needed — it's solid throughout). The top nails into studs.

5. **Caulk the top edge** where baseboard meets the wall. This hides any gap and is what gets painted. Don't caulk the bottom edge to the floor — floors move seasonally.

6. **Fill nail holes** with wood filler or lightweight spackling. Let dry, sand flush, prime and paint.

## Door Installation

### Pre-hung Doors

Pre-hung doors come with the door already mounted on the jamb. You install the entire assembly into the rough opening.

**Rough opening size:** Typically door width + 2" wide, door height + 2" tall. The extra space allows for shimming the unit level and plumb.

**Installation:**
1. Set the door unit in the opening. Check that it's plumb and level. The hinge-side jamb is most critical — get that perfectly plumb first.
2. Shim behind the hinge-side jamb at each hinge location. Drive screws through the jamb and shims into the jack stud.
3. Check that the door swings freely and the reveal (gap between door and jamb) is even on the hinge side.
4. Shim the latch side and top. Nail into place.
5. Score the shims with a utility knife and snap them off flush. Install casing over the gap between jamb and wall.

### Adjusting a Door That Doesn't Close Right

**Hinge-bound (door binds on the latch side when closing):** The hinges are set too deep. Remove the hinge and add cardboard shims behind it to bring it out.

**Door swings open or closed on its own:** The jamb isn't plumb. Loosen the screws and re-shim.

**Gap at the top corner of the latch side:** The door is sagging — the hinge screws have worked loose. Replace with 3" screws that reach into the stud behind the jamb.

## Cabinet Installation

Cabinets are installed in two phases: base cabinets (on the floor) then upper cabinets (on the wall).

### Upper Cabinets First

Install uppers before bases so you can stand on the floor without bases in the way. Most installers build a ledger board — a temporary 1x4 screwed to the wall at the bottom of where the uppers will sit. This supports the cabinet weight while you level and fasten.

**Scribing:** If the wall is not plumb, the side of the end cabinet may have a gap. Use a compass or scribe tool to transfer the wall's contour onto the cabinet side, then shave that line with a hand plane or belt sander. The cabinet face must be plumb, not the wall.

### Base Cabinets

Base cabinets sit on the floor. Because floors aren't level, you shim the base cabinets up from the lowest point. Find the high point of the floor in the cabinet run, establish your finished height from there, and shim the other cabinets up to that height.

**Shimming:** Use tapered shims under the cabinet base. Check level front-to-back and side-to-side before fastening.

**Fastening to the wall:** Locate studs. Drive 3" screws through the cabinet's hanging rail (a horizontal strip inside at the top) into the studs.

**Securing adjacent cabinets together:** Clamp cabinets side-by-side, check that the faces are flush, and drive 1-1/4" screws through the stile of one cabinet into the stile of the adjacent one. This creates a solid run.

---

## Scribing

When a wall is wavy or not plumb, a cabinet, shelf, or piece of trim placed against it will have a gap. Scribing fits the piece to the wall:

1. Place the cabinet/trim in its final position, held out from the wall by the size of the largest gap
2. Set a compass or scriber to the width of the largest gap
3. Run the compass along the wall with the pencil marking the piece — the line follows the wall's contour
4. Remove the piece and cut to the line with a jigsaw or hand plane
5. The piece now fits the wall profile exactly

## Caulking and Finishing

Caulk hides the imperfections that are inevitable where trim meets a wall. Use paintable latex caulk (acrylic) for interior trim.

**Technique:** Cut the caulk tube at a 45° angle, small opening — you can always make it bigger, not smaller. Apply a thin, consistent bead. Smooth immediately with a wet finger. Remove the excess in one pass. Let dry before painting.

**What to caulk:** The top edge of baseboard, the joint between casing and wall, the joint between crown and wall and ceiling. Don't caulk trim joints (miters and copes) — use wood glue there.

**Priming matters.** MDF trim (very common) must be primed on all cut ends before installation or they'll absorb moisture and swell. Prime the entire piece before installation, then paint after installation and caulking.
