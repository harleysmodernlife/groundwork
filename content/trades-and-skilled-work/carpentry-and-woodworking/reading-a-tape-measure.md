## Measuring and Layout

Carpentry is applied geometry. You're constantly converting a mental picture into a series of marks on wood, and every one of those marks has to be right. Sloppy measuring means sloppy cuts, and sloppy cuts mean joints that gap, walls that aren't plumb, and projects that don't fit. The tools and habits in this lesson are foundational to everything else.

---

## The Tape Measure

A standard US tape measure reads in feet, inches, and fractions of an inch. Most carpentry is done in inches and fractions, not decimal inches.

### Reading the Markings

Every inch is divided into smaller fractions. The marks are different lengths so you can read them without counting:

- **1" marks:** labeled — the longest marks with numbers
- **1/2" marks:** the second-longest unlabeled mark, exactly midway between inch marks
- **1/4" marks:** the third-longest, dividing each half-inch in half (at 1/4 and 3/4 of an inch)
- **1/8" marks:** shorter again, dividing each quarter in half
- **1/16" marks:** the shortest marks, giving 16 divisions per inch

For rough framing, you work in 1/8" increments. For finish work, you work in 1/16". For fine joinery, you may need a ruler that reads in 1/32".

**Practice:** Look at the 1-inch space on your tape and identify: the 1/2" mark, the 1/4" and 3/4" marks, the 3/8" and 5/8" marks, and the 1/16" marks.

### The Floating Hook

The metal hook at the end of the tape wiggles by exactly one hook-thickness. This is intentional.

- **External measurement (hooking over a board):** the hook slides out to add its own thickness, so the zero point starts at the board's face
- **Internal measurement (pushing hook into a corner):** the hook slides in to subtract its own thickness, so the zero point is at the wall or surface

If your hook is bent or doesn't slide freely, your measurements will be off by the same amount every time. Test it: measure a board by hooking, then by pushing against a stop. Both measurements should be identical.

### Burning an Inch

The first inch of a tape measure takes a beating — the hook gets banged around and the numbers near it can be hard to read clearly. For precise measurements, "burn an inch": position the 1-inch mark at your start point, read the tape, subtract 1 inch. If the tape reads 13", your piece is 12" long.

## Layout Tools

The tape tells you distance. Layout tools help you transfer that distance into lines and angles.

### Speed Square (Rafter Square)

A triangular steel or aluminum square with a lip that hooks over the edge of a board. Probably the most-used layout tool in carpentry.

**Uses:**
- Mark a perfectly square line across a board in seconds (slide the lip along the edge, mark against the blade)
- Used as a saw guide — hold it against a circular saw's base plate to keep the cut straight
- Mark 45° angles using the diagonal edge
- Check corners for square: put both legs in the corner and look for gaps

**The 3-4-5 check for large squares:** On a layout where you can't use a square, measure 3 feet along one line and 4 feet along the perpendicular. If the diagonal between those two points is exactly 5 feet, the corner is square. This works with any multiple: 6-8-10, 9-12-15.

### Combination Square

A sliding square with a ruler, adjustable to any distance. More versatile than a speed square.

**Uses:**
- Mark a line at a consistent distance from the edge (set the ruler to the measurement, slide along the board, mark with a pencil)
- Check 90° and 45° angles
- Check depth: set the ruler to a specific depth to mark mortises, grooves, or rabbet depths

### Chalk Line

A string loaded with chalk in a reel. Stretch it between two points on a surface, snap it, and it leaves a straight chalk line. Essential for layout on floors and large sheet goods where a ruler can't reach.

**Use:** Hook one end at the start point, stretch to the end point keeping tension, pinch the string in the middle, lift and release. The snap deposits chalk in a straight line.

Reload blue chalk (washes off) for temporary layout lines. Red chalk is harder to remove and stains some surfaces permanently.

### Marking Gauge

A wooden or metal tool with an adjustable fence and a scoring pin. Used for scribing a line parallel to an edge, typically in joinery. More accurate than a pencil because the pin scores the wood fiber rather than drawing alongside a ruler.

## Marking Technique

How you mark is as important as how you measure.

**Use a sharp pencil or marking knife.** A dull pencil point has width — you can be off by 1/32" just from pencil width. For rough work, a pencil is fine. For joinery, use a marking knife and score the line.

**Mark with a V (crow's foot).** Don't draw a straight line across the board — draw a V-shape with the point of the V at the exact measurement. A straight line is ambiguous (do you cut to the left of the line? the right? through it?). The V-point is unambiguous.

**Mark which side to cut.** Make an X on the waste side of the line. When you're cutting multiple pieces, you can forget which side the blade goes on. The X tells you — the blade goes on the X side.

**Mark, don't measure twice.** When cutting multiple pieces to the same length, cut one piece and use it as a template to mark the others. Measuring each piece separately accumulates small errors. Using a story pole (a straight stick with measurements marked directly on it) is even better for repetitive layouts.

## Common Measuring Mistakes

**Reading the wrong graduation:** Half-inch marks look like inch marks if you're not careful. Count carefully in the 1" to 2" zone where both numbers are visible.

**Holding the tape at an angle:** A tape that's angled across a board reads longer than true width. Keep the tape perpendicular to the edge you're measuring from.

**Measuring to the wrong reference:** Know whether you're measuring to the outside face, centerline, or inside face of a board. Write it down if necessary.

**Not accounting for kerf:** A saw blade removes material (the "kerf" — typically 1/8"). If you need two pieces of 24" from a 48" board, they won't both fit because the blade removes 1/8" in the middle. Account for kerf in your layout.

## Metric vs. Imperial

US construction uses imperial (feet, inches, fractions). If you work in any other country or with imported materials, you'll encounter metric. Most tape measures have both scales — imperial on one edge, metric on the other. The conversion: 1 inch = 25.4 mm. A 2x4 stud is 38mm × 89mm in metric (same "nominal vs. actual" situation as in the US).

## Layout for a Wall

To show how this comes together: laying out a stud wall requires measuring and marking stud locations on the top and bottom plates before framing.

1. Snap a chalk line on the subfloor marking the inside face of the wall
2. Cut top and bottom plates to length and lay them side by side
3. Mark the first stud location: 15-1/4" from the end (so the face of the stud falls at 16" when you add 3/4" for the drywall edge)
4. From there, mark stud centers at 16" O.C. (on-center) — mark X where each stud goes
5. Mark doubled studs at window and door openings
6. Keep both plates aligned when marking — a mistake on one that's not on the other means your wall won't go together right
