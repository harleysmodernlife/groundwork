## Joinery: How Wood Comes Together

Joinery is how two pieces of wood connect. The joint you choose determines the strength of the connection, how much work it takes to make, and how it looks. In rough framing, most joints are just two pieces of lumber fastened with nails. In furniture and cabinet work, proper joinery is what makes the difference between something that lasts decades and something that falls apart.

---

## Why Joinery Matters

Wood moves. Humidity changes cause wood to expand and contract across the grain throughout the year. Joints that don't account for this movement will fail — a glued butt joint between end grain and long grain will crack because end grain absorbs moisture differently than long grain.

Good joinery also distributes load across a larger surface area. A dowel joint has glue surface on two circular faces; a mortise and tenon joint has glue surface on four faces and mechanical interlocking.

---

## The Role of Glue and Mechanical Fasteners

**Wood glue (PVA):** When applied to long-grain surfaces of two pieces and clamped until dry, PVA glue creates a bond stronger than the wood itself — the wood will split before a good glue joint fails. But it's only strong on long grain to long grain. End grain is porous and weak for gluing.

**Screws:** Provide mechanical holding power independent of glue. Good for structural connections and for pulling joints tight during assembly. Don't rely on screws into end grain — it pulls out easily.

**Nails:** Shear strength (resistance to side loading) but relatively poor withdrawal resistance. Structural framing uses many nails in combination, which builds up cumulative strength.

**Dowels:** Wood cylinders glued into matching holes in both pieces. Provide alignment and add glue surface. Weak by themselves; strong when combined with glued long-grain surfaces.

## Basic Joints

### Butt Joint

The simplest joint: two pieces meeting face to face or end to face, fastened with screws, nails, or glue.

**Strength:** Low, unless reinforced. End grain glue joints are weak. Face-to-face and edge-to-face are stronger.

**Uses:** Carcass construction (cabinet boxes), rough carpentry connections, anywhere appearance doesn't matter or the joint is reinforced by other means (angle brackets, pocket screws, etc.).

**When it's adequate:** Box construction where multiple panels support each other — a cabinet box with a back panel triangulates the structure so butt joints are adequate.

### Pocket Screw Joint

A screw driven at an angle through a pocket drilled in one piece into the face of the adjoining piece. Pocket screw jigs (Kreg is the most common brand) drill the pocket at the correct angle and provide a step drill bit that makes the hole and counterbore in one pass.

**Strength:** Good shear resistance, moderate withdrawal resistance. Not as strong as mortise-and-tenon but far stronger than a simple butt joint.

**Uses:** Cabinet face frames, table aprons, bed frames, general furniture carcass construction. The industry-standard method for cabinet face frames.

**Advantage:** Fast, requires no clamps to hold alignment (the screw pulls the joint tight), and invisible from the face.

### Dado Joint

A flat-bottomed groove (dado) cut across the grain of one piece, with the other piece fitting into it.

**Uses:** Shelf-to-cabinet-side connections, drawer dividers, bookcase shelves. The shelf sits in the dado and is supported from below — it can't pull free under load.

**Strength:** Good for shear and compression loads. Weak in tension (pulling the shelf out) unless glued.

**Variation — Stopped dado:** The dado doesn't run the full width of the piece — it stops short of the front edge so the groove isn't visible from the front. The fitting piece gets a notch at its front corner to fit the stopped end.

### Rabbet Joint

An L-shaped notch cut along the edge of a board. One piece's rabbet fits over the edge of the other, creating a mechanical step.

**Uses:** Cabinet backs (rabbeted into the cabinet sides), drawer bottoms, frame-and-panel backs, picture frame joining.

**Strength:** Better than a butt joint for cabinet backs because the rabbet provides mechanical support and increases glue surface.

### Half-Lap Joint

Both pieces are cut to half their thickness at the joint location, and the two halves overlap to form a connection that's flush on both faces.

**Types:**
- **End half-lap:** Both pieces at their ends, commonly used for frame corners
- **Cross half-lap:** In the middle of both pieces, for crossing members in a grid
- **T half-lap:** One piece at its end, the other in its middle

**Strength:** Very good — mechanical interlocking and large glue surface. Much stronger than a butt joint for frame construction.

**Uses:** Trestle frames, workbench legs, any situation where two crossing or meeting pieces need to be flush.

### Mortise and Tenon

The traditional joint of furniture and timber framing. A rectangular tenon (projection) cut on the end of one piece fits into a matching mortise (hole) in the other.

**Geometry:**
- Tenon thickness: typically 1/3 the thickness of the piece
- Tenon length: typically 2/3 of the depth of the mortised piece
- Tenon width: can run nearly the full width of the piece with some shoulder on each side

**Strength:** Excellent. One of the strongest wood-to-wood joints, with large glue surface on four faces of the tenon.

**Uses:** Chair legs to seats, table aprons to legs, door rails to stiles, timber framing.

**Cutting:** The tenon is cut on a table saw or with a router. The mortise is cut with a mortising chisel and mallet, a router with a template, or a dedicated mortising machine.

### Dovetail Joint

A joint where trapezoidal "tails" on one piece fit into matching "pins" on the other. The shape of the tails prevents the joint from pulling apart in one direction.

**Uses:** Drawer corners (the primary joint for hand-cut furniture drawers), box corners, carcase joinery.

**Strength:** Exceptional mechanical strength in one direction — specifically the direction that would pull the tail out is precisely the direction a drawer is most stressed.

**Types:**
- **Through dovetail:** Joint visible from both sides — used as a decorative feature
- **Half-blind dovetail:** Tail is hidden from one face — standard drawer front joinery
- **Sliding dovetail:** A dovetail-shaped tongue slides into a matching groove — used for shelves, breadboard ends

**Difficulty:** Hand-cutting dovetails requires practice and precise work. Router jigs simplify the process but typically produce a uniform pin/tail pattern rather than the variable spacing of hand-cut work.

## Box Construction (Cabinet Carcases)

Most cabinet boxes use simple joinery — dado joints for fixed shelves, rabbet joints for backs, and butt joints or pocket screws for corners. The back panel is critical: a glued-and-nailed back holds the box square and prevents racking.

**Assembly sequence:**
1. Cut all parts to size
2. Cut dadoes and rabbets
3. Dry-fit (assemble without glue) to check fit and confirm squareness
4. Apply glue, assemble, and clamp
5. Check diagonals — a square box has equal diagonals. If they differ, the box is out of square; squeeze the longer diagonal corner with clamps until both diagonals match
6. Add the back panel last, check square, nail or screw in place

---

## Frame and Panel Construction

Cabinet doors, large flat panels, and wainscoting often use frame-and-panel construction. The frame (rails and stiles) provides structure. The panel floats in a groove in the frame — it's not glued, only captured. This allows the panel to expand and contract with humidity changes without splitting or distorting the frame.

**Parts:**
- **Stiles:** Vertical frame members
- **Rails:** Horizontal frame members
- **Panel:** The flat center section, captured in a groove (or rabbet) around the frame's inside edge

**Why floating works:** The panel expands across the grain with humidity. If it were glued into the frame, seasonal movement would crack the panel or distort the frame. Floating allows free movement.

**Gap allowance:** Leave 1/8" to 1/4" of clearance in the groove so the panel can move. In very dry climates, a full 1/4" gap is appropriate.

## Fastener Selection for Joinery

**Pocket screws:** Coarse-thread, hardened. Sizes 1" to 2-1/2". Choose length based on material thickness.

**Finish nails:** For trim and casing. 15g or 16g for heavier trim, 18g or 23g (pin nails) for fine trim where hole size matters.

**Structural screws (construction screws):** For structural connections. 3" and 3-1/4" for framing. Self-tapping types drive without pilot holes in many situations.

**Lag screws:** Heavy-duty bolts for structural connections — fence posts, deck ledgers, beam-to-post connections. Pre-drill a clearance hole in the first member and a pilot hole in the second.
