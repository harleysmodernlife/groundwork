## Cutting Techniques and Saw Selection

Cutting wood is one of those things that looks straightforward until you've made a bad cut — the piece that's 1/4" short, the miter that gaps, the plywood sheet with a tear-out edge. Getting clean, accurate cuts requires choosing the right saw, setting it up correctly, and using consistent technique. This lesson covers the major cutting operations and when to use each tool.

---

## Types of Cuts

**Crosscut:** Cutting across the grain, shortening a board's length. The most common cut in carpentry.

**Rip cut:** Cutting with the grain, narrowing a board's width. Requires different technique and blade selection than crosscutting.

**Miter cut:** A crosscut at an angle other than 90° (looking down from above). Used for picture frames, trim, and angled layouts.

**Bevel cut:** A cut at an angle through the thickness of the material (tilted blade). Used for chamfers, angled edges, and compound cuts.

**Compound miter:** Both a miter angle and a bevel angle simultaneously. Required for crown molding and some roof work.

**Dado:** A flat-bottomed groove cut partway through the thickness of a board (not through completely). Used for shelf supports in cabinets, drawer dividers, and joinery.

**Rabbet:** An L-shaped notch cut along the edge of a board. Used for cabinet backs, drawer bottoms, and frame-and-panel construction.

## Saw Selection

### For Crosscuts

**Miter saw:** The best tool for crosscutting boards and dimensional lumber. Accurate, fast, and consistent. The fence and angle settings make repeatable cuts easy. Limitation: can only crosscut material as wide as the blade reach (typically 12-16" on a sliding model).

**Circular saw with a guide:** For cutting large sheet goods (plywood, OSB, MDF) where a miter saw is too small. Use a factory-straight edge or a track system as a guide. Without a guide, circular saw cuts wander.

**Handsaw:** For one-off cuts where setting up a power saw isn't worth the time, or where a power saw won't fit. Slower but always available.

### For Rip Cuts

**Table saw:** The primary tool for ripping. The rip fence ensures a straight, parallel cut over the full length of the board. Safe and accurate when used correctly with push sticks.

**Circular saw with a rip guide:** A fence attachment that mounts to the saw's base plate, running along the board edge. Works well for rip cuts in plywood but is harder to control on long boards.

**Track saw (guide rail system):** A circular saw that rides on an aluminum track, providing table-saw-quality rip cuts in sheet goods without needing to move the sheet. Used by professional installers who work without a shop setup.

### For Curves and Irregular Shapes

**Jigsaw:** The go-to for curved cuts. Accepts different blade types for wood, metal, and plastic. Limitation: blade tends to flex on tight curves and thick material, leading to non-perpendicular cuts.

**Band saw:** A continuous loop blade on a stationary machine. Better than a jigsaw for curves in thick material — the blade is under tension and doesn't flex. Essential for resaw operations (slicing a board into thinner pieces through the width).

**Coping saw (hand):** For tight curves in thin material, particularly trim coping work.

## Getting Accurate Crosscuts with a Miter Saw

**Square your saw.** A miter saw can drift out of square over time. Check it: cut a piece in two, flip one piece, and hold the cut faces together. Any gap visible along the length means the saw isn't cutting 90°. Adjust the detent (the preset stop) according to the manual.

**Use a stop block for repetitive cuts.** Clamp a block to the fence at the exact measurement from the blade. Every piece cut to that stop is identical — much faster and more accurate than measuring and marking each piece.

**Support long pieces.** Material that's not supported on both sides of the cut can fall and twist the blade mid-cut. Use roller stands or a helper.

**Let the blade stop before raising it.** After the cut, hold the handle down briefly until the blade completely stops spinning. Raising a spinning blade can catch the kerf and throw the piece.

---

## Getting Accurate Cuts with a Circular Saw

**Mark the cut line clearly.** A pencil line you can see against the shadow of the blade.

**Understand the blade position.** The blade doesn't line up with the edge of the base plate — it's offset. Your saw has a notch or line in the front of the base plate that shows where the blade kerf will be. Use this to align the blade to your mark before turning on the saw.

**Use a guide for straight cuts.** Clamp a factory-straight edge or dedicated saw guide to the material, positioned so the base plate rides along it and the blade cuts on your line. Without a guide, circular saw cuts on sheet goods are rarely straight.

**Support sheet goods flat.** Cutting a 4x8 sheet of plywood in the air is dangerous — the waste side can sag and pinch the blade. Lay the sheet on two sawhorses with rigid foam insulation under it. Cut right through the foam — the sheet stays flat throughout the cut.

## Ripping on a Table Saw

**Set the fence parallel to the blade.** Use a reliable method to check: measure from the blade tooth at the front to the fence, then from the tooth at the back of the blade to the fence. Both measurements should be identical. A fence that angles toward the back causes kickback.

**Blade height:** Set the blade just above the material — 1/8" of blade exposed above the top surface. Minimum exposure reduces kickback risk and is safer if something goes wrong.

**Use a push stick.** Any time your right hand would be within 6" of the blade at the end of the cut, use a push stick to complete the cut. This is non-negotiable. Keep a push stick on the saw table at all times.

**Never reach past the blade.** To remove a cut piece from behind the blade, turn the saw off, wait for the blade to stop, then retrieve the piece.

**Featherboards:** Spring-loaded guides that press the workpiece against the fence and down onto the table, keeping consistent pressure throughout the cut. They significantly improve cut quality and safety on long rip cuts.

## Cutting Plywood

Plywood is tricky because the face veneer tears when sawn with the wrong blade or in the wrong direction.

**Blade selection:** Use a fine-tooth blade (60-80 teeth on a 10" blade) for clean cuts in plywood veneers. A framing blade (24 teeth) will tear the veneer badly.

**Cut with the good face down on a table saw** (the blade cuts up through the material, so tearout appears on the top face — keep the finished face on the bottom). On a circular saw, **cut with the good face up** (the blade cuts down through the material, so tearout appears on the bottom face).

**Score the cut line first** with a marking knife for critical cuts. Scoring severs the veneer fibers cleanly before the blade gets there.

**Solid backer:** Clamping a scrap piece of wood exactly along the cut line and cutting through both gives the fibers something to support against tearout.

## Dados and Rabbets

Dados (grooves) and rabbets (edge notches) are usually cut with a router or on a table saw.

### Router

**Straight bit or spiral upcut bit.** Clamp a guide fence to the workpiece at the right offset from the cut. Make multiple passes to reach the full depth — don't try to cut to full depth in one pass. The router bit will overheat and the cut quality will be poor.

**Down-spiral vs. up-spiral:** Down-spiral (compression) bits push fibers down, keeping the top face clean. Up-spiral bits pull chips out (good for deep grooves) but can tear the top surface.

### Table Saw

**Standard blade dado:** Make multiple passes with a regular blade, moving the fence slightly between passes. Time-consuming.

**Dado stack:** A set of stacked blades that together cut a flat-bottomed groove in one pass. You add or remove chippers between the outer blades to set the exact width. More setup but faster and cleaner.

## Measuring for Cuts: The "Story Stick" Method

When you need many pieces cut to different lengths, measuring each piece introduces cumulative error. A better method:

**Mark the story stick.** Take a long straight scrap of wood (the "story stick") and mark the actual length of each piece directly on it — pull from the installed piece, or from the actual opening you're fitting to. Don't convert to numbers.

**Transfer from the story stick to the material.** Hold the story stick against the material and transfer the mark directly. You bypass all numerical measurement, and small measurement errors disappear.

This method is standard practice for experienced trim carpenters, cabinet installers, and anyone fitting pieces to irregular spaces.
