## Rough Framing Basics

Framing is the skeleton of a building. Everything else — insulation, drywall, cabinets, windows, doors — attaches to the frame. A well-built frame is square, level, and plumb. A poorly built frame creates problems for every trade that follows. If the walls aren't plumb, the cabinets won't fit right. If the floor isn't flat, the tile will crack. Getting framing right matters.

---

## Lumber for Framing

Standard framing uses dimensional softwood lumber — typically SPF (Spruce-Pine-Fir) or Douglas Fir in the West, Southern Yellow Pine in the Southeast.

**Common framing sizes:**
- 2x4: Wall studs, non-load-bearing partitions, rafters in lighter applications
- 2x6: Exterior walls (provides more insulation depth), load-bearing walls where 2x4 isn't adequate
- 2x8, 2x10, 2x12: Floor joists, roof rafters, headers over large openings
- 4x4, 4x6: Posts, columns
- 6x6 and larger: Heavy timber posts and beams

**Grading:** Use No. 2 or better for structural framing. No. 3 is acceptable for non-structural applications. "Stud grade" is a specific grade optimized for wall studs — consistent and straight.

**Moisture content:** Kiln-dried (KD) lumber at 19% MC or less. Green lumber shrinks as it dries, causing nail pops, squeaky floors, and settling. KD lumber is more stable.

## Framing Spacing: 16" O.C. vs 24" O.C.

"On center" (O.C.) refers to the distance between the centers of adjacent framing members.

**16" O.C.:** Standard for most walls and floors. At 16" spacing, three studs fit in a 4-foot section, and 4x8 sheet goods (drywall, plywood) are always supported at an edge on a stud.

**24" O.C.:** Used in some systems (advanced framing / optimum value engineering) to reduce material use and increase insulation space. Requires 3/4" drywall or specific sheathing to span 24" without sagging.

## Platform Framing: How Modern Houses Are Built

Most residential construction in the US uses platform framing. Each floor is framed, decked with plywood, and then the next story's walls are framed on top of that deck. The "platform" is the floor deck, which the workers stand on to frame the walls.

**Balloon framing** (older construction, mostly pre-1940s) used continuous studs from foundation to roof. This creates a fire pathway from basement to attic inside the wall cavity — modern code requires fire blocking.

## Anatomy of a Framed Wall

**Sole plate (bottom plate):** The horizontal 2x lumber at the bottom of the wall, on the floor. The studs sit on it. In exterior walls, the sole plate is pressure-treated where it contacts concrete.

**Top plate:** The horizontal lumber at the top of the wall. Standard framing uses a double top plate — two 2x's stacked. The double top plate allows adjacent walls to lap over each other and tie together.

**Studs:** Vertical members between the plates. Standard stud height for 8-foot ceilings is 92-5/8" (this gives a wall height that's slightly less than 8 feet after plates are added, accounting for finish materials).

**King stud:** A full-length stud that runs continuously from sole plate to top plate on each side of a window or door opening.

**Jack stud (trimmer stud):** A shorter stud that supports the header over a window or door opening. It runs from the sole plate to the underside of the header, inside the king stud.

**Header:** The structural member spanning a window or door opening, carrying the load from above. Header size depends on the span and the load above it. Typical residential headers use two pieces of dimensional lumber with 1/2" plywood between them (to equal the 3.5" width of the wall), or engineered lumber for larger spans.

**Cripple studs:** Short studs above a header (between the header and top plate) and below a rough sill (between the rough sill and the sole plate).

**Rough sill:** The horizontal member at the bottom of a window opening.

## Laying Out a Wall

Layout happens before you cut a single stud. It's done on the plates — top and bottom plate laid side-by-side on the floor, edges aligned.

**Stud layout (16" O.C.):**
1. Mark the end stud position (flush with the end of the plates)
2. From the end, mark 15-1/4" — this is where the EDGE of the first interior stud sits (so the CENTER is at 16")
3. From there, mark every 16" — these are also edge marks, and you mark the stud with an X on the correct side of each mark
4. Mark openings: king studs, jack studs, headers, cripples

**The 16" layout reason:** Drywall and plywood sheets are 48" wide. 16" × 3 = 48". Three studs cover exactly one sheet width, with stud edges at the sheet edge. If the first stud edge hits at 15-1/4" (16" to center), then the drywall sheet edge lands on the stud face rather than in the middle of a stud.

## Building a Stud Wall

**Step 1: Cut the plates to length.** Both top and bottom plates are the same length.

**Step 2: Lay out the plates** (as above) with the marks corresponding exactly on both plates.

**Step 3: Cut the studs.** For a standard 8-foot ceiling, precut studs are 92-5/8". Cut all studs at once using a stop block on the miter saw for consistency.

**Step 4: Assemble on the floor.** Lay the top plate and bottom plate parallel, with the stud marks facing up. Set each stud between them at its mark location. The studs should sit perpendicular to the plates.

**Step 5: Nail off.** Drive two 16d nails (or three for critical connections) through the plate face into the end of each stud. One person on each end is efficient — one nails the top plate while the other nails the bottom. A framing nailer dramatically speeds this up.

**Step 6: Frame the openings.** Install king studs, jack studs, headers, and cripples as marked.

**Step 7: Raise the wall.** Tilt the wall up — this requires at least two people for any wall over 8 feet long. Brace it plumb with diagonal 2x4 braces nailed to the wall and to stakes driven into the floor.

**Step 8: Plumb and level.** Check the wall is plumb in two directions (along its face, and in/out). Adjust the braces. Then nail the sole plate to the floor (concrete nails or powder-actuated fasteners for concrete; 16d nails for wood subfloor). Lap the double top plate over the adjacent walls and nail.

## Floor Framing

Floor framing spans between the foundation walls (or beams), supporting the floor load.

**Rim joist (band joist):** Runs around the perimeter of the floor, tying the tops of the joists together and providing a nailing surface.

**Floor joists:** Horizontal members spaced at 16" O.C. that span between beams or foundation walls. Size depends on span — a 2x8 can span about 12-13 feet; a 2x10 can span about 15-16 feet (depending on species and grade).

**Crown up:** Every piece of dimensional lumber has a slight curve (crown) along its length. Always install joists with the crown facing up. Under load, the floor deck pushes the crown down, and the joist naturally wants to straighten. A joist installed crown-down will sag worse under load.

**Blocking:** Installed perpendicular between joists at mid-span (for longer spans) and at supports to prevent joists from rotating under load. Also installed at load transfer points.

**Engineered floor joists (TJI):** I-beam shaped members with LVL flanges and OSB web. Span longer with less material, are dimensionally consistent, and won't shrink or crown. Standard in most new construction for floor framing.

## Headers Over Openings

Load-bearing walls carry weight from above. Any opening in a load-bearing wall needs a header to transfer that weight to the jack studs and down to the foundation.

**Header sizing (rough guide, varies by load and lumber species):**
- Up to 4' opening: 2x6 header
- 4-6' opening: 2x8 header
- 6-8' opening: 2x10 header
- 8-10' opening: 2x12 header
- Over 10': Engineered lumber (LVL, glulam)

Headers in exterior walls with 2x4 framing are made from two pieces of lumber with 1/2" OSB or plywood sandwiched between to equal the 3.5" wall width. In 2x6 walls, you can use a single 5-1/4" LVL.

**Non-load-bearing partitions** don't need structural headers — a flat 2x4 header is adequate over openings up to about 36".

## Checking for Square and Plumb

**Square:** Equal diagonals. Measure corner to corner both ways. When they match, the frame is square.

**Level:** Horizontal surfaces are level — check with a 4-foot level in multiple locations.

**Plumb:** Vertical surfaces are plumb — check with a level held against the stud or wall face. Check in both directions (face-on and in-profile — a wall can be plumb on its face but leaning forward or back).

**Flat:** For floors and subfloors, check for flatness with a long straightedge. A floor that's not flat creates problems for flooring installation. Sand high spots, shim low spots before installing finish flooring.
