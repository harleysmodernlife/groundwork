## Power Tools: Safe Use and Applications

Power tools multiply what you can accomplish in a day. They also multiply the consequences of carelessness. A handsaw that slips hurts you. A table saw that catches you can take fingers or a hand. The tools in this lesson are worth learning deeply — the correct technique, the safety rules, and why each rule exists.

---

## Before Using Any Power Tool

**Read the manual.** Not the whole thing — but the safety section and the setup section for whatever operation you're about to do. Every tool has specific hazards.

**Inspect the tool.** Check that guards are in place, the blade or bit is sharp and undamaged, and the power cord or battery isn't damaged.

**Personal protective equipment:**
- **Safety glasses:** Non-negotiable. A chip at 150 mph takes an eye. Wear glasses rated for impact.
- **Hearing protection:** Power tools run 95-110 dB. Sustained exposure at those levels causes permanent hearing loss. Foam earplugs or earmuffs — use them every time.
- **Dust mask:** Cutting wood generates fine dust, particularly in enclosed spaces. An N95 minimum for general sawdust. Some woods (walnut, cedar, exotic species) are sensitizing — a respirator is worth it.

**Secure the workpiece.** A piece of wood that isn't clamped or held can move, kick, or trap the blade. Never hold small pieces freehand under a saw.

## Circular Saw

The most common power saw on a job site. A blade spins at 4,500-5,500 RPM and cuts through material as you push the saw forward.

### Setup

- **Blade depth:** Adjust so the blade extends about 1/4" below the material. More exposure isn't faster — it's more dangerous. The blade should barely clear the bottom of the material.
- **Blade selection:** Framing blades (24 teeth) cut fast and rough. Finish blades (40-60+ teeth) cut slowly but cleanly. Match the blade to the work.
- **The guard:** The lower blade guard springs back as the blade enters the material and covers the blade as you finish the cut. Never remove or wire it open. If it's not retracting properly, fix it before using the saw.

### Technique

Support the workpiece on both sides of the cut — a sawhorse on each side. As you cut, the waste side needs support too, or it'll sag and pinch the blade (kickback). Alternatively, support the sheet on foam insulation — you can cut right through the foam and the sheet stays flat.

Guide the saw with a straightedge or speed square for accurate cuts. The saw's base plate rides along the guide; the blade follows.

### Kickback

Kickback happens when the blade pinches — the spinning blade grabs the wood and the saw launches backward toward you at full speed. Causes:
- Waste side sagging onto the blade
- Cutting into green (wet) wood that springs back
- Forcing the saw through a dull blade

Prevention: support the workpiece correctly, use sharp blades, don't force the saw. If you feel the saw dragging, stop — find out why.

## Miter Saw (Chop Saw)

A blade mounted on an arm that pivots down onto the material. Makes precise crosscuts (cutting across the board's width) and angled cuts.

**Single-bevel miter saw:** Tilts left or right for angled (miter) cuts in one plane.

**Compound miter saw:** Tilts both along the miter angle AND the bevel angle. Needed for crown molding and complex trim cuts.

**Sliding compound miter saw:** The arm slides forward, allowing cuts across wider material.

### Uses

- Cutting framing lumber to length
- Cutting trim at precise angles (baseboards, door casing, crown)
- Repetitive cuts at a fixed length (set a stop block, cut many pieces to identical length)

### Safety

Keep both hands away from the blade's path — the entire arc the blade sweeps through, not just where it comes down. Let the blade reach full speed before lowering into material. Let the blade fully stop before raising it after the cut. Hold the material firmly against the fence.

## Table Saw

The most powerful and most dangerous shop tool. A blade protrudes through a table surface, and you push material through the spinning blade. Used for ripping (cutting a board along its length to a narrower width).

**The rip fence:** A guide that runs parallel to the blade. Set it to the desired width, lock it, push the board through with the fence as a guide.

**The blade guard:** Covers the blade. Some operations require removing it — when you do, be extra careful. Replace it immediately after.

**The riving knife (or splitter):** A piece of steel directly behind the blade that holds the kerf open as the wood passes. Prevents the cut wood from pinching on the back of the blade — pinching causes kickback. Never remove it.

**Push sticks:** Narrow strips of wood or plastic used to push material past the blade when your hands would get within 6" of the blade. Keep a few on hand and use them.

**Kickback with a table saw is severe.** The blade can throw material at 80-100 mph directly at the operator. Don't stand directly behind the blade line — stand slightly to the side. Never reach over or behind the spinning blade.

### Setup

- Blade height: raise the blade so it's about 1/8" above the material. Just enough to clear — more exposure means more blade in the air, which is more dangerous.
- Parallel fence: the rip fence must be exactly parallel to the blade. A fence that's slightly angled causes the wood to bind and kick back.

## Drill and Impact Driver

### Drill/Driver

Drills holes and drives screws. Variable speed, adjustable clutch that stops driving at a set torque (prevents overdriving screws and stripping holes).

**Choosing bits:**
- **Twist bits:** For metal and wood. The most common.
- **Brad-point bits:** For clean holes in wood — the center point prevents the bit from wandering.
- **Spade bits:** For large holes (3/4" to 1-1/2") in wood. Fast but rough.
- **Hole saw:** For large holes (1" to 6") — for locksets, plumbing, duct penetrations.
- **Forstner bit:** Clean, flat-bottomed holes. For hardware installation and joinery.

### Impact Driver

Rotates with an internal hammering mechanism that pulses torque. Drives long screws through framing lumber without stripping the head or bogging down. Not great for precision hole drilling — use a drill for that. An impact driver and a drill are often used together, one set up for drilling pilot holes and the other for driving.

## Jigsaw

A small blade that moves up and down (reciprocating), allowing curved and irregular cuts. Use it for:
- Cutting curves in plywood
- Cutouts in countertops (for sinks)
- Notches and irregular shapes that a circular saw can't follow

**Blade selection:** Fine-tooth blades (10-14 TPI) for clean cuts in plywood and solid wood. Coarser blades for rough cuts. Metal-cutting blades for thin steel.

**Technique:** Let the saw do the cutting — don't push too hard or the blade will flex and the cut won't be perpendicular. Score the layout line with a knife first for cleaner cut edges.

## Random Orbit Sander

A sanding disc that orbits (moves in a circular path) while also spinning. The dual motion prevents the sanding scratches from all running in one direction, giving a smoother result than a belt sander or straight-line sander.

**Grits:**
- **60-80 grit:** Aggressive material removal, scratches to remove, rough surfaces
- **100-120 grit:** General smoothing
- **150-180 grit:** Finish sanding before paint
- **220 grit:** Final sanding, between finish coats

**Always sand with the grain on the final pass with fine grit.** The random-orbit scratches are usually invisible, but a final hand-sand with the grain gives the best result before staining.

---

## Router

A high-speed spinning bit (18,000-25,000 RPM) that cuts profiles, grooves, and shapes in wood.

**Uses:**
- Decorative edges (roundover, chamfer, cove, ogee profiles)
- Dadoes and rabbets (grooves for shelf supports, cabinet backs)
- Template routing (following a pattern to cut duplicate shapes)
- Mortises (with a template and spiral upcut bit)

**Feed direction matters.** When routing around the edge of a board, feed the router so the bit rotation is climbing against the wood (climb-cut), not spinning away from it. Going the wrong direction causes the router to dart forward uncontrollably.

**Sharp bits:** Router bits get dull with use. A dull bit burns the wood and requires excessive force. Sharp bits make clean cuts with minimal effort.

## Cordless Tools: Battery Systems

Most professional carpentry is done with cordless tools (no cords to manage on site). Pick one battery platform and stick with it — batteries and chargers aren't interchangeable between brands.

Common platforms: Milwaukee M12/M18, DeWalt 20V MAX/FLEXVOLT, Makita LXT/XGT, Bosch 18V.

**Voltage is a rough guide to power.** An 18V/20V tool is a full-size professional tool. 12V tools are compact but lower power. Both are useful — heavy drilling and sawing need 18V; driving screws in tight spaces is fine with 12V.

**Runtime vs. capacity:** Higher amp-hour (Ah) batteries run longer on a charge but weigh more. A 5.0Ah battery runs about twice as long as a 2.5Ah, but weighs more. Have at least two batteries per tool so one charges while you work.
