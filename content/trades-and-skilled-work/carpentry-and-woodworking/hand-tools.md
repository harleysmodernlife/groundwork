## Hand Tools Every Carpenter Uses

Power tools are for speed. Hand tools are for control, precision, and the work that power tools can't do. A carpenter who doesn't know how to use hand tools effectively is always fighting their own work — they can't get into tight spots, can't refine a fit, and can't work quietly when the situation demands it. These are the tools you'll reach for constantly.

---

## Saws

### Handsaw

The basic cross-cut handsaw cuts wood across the grain. The teeth are set (bent alternately left and right) to cut a kerf slightly wider than the blade, preventing binding. Choose a handsaw based on teeth per inch (TPI):

- **7-8 TPI:** Faster cut, rougher finish — for framing lumber
- **10-12 TPI:** Slower but cleaner — for trim and general woodworking

**Using a handsaw:** Start the cut with a few light pull strokes to establish the kerf, then switch to full strokes. Don't push the saw — it just buckles. The weight of the saw does the cutting; your arm provides direction.

### Japanese Pull Saw

Cuts on the pull stroke (opposite of a Western handsaw). The teeth can be much thinner because the blade is under tension during the cut, not compression. Results in a finer, cleaner kerf with less effort. Most woodworkers who try a pull saw prefer it for fine work.

Common styles:
- **Ryoba:** Two sets of teeth on opposite edges — cross-cut teeth on one side, rip teeth on the other
- **Dozuki:** Smaller saw with a stiffened back, for precision joinery (tenons, dovetails)

### Coping Saw

A thin blade held in a C-frame, used for curved cuts in wood and for "coping" trim joints (cutting the profile of one piece of trim to fit against another). The blade can be rotated in the frame to change the cut direction.

## Squares and Layout Tools

### Framing Square (Steel Square)

An L-shaped steel square with one 24" leg and one 16" leg. Used for:
- Checking large corners for square
- Laying out roof rafters (the square has rafter tables stamped on it)
- Stair layout

### Speed Square

A triangular square with a lip that hooks over a board's edge. The most-used layout tool for framing — marks 90° lines across boards in seconds and guides a circular saw for square cuts. Can also mark common angles up to 45°.

### Combination Square

A ruler that slides in an adjustable head with 90° and 45° faces. More versatile than a speed square:
- Mark lines parallel to an edge at a set distance
- Check squareness and 45° angles
- Use the ruler's end as a depth gauge

### Level

Essential for ensuring things are truly horizontal (level) or truly vertical (plumb). The bubble in the vial must be centered between the lines.

**48" level:** Standard for checking walls, door frames, cabinets. Longer levels give more accurate readings because small errors in the floor or wall surface average out.

**Torpedo level:** 9-12" long, fits in tight spots. Use it for small work — setting a junction box, checking a short piece of framing.

**A level is only as accurate as it is calibrated.** Check yours: hold it against a wall and note the bubble position. Rotate the level 180° and hold it in the same spot. If the bubble reads the same, it's accurate. If not, it needs adjustment or replacement.

## Chisels

A steel blade with a beveled edge, driven by hand or with a mallet.

**Common uses:**
- Chopping mortises (square holes for tenon joints)
- Paring the flat surface of a joint (shaving thin slices to achieve a perfect fit)
- Chopping hinge mortises in door frames
- Cleaning corners that a saw can't reach

**Chisel sizes:** Typically 1/4", 1/2", 3/4", and 1" cover most carpentry needs.

**Keep them sharp.** A sharp chisel cuts with light pressure and gives you control. A dull chisel requires force, which makes it unpredictable — it can slip and take off more material than you intended, or slip into your hand. Sharpen on a whetstone or diamond plate, keeping the bevel angle at 25-30°. A leather strop maintains the edge between sharpening sessions.

**Use a wooden mallet with chisels, not a steel hammer.** Steel hammers mushroom the handle end over time and can crack the handle.

## Planes

A plane is a chisel held at a fixed angle in a body, used to shave thin layers from a wood surface. Hand planes were the primary surface-flattening and thicknessing tools before power equipment.

### Block Plane

Small (6-7" long), one-handed, with the blade at a lower angle. Used to:
- Trim the end grain of boards
- Shave a door edge that's sticking
- Clean up saw cuts
- Chamfer (bevel) an edge

### No. 4 Bench Plane

The standard workbench plane, 9-10" long. Used to flatten and smooth boards, remove high spots, and prepare surfaces for finishing.

**Plane adjustment:** The blade depth (how much material is removed per pass) is adjusted by a knob on the back. Start with minimal blade extension and increase until you get a thin shaving. Too much blade = tearout and jamming.

## Hammers

### Framing Hammer

Heavier (20-32 oz), longer handle, often with a waffle face that grips nail heads and prevents the hammer from skipping. Used for driving 16d and larger nails in structural framing. The extra weight lets you drive big nails in fewer swings.

### Finish Hammer

Lighter (16 oz typical), smooth face, curved claw. Used for trim, cabinet work, and anywhere you need precision — the smooth face is less likely to leave marks in finish materials.

**Technique:** Choke up on the handle for starting the nail, then slide to the end for driving power. Your wrist should snap through the swing — don't muscle it with your arm.

## Clamps

You cannot clamp too many times when something needs to be square and tight before driving fasteners or while glue dries. Common types:

**F-clamp (bar clamp):** Sliding jaw on a steel bar, tightened with a screw. Available in many sizes. General-purpose clamping.

**Spring clamp:** Quick one-handed operation, moderate pressure. Good for holding pieces while you drive fasteners.

**Pipe clamp:** Clamp fixtures that attach to pipe — the pipe determines the length. Strong clamping force for wide assemblies like tabletops or door frames.

**Band clamp (strap clamp):** A nylon strap that wraps around irregular shapes (like a picture frame or a chair). Applies even pressure around the entire perimeter.

**Parallel clamp:** High-end cabinet clamp with jaws that stay parallel under load. Excellent for gluing up flat panels.

## Utility Knife and Marking Knife

**Utility knife:** The most-reached-for cutting tool on any job site. Scoring drywall, cutting felt paper, trimming caulk, opening boxes. Keep fresh blades in it — a dull blade requires pressure and is dangerous.

**Marking knife:** A thin, sharp blade used to score layout lines in wood joinery. More precise than a pencil because the knife severs the wood fibers exactly at the line, which also prevents tearout when you chisel to the line.

---

## Nail Set

A small steel punch with a dimpled point, used to drive a nail head slightly below the wood surface without leaving a hammer mark. You then fill the dimple with wood filler or putty and paint over it — the nail becomes invisible.

Sizes correspond to nail head sizes. Have a set of three (1/32", 2/32", 3/32") to cover common finish nails.

---

## Screwdrivers

Most screw driving is done with a drill/driver, but you still need manual screwdrivers for:
- Working in tight spaces a drill won't fit
- Delicate adjustments (like hinge screws) where you might strip the head with a drill
- Setting screws that need specific torque

Keep a set with both Phillips and flathead in multiple sizes. A good screwdriver fits the fastener exactly — an undersized tip damages the head and slips.

## Maintaining Your Tools

**Clean after use.** Wipe wood shavings and sawdust off planes and chisels. Rust is the enemy of precision tools — a rust pit in a chisel's flat back can't be removed without serious work.

**Oil moving parts.** A drop of light machine oil on the adjustment mechanisms of planes and combination squares keeps them working smoothly.

**Sharp is safe.** More hand tool injuries happen from dull tools than from sharp ones. A dull tool requires force to work, and force makes it unpredictable. Sharp tools cut efficiently and go where you aim them.

**Protect the edges.** Don't store chisels or plane blades loose in a drawer where they'll bang against each other and nick the edges. Use blade guards, tool rolls, or a dedicated chisel rack.
