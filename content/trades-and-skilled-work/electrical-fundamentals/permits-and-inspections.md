## Permits and Inspections

Most electrical work beyond simple device replacement requires a permit. Getting one isn't just bureaucracy — it gets your work inspected by someone with authority to catch mistakes before they become fires or electrocutions. Understanding the process takes the mystery out of it.

---

## What Requires a Permit

Requirements vary by jurisdiction, but in general, permits are required for:

- Adding new circuits
- Upgrading an electrical panel
- Installing new outlets, switches, or light fixtures (beyond simple replacement)
- Any work on 240V circuits
- Adding subpanels
- Outdoor electrical installations
- Electrical work on remodels and additions
- Installing EV chargers (in most jurisdictions)

Generally **not** requiring permits:
- Replacing like-for-like: an outlet for an outlet, a switch for a switch
- Replacing light fixtures with similar fixtures
- Replacing a circuit breaker with the same size and type

When in doubt, call your local building department. They'll tell you what requires a permit in your jurisdiction.

---

## Why You Should Pull a Permit

**Safety:** An inspector checks that the work is done correctly. A second set of eyes catches mistakes before they cause fires or shocks.

**Insurance:** If a fire starts in unpermitted electrical work, your insurance company may deny the claim. "The work was never inspected" is a strong argument for the insurer.

**Liability:** If you sell the home and unpermitted work is discovered during inspection, you must either correct it or disclose it. Buyers may walk away or demand a price reduction.

**Your protection:** If you hire a contractor who does unpermitted work and something goes wrong, you have less recourse. A contractor who pulls permits is accountable to the inspection process.

Some people do unpermitted electrical work their entire lives without incident. The risk is real and concentrated — the fire happens when the wiring fails, not when the outlet is installed. Many house fires are traced to unpermitted or uninspected electrical work.

---

## How to Get a Permit

The process varies by jurisdiction, but typically:

1. **Contact your local building department.** In most cities and counties, this is done online, in person, or by phone. Search for "[your city/county] building permit" or "building department."

2. **Describe the work.** You'll need to describe what you're doing, where, and roughly how. For most residential electrical, you don't need formal engineering drawings — a simple description and a sketch is sufficient for common work.

3. **Pay the fee.** Electrical permit fees range from $25-50 for small jobs to a few hundred for panel upgrades or significant new circuits.

4. **Receive the permit.** Sometimes issued immediately; sometimes requires review. Post it where visible at the job site (some jurisdictions require this; most just want you to have it available).

---

## Homeowner Permits

In most jurisdictions, a licensed electrician can pull a permit. But homeowners can also pull their own permits for work on their own residence. "Owner-builder" provisions typically allow you to do your own electrical work on your own home (where you live — not a rental property) and pull the permit yourself.

This varies significantly by jurisdiction. Some require all electrical work to be done by licensed contractors. Some allow only limited homeowner work. Verify before starting.

## The Inspection Process

Once you've done the work, you request an inspection. The building department schedules an inspector to come to the site.

**Rough inspection (before closing walls):** If you're running new wire through walls, the inspector looks at:
- Wire size appropriate for the circuit
- Breaker size matching wire
- Proper cable stapling and protection
- Correct junction box installation
- Nail plates protecting wires near framing edges
- Cable entering boxes secured properly

**Final inspection (after everything is completed):** The inspector checks:
- Devices properly installed and secured
- Covers on all boxes
- GFCI and AFCI protection where required
- Circuit breakers properly labeled
- Panel cover installed
- Everything functioning

---

## What Happens If You Fail Inspection

The inspector writes up the deficiencies. You correct them. You call for re-inspection. This is normal — many projects require corrections.

Common inspection failures:
- Wrong wire gauge for circuit
- Missing GFCI where required
- Wires not properly stapled to framing
- Junction boxes not accessible (buried in a wall)
- Missing cable clamps in boxes
- Improper panel labeling

Inspectors generally want the work to pass. They're not looking for reasons to fail you — they're checking safety. If you're not sure about something, ask the inspector or call the building department before the inspection.

---

## Finding Your Local Building Department

- Search "[your city or county] building department permits"
- Many jurisdictions now offer online permit applications and inspection scheduling
- The building department is separate from the health department, planning department, etc. — it specifically handles construction permits

For most residential electrical work, the permit process is straightforward. The inspection is typically brief (30-60 minutes for most residential work) and, if the work is done correctly, passes without issue.

---

## Professional Electricians and Permits

When you hire a licensed electrician, they should pull the permit for any work that requires one. If a contractor offers to do the work "without a permit to save you money," this is a red flag. The permit protects you — not the contractor.

Licensed contractors in most states are required to pull permits for code-required work. A contractor who routinely skips permits is likely cutting other corners too.
