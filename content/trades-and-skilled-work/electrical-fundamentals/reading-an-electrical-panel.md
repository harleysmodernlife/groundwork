## Reading a Service Panel and Circuit Breakers

The service panel (also called the breaker box, load center, or electrical panel) is the hub of your home's electrical system. Understanding what's in it and how it works lets you troubleshoot problems, plan circuits, and work safely.

---

## What the Panel Does

The utility provides power to your home. That power enters the panel, where it's distributed to individual circuits. The panel:

- Receives power from the utility (via the service entrance)
- Contains the main breaker that controls all power to the home
- Distributes power to individual circuit breakers
- Protects circuits from overcurrent (too much current)

The panel doesn't generate electricity — it's a distribution and protection device.

---

## What's Inside

**Main breaker:** The large double-pole breaker at the top (usually 100A, 150A, or 200A). Turning this off cuts power to all circuits in the panel. The service entrance wires above it are still live — always.

**Bus bars:** Two metal rails running down the center (or both sides) of the panel. These are the hot legs — 120V each, 240V between them. Circuit breakers clip onto these.

**Neutral bar:** A grounded bus bar connected to white (neutral) wires and bare copper (ground) wires. In a main panel, neutral and ground are bonded here.

**Circuit breakers:** Individual switches that protect each circuit. Each breaker is rated for a maximum current (15A, 20A, 30A, 50A, etc.).

---

## Reading Voltage: Single-Phase Residential Service

US residential service is single-phase, 240V split into two legs:
- **Leg A:** 120V to neutral
- **Leg B:** 120V to neutral
- **Between A and B:** 240V

Standard outlets use one leg (120V). Large appliances use both legs (240V).

Breakers on alternating positions in the panel alternate between Leg A and Leg B. This is why your panel is balanced — loads distributed evenly.

---

## Circuit Breakers

**Single-pole breakers:** One slot, one hot wire, 120V circuits. Rated 15A (for 14-gauge wire) or 20A (for 12-gauge wire) for most residential circuits.

**Double-pole breakers:** Two slots, two hot wires, 240V. Used for dryers (30A), ranges (50A), air conditioners, water heaters.

**Tandem breakers:** Two circuit breakers in one slot (a "slim" or "piggyback" design). Used to add circuits when the panel is full. Only use in panels that explicitly allow them — not all panels can accept tandems.

---

## Ampacity: Panel Capacity

Your panel's rating (100A, 150A, 200A) is the maximum current the main breaker allows before tripping. This limits the total load you can put on the home.

**Adding up circuit breakers:** Don't add up all your breaker ratings — that will exceed the main breaker rating, which is normal. In practice, not all circuits are on simultaneously and never at full load. The panel rating is a safety limit, not a total-usage specification.

**A 200A panel is standard for modern homes.** Older homes may have 100A service, which can be insufficient for electric vehicles, large AC systems, or whole-home electrification.

---

## What the Panel Label Tells You

Open the door. There should be a schedule (label) listing each breaker and what it controls.

Common issues with panel labels:
- Labels are missing or never filled in
- Labels are wrong (the previous owner labeled incorrectly)
- Circuits are mislabeled after renovations

**Never trust the label without verification.** Use a non-contact voltage tester and plug a lamp or radio into outlets to identify which breaker controls each circuit. Create an accurate map.

---

## Identifying Tripped Breakers

A tripped breaker moves to a middle position — not fully on, not fully off. Some panels have a red indicator window that shows when a breaker has tripped.

To reset a tripped breaker:
1. Push it fully to the OFF position first (you must go fully off before it will reset)
2. Then push it back to ON

If it immediately trips again, there's still a fault — an overloaded circuit, a short, or a failing breaker.

**A breaker that trips repeatedly is telling you something is wrong.** Don't just keep resetting it without investigating the cause.

---

## Overloaded Circuits vs. Short Circuits vs. Ground Faults

**Overloaded circuit:** Too many devices drawing too much current. Breaker heats up and trips. Fix: reduce the load, or add a new circuit.

**Short circuit:** Hot wire and neutral (or hot wire and ground) contact directly with near-zero resistance. Massive current spike, breaker trips immediately. Usually caused by damaged wiring or a failed device.

**Ground fault:** Hot wire contacts the ground path or a grounded surface. GFCI protection catches this at low current levels; the main breaker catches it at higher levels.

---

## What the Panel Can Tell You About Your Wiring Age

**Older panels to watch for:**

**Federal Pacific Electric (FPE) Stab-Lok:** Manufactured until the 1980s. These breakers have documented reliability problems — they sometimes fail to trip on overloads, creating fire risk. If you have a Federal Pacific panel, get it evaluated by an electrician.

**Pushmatic/Bulldog:** Also older panels; the main breaker is a push-button style. These are often 60A or 100A and may be undersized for modern loads.

**Split-bus panels:** No single main breaker; have a group of large breakers that together serve as the "main." Legal when installed but now considered dated.

**Aluminum wiring panels:** Aluminum wiring was used in the 1960s-70s. The wiring itself and the connections require special attention — aluminum behaves differently than copper and can create fire hazards if not properly managed.

---

## Safety: What You Can Touch, What You Can't

**Safe with the main breaker off:**
- Individual circuit breakers and their wiring
- Neutral and ground bars

**Never safe, even with the main breaker off:**
- The service entrance conductors coming in from the utility (the wires feeding the main breaker)
- The main breaker's line-side lugs

The only person who can de-energize those wires is the utility company, which pulls the meter. Never work on service entrance conductors.
