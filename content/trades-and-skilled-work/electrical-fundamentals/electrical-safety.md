## Electrical Safety: The Non-Negotiables

Electricity kills. It does so quickly, silently, and without warning. A mistake that would break a bone in another trade can stop your heart in electrical work. These rules are not suggestions — they are the difference between going home and not going home.

---

## How Electricity Kills

**Electrocution** occurs when current passes through the body. The amount of current matters:
- 1 mA (milliamp): You feel a tingle
- 10 mA: Painful; muscle control begins to fail
- 15-20 mA: Can't let go — muscles contract involuntarily ("freeze" to the conductor)
- 50-100 mA: Ventricular fibrillation — your heart stops coordinating. This is the lethal range.
- 200+ mA: Severe burns, death from cardiac and respiratory arrest

**Most household circuits can deliver 15-20 amps = 15,000-20,000 mA. This is hundreds of times the lethal amount.**

The path of current through the body matters too. Hand-to-hand (across the chest, through the heart) is most dangerous. The 60 Hz AC frequency is particularly good at disrupting the heart's rhythm.

**Arc flash** is the explosive release of energy when electricity jumps through the air between conductors. It produces blinding light, intense heat (up to 35,000°F — four times the surface of the sun), a pressure wave, and molten metal. Arc flash kills and maims. Even at lower energy levels, it causes severe burns.

---

## Rule 1: Assume Everything Is Live

Never assume a circuit is de-energized because you think you turned it off. Verify before touching.

The three steps of de-energization:
1. **Turn off** — flip the breaker or disconnect the power source
2. **Lock out / Tag out** — if working in a setting where someone might restore power, lock the breaker in the off position with a padlock and tag it. This is OSHA-required in commercial/industrial settings.
3. **Verify** — use a non-contact voltage tester to confirm the circuit is dead before touching anything

A non-contact voltage tester (NCV tester) is a pen-shaped device that beeps and lights up near live conductors without touching them. They cost $15-30 and are mandatory before any electrical work.

---

## Rule 2: One-Hand Rule

When possible, keep one hand in your pocket or behind your back while working near live circuits. This limits the current path — electricity can only pass through the arm touching the live part, not hand-to-hand across your chest and through your heart.

---

## Rule 3: PPE Every Time

**Appropriate PPE for the task:**

- **Insulated gloves:** Rubber insulating gloves rated for the voltage you're working near. Class 00 (500V) for residential work. These go over thin cotton gloves (liner gloves) that absorb sweat.
- **Safety glasses:** Arc flash produces intense light and flying debris.
- **Arc flash face shield and rated clothing:** Required for work near energized equipment that could arc. NFPA 70E specifies what's required for each hazard category.
- **Insulated tools:** Tools with insulated handles rated for 1000V+. These have orange handles or markings. Use them near live circuits.
- **Rubber-soled shoes:** Not slippers or sneakers — boots rated for electrical work.

For basic residential work at 120/240V with the circuit de-energized: NCV tester verification + insulated tools + safety glasses.

---

## Rule 4: Never Work Alone Near Live Electricity

If something goes wrong, you need someone to call 911, perform CPR, or use a defibrillator. A person unconscious from electrocution is not obvious from a distance — the only sound may be the circuit breaker tripping.

---

## Ground Fault Circuit Interrupters (GFCI)

A GFCI monitors the current going out on the hot wire and coming back on the neutral wire. If there's a difference — meaning current is taking an unexpected path (like through a person) — the GFCI trips within milliseconds, at a threshold of about 5 milliamps.

This is why GFCI outlets are required in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, outdoor locations, and near water. They save lives.

**A GFCI does not protect against all shocks.** It trips too slowly to prevent pain and muscle lock. It does often trip fast enough to prevent death.

## Common Causes of Electrical Accidents

**Working on live circuits.** The most common cause. De-energize, lock out, verify before touching.

**Assuming the panel label is accurate.** Panel labels are often wrong. A breaker labeled "Bedroom 1" might also power something in the hallway. Verify with your tester.

**Touching both hot and neutral simultaneously.** Even if you're not grounded, completing the circuit this way causes current to flow through you.

**Working in wet conditions.** Water dramatically lowers skin resistance. Never work on electrical systems in wet conditions or with wet hands.

**Damaged insulation.** Frayed, cracked, or gnawed wiring insulation is a direct path to the conductor.

**Capacitors in electronics.** Even when unplugged, large capacitors (in TVs, microwave ovens, power supplies) can hold lethal charge for minutes or hours. Don't open electronics unless you know how to safely discharge them.

---

## What to Do If Someone Is Electrocuted

**Do not touch them directly.** You will also be electrocuted.

1. Disconnect the power source immediately — flip the breaker, pull the plug, or push them away with a non-conducting object (wooden board, rubber mat, plastic object)
2. Call 911
3. Begin CPR if they're unresponsive and not breathing
4. Use an AED (defibrillator) if available — ventricular fibrillation is treatable with defibrillation

Burns from electrical injury may be less severe externally than internally — electricity follows current paths through the body, burning tissues along the way. Even apparent survivors may have internal injuries. Always seek medical evaluation after any significant electrical shock.

---

## The Summary

- De-energize, lock out, verify — every time
- Test before touching, not before
- Use appropriate PPE
- Keep one hand back when possible near live circuits
- Never work alone near energized high-voltage systems
- Respect capacitors in electronics

Electrical safety is not about paranoia. Professionals who understand electricity work efficiently and calmly. They are also the ones who go home every night because they haven't skipped the steps.
