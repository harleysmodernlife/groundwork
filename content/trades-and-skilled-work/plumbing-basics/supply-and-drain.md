## Supply Lines and Drain Systems

A home's plumbing consists of two completely separate systems that never mix: supply and drain. Water comes in through supply lines under pressure; waste water leaves through drain lines by gravity. Understanding how each works separately makes troubleshooting and repair logical instead of mysterious.

---

## The Supply System

Supply water enters your home from a single source — municipal water main or a private well. It arrives under pressure, typically 40-80 PSI (pounds per square inch), which is what pushes it to every faucet and appliance in the building.

**Path of water through a house:**

1. **Water main / service line:** Underground pipe from the street (or well) to the house. In cold climates, buried below frost depth.
2. **Main shutoff valve:** The first thing the water hits when it enters the home. This valve controls all water to the house. Know where it is — you'll need it when a pipe bursts.
3. **Water meter:** Tracks usage. Located near the main shutoff.
4. **Cold water trunk line:** Runs through the home, branching to each fixture.
5. **Water heater:** Cold water feeds the water heater, which then feeds a parallel hot water trunk line.
6. **Individual fixture supply lines:** Short flexible supply lines connecting trunk lines to each faucet, toilet, or appliance.

**Pressure keeps everything moving.** Supply pipes don't need to slope — water goes uphill because it's under pressure.

---

## Hot Water Distribution

Cold water at the main branches into two paths:

- One branch feeds fixtures that only use cold water (toilets, outdoor hose bibs)
- One branch feeds the water heater, which distributes hot water to fixtures that need both

**Tank water heaters** store 30-80 gallons of hot water. The tank maintains water temperature continuously, so hot water is available immediately (until the tank is depleted).

**Tankless (on-demand) water heaters** heat water only as it flows through. Endless hot water but a few seconds of delay while the unit fires up. More energy efficient since there's no standby heat loss.

**Hot water delivery lag:** The time between turning on a hot faucet and hot water arriving is the time it takes to push cold water sitting in the hot water pipes out of the way. This is why hot water arrives faster in rooms close to the water heater. Recirculation systems keep hot water circulating continuously, eliminating the wait.

---

## Shutoffs: Know Where They Are

Every responsible homeowner knows the location of:

**Main house shutoff:** Typically near where the service line enters the house — in the basement, crawlspace, utility room, or in a valve box near the foundation. In warm climates, sometimes at the meter. Turning this off stops all water to the house.

**Individual fixture shutoffs:** Small valves on the supply lines under sinks, behind toilets, and often behind washing machines and dishwashers. Turn them clockwise to close. These let you service a single fixture without shutting off the whole house.

**If there's no fixture shutoff** and the fixture is failing, you'll need the main shutoff.

## The Drain, Waste, and Vent System (DWV)

Drain lines carry waste water away by gravity. Unlike supply lines, drain lines must slope continuously toward the drain, typically 1/4 inch per foot of horizontal run.

**The DWV system has three components:**

**Drains (waste pipes):** Carry liquid and solid waste. Size matters: kitchen drains are typically 1.5-2 inch; toilet drains are 3-4 inch. All drains slope toward the main stack.

**Vents:** This is what most people don't know about. Every drain needs venting — a pipe connected to the drain line that runs up through the roof, open to outside air. Without venting, the rushing water in a drain creates negative pressure that sucks water out of the P-traps below other fixtures, letting sewer gas into the house.

**P-traps:** The U-shaped pipe under every sink and built into every toilet. It holds a small amount of water that creates a seal against sewer gas. The water in a P-trap is the only thing between your bathroom and the sewer. This is also why you shouldn't leave sinks unused for weeks — the water evaporates, the seal breaks, and you smell sewer gas.

---

## The Main Stack

The main stack is the central vertical drain pipe — typically 3-4 inch diameter — that runs from the basement to above the roof. All drain lines connect to it. At the bottom, it connects to the house's sewer line that exits to the municipal sewer or septic tank. At the top, it's open to the atmosphere as a vent.

When you flush a toilet, waste enters the main stack and falls straight down. When you hear that "whoosh" in the pipes when someone flushes, you're hearing the change in pressure through the system.

---

## Gravity Is the Design Constraint

Every decision in drain system design is about maintaining downward slope for gravity flow:

- **1/4 inch per foot slope** is typical. Too shallow and waste doesn't move cleanly. Too steep and liquid races away leaving solids behind.
- **Horizontal runs should be short.** Long horizontal runs risk clogs because gravity does less work.
- **No "low spots" or sags** in horizontal drains — water pools, solids settle, clogs develop.
- **Clean-outs:** Access points in the drain lines for clearing clogs. Typically at the base of the stack, at direction changes, and where the drain exits the house.

---

## Water Pressure in Supply vs. Gravity in Drain

This difference explains everything about why the two systems look different:

- Supply pipes can run any direction (they're pressurized)
- Supply pipes can be smaller (pressure pushes water efficiently)
- Drain pipes must slope consistently (gravity only)
- Drain pipes are larger (they flow partially full by design, unlike supply pipes which run completely full)
- Every fixture needs both a supply connection AND a drain connection AND a vent connection
