## Pipe Materials: Copper, PVC, PEX, and More

Walk into any home and you might find four different pipe materials installed in different eras and for different purposes. Each has specific strengths, weaknesses, and appropriate applications. Knowing what you're looking at and why it was used makes repairs and upgrades logical.

---

## Copper

The gold standard for supply piping for most of the 20th century. You'll find copper in the majority of homes built between 1950 and 2000.

**Properties:**
- Extremely durable — copper pipes from the 1950s often still function fine
- Resists corrosion from water
- Handles temperature extremes well (hot water, freezing)
- Naturally antimicrobial (resists bacterial growth)
- Relatively easy to work with once you learn soldering

**Types:**
- **Type M (thin wall):** For interior residential supply lines. The most common residential copper.
- **Type L (medium wall):** Thicker walls, more durable. Used underground and in some commercial applications.
- **Type K (heavy wall):** Thickest. Used underground and for service lines.

**Joining:** Copper is traditionally joined by soldering (sweating) — heating the joint with a torch, applying solder flux, and flowing solder into the joint. Press-fit (push-connect) fittings are now common for easier repairs without a torch.

**Limitations:** Expensive — copper prices have risen significantly. Labor-intensive for large installations (every joint must be soldered). Susceptible to pinhole leaks in areas with aggressive water chemistry (low pH, high chloramines). Freezing can burst copper pipes.

---

## CPVC (Chlorinated Polyvinyl Chloride)

A plastic pipe rated for both hot and cold supply water. More common in the Southeast US, in mobile homes, and in some older construction.

**Properties:** Cheap, lightweight, handles hot water (rated to 200°F). Joined with solvent cement (no flame needed).

**Limitations:** Brittle — becomes more so with age. CPVC is prone to cracking when stressed or when pipes shift. Not approved in all jurisdictions for all applications. Cannot be used underground without protection.

**Color:** Off-white or cream.

---

## PVC (Polyvinyl Chloride)

PVC is for drain, waste, and vent (DWV) lines — not for supply lines (it's not rated for water pressure). The most common drain material in modern residential construction.

**Properties:** Lightweight, inexpensive, easy to cut and assemble. Solvent welded (glued). Handles drain temperatures fine, though not hot enough for steam.

**Sizes:** 1.5" for sink drains, 2" for common drain lines, 3" for toilets and main stacks, 4" for sewer lines.

**Do not use for supply lines under pressure.** PVC isn't rated for supply pressure. CPVC is the supply-line version.

**Color:** White or gray (sewer-grade).

---

## ABS (Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene)

Another drain pipe plastic, similar to PVC. More common in the western US and Canada. Slightly more flexible than PVC.

**Color:** Black. Easy to distinguish from PVC.

**Joining:** ABS cement (different from PVC cement — not interchangeable).

**Transitioning:** You can connect ABS to PVC with a special transition cement and/or a rubber coupling — but connecting them wrong (using the wrong cement, or without a proper transition fitting) creates a poor joint.

---

## PEX (Cross-Linked Polyethylene)

The dominant supply pipe material in new residential construction. PEX has largely replaced copper for supply lines because it's faster to install, cheaper, and more durable in many respects.

**Properties:**
- Flexible — can bend around corners without fittings
- Won't burst when frozen (it expands and then contracts)
- Resistant to scale and corrosion
- Easy to run through walls and around obstacles
- Color-coded: red for hot, blue for cold

**Joining:** Three main methods:
- **PEX-A (expansion):** Most flexible, best joints. Requires an expansion tool and rings. Stronger than the pipe itself when properly made.
- **PEX-B (crimp):** Most common. Crimp ring squeezed over the fitting with a crimp tool. Requires separate tools per pipe size.
- **Push-to-connect (SharkBite-style):** No tools — push the pipe in and it locks. Easy but more expensive per fitting. Good for repairs and tight spaces.

**Limitations:** Cannot be used outdoors in direct sunlight (UV degrades it). Cannot be solvent welded. Fittings slightly restrict internal diameter (less than copper). Some early formulations were associated with MTBE contamination concerns (resolved in modern PEX).

---

## Galvanized Steel

Used in homes built before the 1950s-60s. Steel pipe coated in zinc to resist corrosion.

**The problem:** Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside over decades. The zinc lining wears away, rust builds up on the inner walls, and eventually:
- Flow rate drops dramatically (rust narrows the pipe)
- The pipe develops pinhole leaks
- Water quality suffers (rusty water)

If you see galvanized pipe in an older home, assume it's near the end of its life. Re-piping in copper or PEX is a significant project but often necessary.

**Identification:** Dull gray-silver metal pipe, threads at connections.

---

## Cast Iron

Used for drain lines in older construction (pre-1970s). Extremely heavy, durable, and quiet. Still used in some commercial applications where noise transmission is a concern.

**In older homes:** Cast iron drains in good condition can last indefinitely. But cast iron corrodes and can crack. If you have cast iron drain lines, inspect them with a camera before assuming they're fine.

**Transitioning:** Rubber "mission couplings" (no-hub couplings with stainless clamps) connect modern PVC drain lines to cast iron cleanly, without threading or special tools.

---

## Choosing the Right Material

| Application | Recommended |
|---|---|
| Interior supply lines (hot and cold) | PEX or copper |
| Drain lines | PVC |
| Underground supply (interior) | Copper type L or PEX with proper protection |
| Underground sewer line | PVC (SDR35 rating) |
| Repairs to existing copper | Copper or push-to-connect fittings |
| Repairs to existing galvanized | Transition to copper or PEX |

When repairing existing systems, match the material if possible, or use appropriate transition fittings.
