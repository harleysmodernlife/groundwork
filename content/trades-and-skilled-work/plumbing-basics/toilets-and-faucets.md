## Toilets and Faucets: Most Common Repairs

Toilets and faucets account for the vast majority of plumbing repair calls. Almost every common problem with these fixtures is fixable by a homeowner with basic tools and a hardware store trip. Learning these repairs saves hundreds of dollars per year.

---

## Toilet Anatomy

Every toilet has two main sections: the tank (the porcelain box on top) and the bowl (the part you sit on). The tank stores water for the flush; the bowl receives waste and connects to the drain.

**Inside the tank:**
- **Fill valve (ballcock):** Refills the tank after a flush. Connected to the cold water supply line.
- **Flapper:** A rubber seal at the bottom of the tank. Opens during a flush to let water from tank to bowl, then closes to allow refilling.
- **Overflow tube:** A standpipe that prevents overfilling — if water gets too high, it drains directly to the bowl rather than onto the floor.
- **Float:** Controls when the fill valve shuts off. Cup floats are integrated into the fill valve; ball floats are on an arm. Adjusting the float adjusts the water level.
- **Flush handle and chain:** Lifting the handle pulls the chain, which lifts the flapper.

## Common Toilet Problems

### Toilet Runs Constantly

Running water sound when you haven't flushed = water continuously flowing from tank to bowl. Two causes:

**Flapper not sealing:**
1. Lift the lid off the tank
2. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, wait 10 minutes without flushing
3. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking
4. Check for mineral deposits around the flapper seat and clean them
5. Check chain length — too long and it gets caught under the flapper; adjust with a different chain link
6. Replace the flapper if it's still leaking (universal flappers fit most toilets, $5-10, install in 5 minutes)

**Water level too high (overflowing into the overflow tube):**
1. Look inside the tank — if water is at or above the overflow tube, the water level is set too high
2. Adjust the float down: on cup-style floats integrated with the fill valve, there's an adjustment screw or pinch clip on the float arm; on ball float types, bend the arm downward or adjust the screw
3. Target water level: 1/2 to 1 inch below the top of the overflow tube

If neither of the above is the issue, the fill valve itself is failing and should be replaced.

### Toilet Won't Flush Properly (Weak Flush)

- Water level in tank too low — raise the float
- Clog in the trap — use a flange plunger (the kind with the extended rubber lip that creates a seal in the toilet drain)
- Scale buildup in the jet holes under the toilet rim — clean with a small mirror to find them and a stiff brush or wire to clear buildup

### Toilet Won't Stop Running After Flushing

If the tank fills but water keeps running: the fill valve is likely worn. Replace the entire fill valve assembly ($10-15, 30 minutes, instructions on box).

### Toilet Rocks

The wax ring under the toilet has failed or the toilet is loose from the floor bolts. A rocking toilet should be fixed immediately — water seeps past the wax ring into the subfloor, causing rot.

1. Turn off supply, flush to empty tank
2. Disconnect supply line
3. Remove the floor bolts and lift the toilet off (they're heavy — 50+ lbs)
4. Scrape off the old wax ring
5. Install a new wax ring (align the toilet horn over the ring) and set the toilet down
6. Replace the floor bolts, reconnect supply

### Toilet is Slow to Drain / Gurgles / Bubbles

This usually indicates a partial clog in the drain line or a vent pipe obstruction. Try a plunger first. If the gurgling continues without obvious backup, the vent stack may be blocked (check the roof opening) or there's a partial clog in the main drain line.

## Faucet Anatomy

Faucets come in four main types, each repaired differently:

**Ball faucets:** A single handle rotates over a ball with holes for hot/cold mix. Very common kitchen faucets.

**Cartridge faucets:** A cartridge controls flow and temperature. Common in Moen and other brands. Single or dual handle.

**Ceramic disc faucets:** A cylinder with ceramic discs. Smooth rotation, very durable, common in modern fixtures.

**Compression faucets:** The oldest type. Two-handle, turning the handle compresses a rubber washer against a seat. Common in homes built before 1980.

---

## Common Faucet Problems

### Dripping from the spout (when off)

The internal mechanism isn't fully seating. The fix depends on faucet type:

**Ball faucet:** Buy a repair kit for your brand (Moen, Delta, etc.). Replace the ball, springs, seats, and O-rings.

**Cartridge faucet:** Remove the handle (screw under the decorative cap), pull the cartridge (usually held by a clip), take it to the hardware store and match it, install new one. Moen cartridges are widely available.

**Ceramic disc:** Remove the cylinder, clean both discs with vinegar, replace if chipped.

**Compression:** Remove handle, unscrew the packing nut, pull out the stem, replace the rubber washer at the bottom. The seat the washer presses against may be worn — resurface with a seat grinder or replace the seat.

### Leaking at the base of the faucet

O-rings on the body of the faucet are worn. For most faucets, the faucet must be disassembled from below (shut off supply, disconnect supply lines, remove the mounting nut under the sink, lift out the faucet) to access and replace the O-rings. Often easier to replace the entire faucet.

### Leaking at the handle

Packing nut or O-ring under the handle. Tighten the packing nut first. If it still drips, replace the O-ring.

### Low flow from one faucet

Clean the aerator. Unscrew the aerator from the faucet tip (turn counterclockwise — sometimes needs pliers with cloth padding). The screen is clogged with mineral deposits. Soak in vinegar, scrub clean, reinstall. This solves low flow in 80% of cases.

## General Faucet Replacement

When a faucet is too old or damaged to repair economically, replacement is often the same effort as repair.

1. Shut off supply lines (the valves under the sink)
2. Disconnect the supply lines from the faucet (basin wrench helps for tight spaces)
3. Remove the mounting nuts under the sink
4. Lift out old faucet
5. Clean the sink surface
6. Install new faucet (follow the included instructions — they're usually clear)
7. Reconnect supply lines, turn on water, check for leaks

Most kitchen faucet replacements take 1-2 hours. Bathroom faucet replacements, 45 minutes to an hour.
