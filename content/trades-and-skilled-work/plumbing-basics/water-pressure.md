## Water Pressure and How to Measure It

Water pressure is what makes your plumbing work. Too low and faucets trickle; showers are miserable; appliances fail. Too high and pipes vibrate, fixtures wear out prematurely, water hammer bangs through the walls, and eventually something fails. Knowing how to measure and interpret pressure is a basic plumbing skill.

---

## What Water Pressure Is

Water pressure is the force per unit area that pushes water through the supply system. Measured in PSI (pounds per square inch) in the US, or kPa (kilopascals) in metric countries.

Pressure comes from two sources:
- **Municipal water supply:** The utility maintains pressure in the distribution mains. Water towers (elevated tanks) use gravity to create consistent pressure.
- **Private wells:** A pump pressurizes a storage tank to a set range (typically 40-60 PSI or 30-50 PSI).

---

## What Pressure Should Be

**Normal range:** 40-80 PSI is the acceptable range for residential service. Most appliances and fixtures are designed for this range.

**Ideal:** 60-70 PSI gives good flow without stressing fixtures.

**Too low (below 40 PSI):** Weak flow from faucets and showers, appliances may not function properly (dishwashers, washing machines need adequate pressure to fill quickly), toilets refill slowly.

**Too high (above 80 PSI):** Accelerated wear on washers, valves, and seals. Water hammer. Risk of pipe joint failure. Chronic dripping faucets (the pressure forces water past washers). Some jurisdictions require a pressure reducing valve if supply pressure exceeds 80 PSI.

**Code requirement:** Most plumbing codes cap supply pressure at 80 PSI at the point of use.

---

## How to Measure Pressure

A **water pressure gauge** screws directly onto a hose bib (outdoor faucet) or a threaded faucet adapter. Costs $10-15 at any hardware store.

**To measure:**
1. Attach the gauge to an outdoor hose bib
2. Turn on the hose bib fully
3. Read the gauge — this is your static pressure (no water flowing elsewhere)
4. Have someone run water inside and read the gauge — this is dynamic pressure (with flow)

Static pressure is what you'll see quoted. Dynamic pressure (under flow conditions) will be somewhat lower.

---

## Pressure Reducing Valves (PRVs)

If municipal supply pressure is too high (or variable), a pressure reducing valve is installed on the main supply line where it enters the house. It has a spring-loaded diaphragm that maintains downstream pressure at a set level regardless of upstream fluctuations.

**Signs your PRV may be failing:**
- Pressure suddenly too high after being normal
- Banging pipes (water hammer)
- Dripping faucets or running toilets that weren't problems before

**Adjusting a PRV:** There's typically an adjustment screw under a locknut. Turning clockwise increases downstream pressure; counterclockwise decreases it. Make small adjustments and measure with a gauge.

**Replacing a PRV:** A plumber's job in most cases — requires cutting the main supply line.

---

## Flow Rate vs. Pressure

These are related but distinct:

**Pressure (PSI):** The force pushing water. Does not diminish significantly through a properly sized pipe over short distances.

**Flow rate (gallons per minute, GPM):** How much water moves through the pipe per unit time. Affected by both pressure and pipe size.

**The relationship:** Higher pressure → higher flow rate (for a given pipe size and restriction). Larger pipe → higher flow rate (for a given pressure). A small pipe acts as a restriction that limits flow rate even at high pressure.

This is why low flow from a fixture might not be a pressure problem — the supply line to that fixture might be undersized, clogged, or kinked.

---

## Water Hammer

Water hammer is the banging sound (and physical shock) when water flow is suddenly stopped — by a valve closing quickly, a solenoid shutting off, or a washing machine fill valve snapping shut.

The momentum of moving water creates a pressure spike when it stops suddenly. This spike can be 10x the normal operating pressure, repeated hundreds of times per year.

**Effects:** Banging pipes, loosened pipe joints, damaged valves, failed appliance connections.

**Solution: Water hammer arrestors.** Devices with a piston and air cushion that absorb the pressure spike. Install near solenoid-operated valves (washing machine, dishwasher, ice maker). Available at hardware stores for $10-20.

Older homes had air chambers (capped vertical pipes) near fixtures that served the same purpose — but air chambers gradually fill with water over time.

---

## Pressure at Height

Pressure decreases with elevation — 0.43 PSI per foot of elevation. If your street supply is at 70 PSI and you have a three-story home:
- First floor: ~70 PSI
- Second floor (10 feet up): ~66 PSI
- Third floor (20 feet up): ~61 PSI

This is usually not significant in residential settings. But in tall commercial buildings, booster pumps are required on upper floors.

## Low Pressure Troubleshooting

If pressure is suddenly low at one fixture:
- Check the fixture's shutoff valve (may be partially closed)
- Check the aerator or showerhead for mineral buildup — clean or replace
- Check supply line for kinks

If pressure is low throughout the house:
- Check if the main shutoff is fully open
- Check the PRV setting and condition
- Call the utility to ask if there's a main pressure issue in your area
- Check the water meter shutoff (there's typically a shutoff at the meter too)

If pressure is low only when multiple fixtures are on simultaneously (fine alone, weak together), the main supply line may be undersized or partially blocked. This is a plumber's diagnosis.
