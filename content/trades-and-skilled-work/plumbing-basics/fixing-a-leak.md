## Finding and Fixing Leaks

A leak found early costs a small repair. A leak ignored costs mold remediation, structural repairs, and potentially thousands of dollars. Learning to find and fix leaks is one of the highest-value home repair skills.

---

## Finding Leaks You Can't See

Many leaks are hidden. Signs of a hidden leak:
- Unexplained increase in water bill
- Sound of running water when everything is off
- Soft or discolored spots on walls, ceilings, or floors
- Mold or mildew smell
- Staining around fixtures, under sinks, or along baseboards

**The meter test:** Find your water meter. Turn off all water in the house (no faucets, appliances, or running toilets). Read the meter. Wait 30 minutes. Read it again. If the numbers changed, you have a leak somewhere.

**Dye tablets for toilets:** Toilet flappers that don't seal completely are one of the most common sources of water waste. Drop a dye tablet (or a few drops of food coloring) in the tank. Wait 15 minutes. If color appears in the bowl without flushing, the flapper is leaking.

**Checking under sinks:** Wipe the supply line fittings and the drain pipes with a dry paper towel. Then check for moisture. This reveals slow drips that evaporate before becoming obvious.

## Common Leak Locations

### Under the sink
Most common: the supply line connections at the shutoff valve or faucet, the P-trap, or the drain connection to the wall.

**Supply line drip:** Usually a loose compression fitting or a failing supply line. Tighten the nut 1/4 turn (don't overtighten — you'll crack plastic fittings). If it still drips, replace the supply line — they're a few dollars and screw on.

**P-trap drip:** Tighten the slip nuts. If they won't tighten without leaking, replace the P-trap assembly ($5-15 at hardware store, 15 minutes).

### Faucet drip
A dripping faucet (from the spout when off) wastes thousands of gallons per year. Usually caused by a worn washer, cartridge, or O-ring.

**Ball faucets (common kitchen faucets):** Replace the ball, springs, seats, and O-rings. Repair kits specific to the faucet brand cost $10-20.

**Cartridge faucets:** Remove and replace the cartridge. Single-handle cartridges usually pull straight out after removing a clip. Note orientation before removal. Brand-specific cartridges are widely available at hardware stores.

**Ceramic disc faucets (high-end, usually bathroom):** Remove and clean the ceramic disc. If it's cracked, replace it.

**Compression faucets (old two-handle types):** The stem has a rubber washer that presses against a seat. Replace the washer. The seat itself can wear — a seat grinder tool can resurface it or it can be replaced.

### Toilet tank to bowl
If water runs continuously in the toilet:
- **Flapper not seating:** The rubber flapper at the bottom of the tank doesn't seal. Check for mineral deposits around the seat, adjust the chain (if too long it can get caught), or replace the flapper. A universal flapper costs $5-10 and installs in minutes.
- **Float set too high:** If the float (ball or cup-style) is set too high, water continuously flows into the overflow tube. Adjust the float downward until the water level is 1/2-1 inch below the overflow tube top.
- **Fill valve failing:** If none of the above, the fill valve is likely faulty. Replace it ($10-15, complete instructions on the packaging).

### Pipe joints
Copper pipe leaks typically occur at solder joints that have developed a pinhole or at compression fittings. Push-to-connect fittings (SharkBite) can repair copper, PEX, or CPVC quickly without soldering — cut out the bad section and push-connect around it.

## When to Turn Off Water First

Before opening any supply connection (supply line, shut-off valve, faucet cartridge), turn off the water supply. For fixture repairs: the fixture shutoff under the sink or behind the toilet. If there is no shutoff, use the main shutoff.

**Always have a bucket and towels ready.** Even after turning off the shutoff, pipes have water in them that will drain out.

---

## Fixing Copper Pipe Leaks

**Pinhole in copper:** Turn off water, dry the pipe, and use either:
- **Push-to-connect repair coupling:** Cut out the damaged section and use a SharkBite or similar coupling. No soldering. Fast and reliable.
- **Soldering:** Clean the pipe with emery cloth, apply flux, heat with torch, flow solder. More durable but requires skill and equipment.

**At a threaded connection:** Old threads dry out over time. Clean old thread sealant off, apply new pipe thread sealant (paste) or Teflon tape, reconnect. Hand tight, then 1-2 turns with a wrench.

---

## Fixing PEX Pipe Leaks

PEX rarely leaks except at connections. Push-to-connect fittings work on PEX. For crimp-ring connections, cut out the old fitting and crimp on a new one.

---

## Fixing PVC Drain Leaks

At a slip connection (rubber gasket seal): tighten the slip nut. If cracked, replace the section.

At a glued PVC joint: you cannot unglue PVC. You must cut out the joint and splice in new pipe with couplings.

---

## Temporary Fixes

Plumber's repair clamps can stop a pipe leak temporarily while you wait for parts. These are not permanent solutions but buy time. Epoxy putty (Plumber's Putty or similar) can also stop slow leaks temporarily on metal pipes.

**Never use duct tape or silicone caulk as a pipe repair.** These do not hold under water pressure and will fail.

---

## When to Call a Plumber

- Leaks in the main supply line
- Leaks under the slab or behind finished walls requiring demolition
- Sewer line leaks
- Any leak you can't locate the source of after reasonable investigation
- Any repair involving soldering you're not equipped or trained to do

A plumber for a simple repair runs $100-300 for most call-out situations. Catching a leak early and getting it fixed by a professional is far cheaper than the water damage from letting it go.
