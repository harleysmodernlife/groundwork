## Water Heaters: Maintenance and Basics

Your water heater is one of the largest energy consumers in your home and one of the appliances most worth understanding. Basic maintenance extends its life by years. Knowing the warning signs prevents a catastrophic failure that floods your home.

---

## Types of Water Heaters

### Tank Water Heaters (Conventional Storage)

The most common type in US homes. A large insulated tank (30-80 gallons) keeps water hot continuously, ready when needed.

**How they work:** Cold water enters at the bottom. A gas burner below the tank (gas models) or electric heating elements inside the tank (electric models) heat the water. A thermostat maintains the set temperature. Hot water exits from the top when a faucet opens, and cold water enters to replace it.

**Fuel sources:**
- **Natural gas:** Most efficient gas model. Burns cleaner, typically cheaper to operate than electric.
- **Propane:** For homes without natural gas service.
- **Electric:** Standard resistance heating elements. Slower recovery but simple, no combustion.
- **Heat pump water heater (hybrid electric):** Uses a heat pump to move heat from surrounding air into the water. 2-4x more efficient than standard electric. Best option for most electric homes.

**Lifespan:** 8-12 years on average. Signs of end of life: rust in hot water, leaking from the tank body, frequent pilot outages, recovery taking much longer than normal.

### Tankless (On-Demand) Water Heaters

Heats water instantly as it flows through the unit. No storage tank, no standby heat loss.

**Advantages:** Never run out of hot water (capacity limited by flow rate, not volume), smaller physical size, longer lifespan (20+ years), more energy efficient.

**Disadvantages:** Higher upfront cost. Gas models require a larger gas line (they fire at very high BTUs for short periods). Can't simultaneously supply high-demand fixtures without proper sizing.

**Sizing:** Tankless units are rated by flow rate in gallons per minute (GPM). Calculate your peak simultaneous demand (shower + faucet + dishwasher) and choose a unit that can deliver it at your incoming water temperature.

## Temperature Settings

**Standard recommended setting: 120°F (49°C)**

- 120°F is hot enough for most uses
- Prevents scalding (scalding occurs at 130°F+ with brief exposure)
- Reduces energy consumption vs. higher settings

**Legionella bacteria concern:** Legionella (the bacteria that causes Legionnaire's disease) can survive and grow at water temperatures between 77-113°F. If you have an at-risk household (compromised immunity, elderly residents) or if the heater will sit unused for extended periods, setting to 140°F and installing a tempering valve (mixing valve) at fixtures can be appropriate. Check with your local health guidelines.

**Finding the thermostat:**
- Electric: Turn off the breaker, remove the access panels, adjust the element thermostats (usually two — upper and lower)
- Gas: A dial on the front of the gas valve (usually marked Vacation/Low/Medium/Hot — these correspond roughly to 80/90/110/120°F). Set to the lowest "Hot" position for 120°F.

## Annual Maintenance

### Flush the Tank (Annually)

Sediment — minerals that precipitate out of water — accumulates at the bottom of the tank. This sediment:
- Reduces efficiency (insulates the heat source from the water)
- Creates a rumbling or popping noise during heating (steam pockets in sediment)
- Accelerates corrosion of the tank bottom
- Shortens water heater life

**How to flush:**
1. Turn off the cold water supply to the water heater
2. Turn off the heater (gas: turn to "Pilot"; electric: turn off breaker)
3. Connect a garden hose to the drain valve at the bottom of the tank
4. Run the hose to a floor drain or outside
5. Open the drain valve and a nearby hot water faucet (to let air in so water flows)
6. Let the water (and sediment) drain until it runs clear — this may take 10-20 minutes
7. Close the drain valve, remove hose, open cold water supply, let tank refill
8. Turn heater back on

In hard water areas (heavy mineral content), flush every 6 months.

### Test the T&P Relief Valve (Annually)

The Temperature and Pressure relief valve (T&P valve) is a critical safety device. If pressure or temperature in the tank exceeds safe limits, it opens and releases water. Without a functioning T&P valve, an overheated water heater can explode.

**Testing:**
1. Place a bucket under the discharge pipe from the T&P valve
2. Lift the lever for 2-3 seconds — water should flow out
3. Release the lever — flow should stop cleanly

If the valve drips after the test, it has failed (valves sometimes don't re-seat after testing, especially if they haven't been tested in years). Replace the T&P valve ($15-25, screw-in replacement, same rating as original). If the valve is frequently opening on its own (not during testing), call a plumber — there's a pressure problem.

## Warning Signs

**Rumbling or popping noise:** Sediment buildup — flush the tank.

**Rust-colored hot water:** Tank corrosion — the water heater is likely near end of life. First, check whether the anode rod is depleted (see below).

**Leaking from the tank body:** Rust-through. The tank cannot be repaired — replace it. Water heaters with tank leaks can fail catastrophically; act promptly.

**Leaking from the T&P valve:** Valve has failed open (replace it) or there's an overpressure/overtemperature issue (call a plumber).

**Inadequate hot water (tank type):** Sediment buildup, element failure (electric), or undersized tank for current household.

---

## The Anode Rod

An anode rod is a magnesium or aluminum rod inside the tank that sacrificially corrodes to protect the steel tank from rust. It's what makes the tank last years instead of months.

Anode rods last 3-5 years depending on water chemistry. When the rod is depleted, the tank itself begins corroding.

**Checking/replacing:** The anode rod screws into a port on the top of the tank (sometimes under the cover, sometimes with its own plug). Use a 1-1/16" socket and a lot of force — they're often seized. If it's less than 1/2" thick or coated in calcium, replace it.

Replacing an anode rod ($20-40 for a good magnesium rod) can extend tank life significantly. This is the most neglected water heater maintenance task.

---

## When to Replace vs. Repair

A water heater under 8 years old with a fixable problem (element, thermostat, T&P valve) is worth repairing.

A water heater over 10 years old with any significant problem is usually better replaced. The tank itself is an unknown — if the anode rod has been depleted for years, corrosion may be advanced even if there's no external sign yet.

When replacing, consider a heat pump water heater if your home uses electric resistance heating — the energy savings pay back the premium cost in a few years.
