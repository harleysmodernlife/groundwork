## Forming and Pouring Concrete

Concrete takes the shape of whatever you pour it into. The form is temporary — built just for the pour, then stripped once the concrete is strong enough. Getting the form right determines the geometry of the finished concrete. A form that fails or moves during the pour is a disaster that's expensive and messy to fix.

---

## Planning the Pour

**Calculate volume.** Concrete is ordered and sold by the cubic yard (27 cubic feet). For a slab: multiply length × width × thickness in feet, divide by 27. Add 10% for waste and variations in subgrade depth.

*Example:* A 20' × 20' slab at 4" thick: 20 × 20 × 0.333 = 133 cubic feet ÷ 27 = 4.9 cubic yards. Order 5.5 yards.

**Assess access.** Where will the concrete truck pull up? Ready-mix trucks have chutes that extend 10-15 feet. Beyond that range, you need a pump truck (adds cost), a concrete buggy, or wheelbarrows. A 9-yard truck weighs 66,000+ pounds — don't drive it where it'll damage the driveway or lawn.

**Schedule correctly.** Concrete begins setting immediately after water contacts cement. Hot weather reduces working time (as little as 30-45 minutes); cool weather extends it (2-3 hours). Plan the pour so you can finish before the concrete becomes unworkable. For large pours, have enough crew to work quickly.

**Check weather.** Don't pour in rain (dilutes the mix), in temperatures below 40°F without protective measures, or when temperatures above 90°F are combined with low humidity and wind.

---

## Subgrade Preparation

The concrete slab sits on a subgrade (the soil beneath it). The subgrade provides support — concrete sitting on settled or poorly compacted soil will crack.

**Compact the subgrade.** Use a plate compactor (for large areas) or hand tamper. Compact in lifts — don't dump 12" of gravel and compact it once; add 4-6" at a time, compact, then add more.

**Granular base.** For slabs, install 4" of compacted gravel (crushed stone, not pea gravel) over the subgrade. Gravel drains well, doesn't settle, and provides a uniform working surface. Screed this base to the correct elevation before forming.

**Vapor barrier.** For interior slabs (garages, basements), install 10-15 mil polyethylene sheeting over the gravel base. This prevents moisture vapor from migrating up through the concrete. Overlap seams by 12", tape seams. Protect the barrier from damage during the pour.

**Grade to drain.** Exterior slabs should slope away from buildings — minimum 1/8" per foot, preferably 1/4" per foot. Interior slabs are typically flat (with any drain pits being the low points). Establish the finished elevation at each corner before setting forms.

## Building Forms

### Wood Forms

**Material:** 2x4 or 2x6 dimensional lumber for forming edges. The lumber must be straight — a bowed form board means a bowed concrete edge. Check and select for straightness. For residential slabs, use doubled 2x4 staked at 24" intervals for a 4" slab.

**Staking:** Drive 2x4 or rebar stakes at 2-3 foot intervals outside the form boards. The stake holds the form board at the correct height and resists the pressure of wet concrete pushing outward. Screws hold the form board to the stake — never use nails alone, because the impact of driving can knock forms out of position.

**Setting elevation:** Use a builder's level, laser level, or water level to set all form boards to the correct finished elevation. Snap chalk lines on stakes, then align form boards to the lines. Concrete naturally finds level, but the form edge determines the finished edge elevation.

**Radius and curves:** Flexible 1/4" or 1/2" plywood bent around stakes handles curves. Multiple thin strips can make tighter curves than a single thicker piece.

**Inside corners:** Form boards meet at an angle. Use double stakes at corners to provide solid nailing surface.

**Release agent:** Spray or brush form release oil on the interior face of form boards before pouring. This prevents concrete from bonding to the wood, making form stripping easier and protecting the wood for reuse.

### Edge Forms for Slabs

The form board top edge defines the finished concrete surface. All screeding is done to the top of the form board. Set the top edge of the form board at the finished concrete elevation (accounting for any screed board thickness you'll use as a guide).

**Screed rails:** For wide slabs, place screed rails — straight boards or pipe spanning across the form — at intervals so the screed can be pulled across the slab. These are set to the same elevation as the form edges and removed as you fill their zone.

## Reinforcement

### Rebar

Steel reinforcing bar adds tensile strength. Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension — rebar carries tension loads and controls cracking.

**Sizing:** Common residential sizes: #3 (3/8" diameter), #4 (1/2" diameter), #5 (5/8" diameter). Designations are in eighths of an inch.

**Cover:** Rebar must be covered by a minimum concrete thickness (cover) to protect it from corrosion:
- Slabs not in contact with ground: 3/4" cover
- Concrete in contact with ground: 3" cover
- Concrete exposed to seawater or deicing chemicals: 4" cover

Use plastic rebar chairs (supports) to hold rebar at the correct height. Don't let rebar rest directly on the subgrade — it'll be at the bottom of the slab with no cover.

**Placement patterns:** Slabs typically use a grid of rebar at 12" or 18" centers in both directions. Footings typically use two or three horizontal bars running lengthwise.

**Laps:** When rebar sections meet, they must overlap a minimum length (lap length) to transfer load between them. Typically 40 bar diameters — for #4 rebar (1/2"), the lap length is 20". Tie laps with wire ties.

### Welded Wire Reinforcement (WWR)

Sheets of welded wire grid used in place of individual rebar in slabs. Easier to install, less material cost, but provides less reinforcement than rebar on a weight basis.

**Placement:** Must be in the top third of the slab for crack control — not the bottom. Chairs or dobies hold it up. A common mistake is placing WWR on the ground, then pouring and hoping it floats up. It doesn't.

### Fiber Reinforcement

Polypropylene, steel, or glass fibers added to the concrete mix. Distributed throughout the mix, they provide crack control (especially plastic shrinkage cracks) but don't replace rebar for structural reinforcement. The truck driver typically adds them at the job site by dumping the bag into the truck.

## Placing Concrete

**Work quickly.** Once the truck is on site, begin placing immediately. Concrete that sits in the truck or in forms without consolidation begins to set, reducing workability.

**Don't move the concrete horizontally.** The natural inclination is to dump concrete in one spot and use a shovel or vibrator to push it sideways to fill the form. This causes segregation — the heavy aggregate stays in the pile while the cement paste spreads. Move the chute or pump hose, not the concrete.

**Layer thickness:** Place in layers of 12-18" for deep sections like walls and columns. Each layer must be consolidated before the next is placed. Concrete placed too thick traps air and doesn't consolidate properly.

**Fill the form corners:** Ensure concrete gets into all corners of the form, especially around rebar. Use a shovel or rod to work concrete into corners.

### Consolidation (Vibrating)

Air voids trapped in concrete dramatically reduce strength. A concrete vibrator (an electric or pneumatic pencil vibrator) liquefies the concrete around it momentarily, allowing air bubbles to rise.

**Vibrator use:**
- Insert at regular intervals (typically every 12-18", or about 1.5× the vibrator head diameter)
- Penetrate the previous layer by about 6" to tie the layers together
- Hold in place for 5-15 seconds — until air bubbles stop rising at that location
- Slowly withdraw at 3" per second — pulling out too fast leaves a channel
- Don't drag the vibrator horizontally or use it to move concrete

**Over-vibration** causes segregation — the aggregate sinks and paste rises. Use the minimum vibration time to consolidate, then move.

## Screeding

Screeding (also called striking off) removes excess concrete and brings the surface to the correct elevation. Done immediately after placing.

**Screed board:** A straight 2x4 long enough to span the form boards or screed rails. The bottom edge rides on the form edge at the correct elevation.

**Motion:** Two people handle a screed board on a wide slab. Use a sawing motion while moving the board forward, removing excess concrete. Make multiple passes if the first doesn't produce a uniformly level surface.

**Bull float:** After screeding, push a large flat float (bull float) back and forth across the surface. This embeds aggregate below the surface, flattens ridges left by screeding, and closes open voids. Push with the leading edge slightly raised, pull with the trailing edge slightly raised.

---

## Timing for Finishing

After screeding and bull floating, you wait for the concrete to set enough for finishing. The timing depends on temperature, humidity, and the mix.

**Bleed water:** As the concrete sets, water rises to the surface (bleed water). This is normal. Do not work the surface while bleed water is present — doing so brings excess water to the surface, creating a weak, dusty surface layer.

**Surface readiness test:** When the bleed water has evaporated and footprints in the surface are 1/4" or less deep, the concrete is ready for finishing. In hot weather this might be 30-60 minutes after placing. In cool weather, several hours.

**The finishing operations are covered in the next lesson:** edging, jointing, troweling, and brooming.

---

## Stripping Forms

**Wait for adequate strength.** Forms for slabs and walls can usually be stripped at 24-48 hours in normal temperatures. Overhead forms (soffits, decks) require longer — typically 7+ days. Cold weather significantly delays adequate strength.

**Strip carefully.** Use a flat bar to pry forms away from the concrete. Don't lever against the concrete surface — you'll chip and spall corners.

**Patch imperfections.** Bug holes (small air voids on the surface), honeycombing (larger voids from poor consolidation), and small surface defects can be patched with cement-sand mortar or a pre-bagged patching compound. Wet the concrete first — dry concrete sucks moisture from the patch, causing it to crack and fall out.
