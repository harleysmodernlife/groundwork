## Finishing and Sealing Concrete

Concrete finishing is where the surface is shaped, smoothed, and textured. The result determines how the concrete looks, how slip-resistant it is, and how well it resists moisture and wear. Most concrete finishing is done in the window between when bleed water evaporates and when the concrete hardens enough to resist working — a window that might be an hour in hot weather or six hours in cool.

---

## The Finishing Sequence

Concrete finishing follows a sequence that you can't reverse or skip:

1. **Screed** to get it level (done during placement — covered in the previous lesson)
2. **Bull float** to embed aggregate and flatten
3. **Wait** for bleed water to evaporate
4. **Edge** the perimeter
5. **Joint** the surface (control joints)
6. **Float** to consolidate and flatten
7. **Trowel** for a smooth finish (or texture instead of troweling)
8. **Apply texture** (broom, exposed aggregate, etc.)
9. **Cure**

---

## Edging

The first hand operation. Run an edger — a hand tool with a curved bottom edge — along the form board to round the edge of the slab. The rounded edge prevents chipping. Press the flat face flat against the concrete surface, with the curved portion tracing against the form board.

Work the edger back and forth while moving along the edge. Apply moderate pressure. The aggregate embeds, the paste comes up, and you leave a smooth, rounded edge that will hold up to impact rather than chipping square.

---

## Control Joints

Concrete cracks. This is not a defect — it's a physical certainty. Concrete shrinks as it cures (drying shrinkage), and it expands and contracts with temperature. Cracks will appear somewhere. Control joints tell the concrete where to crack — in a straight, intentional line rather than wherever it wants.

**How they work:** A groove in the surface creates a plane of weakness. When the slab cracks (and it will), it cracks along the groove, which is hidden from view and doesn't compromise structural integrity.

**Depth:** One-quarter of the slab thickness minimum. For a 4" slab, joints must be at least 1" deep. Shallow joints don't work — the concrete cracks through them rather than at them.

**Spacing:** The rule of thumb is 2-3 times the slab thickness in feet. For a 4" slab, joints every 8-12 feet. Practical guide: divide the slab into roughly square panels. Panels with L-shapes or irregular geometry crack at the re-entrant corners — plan a joint at those locations.

**Methods:**
- *Grooving tool:* A hand tool with a blade of the correct depth, run along a straight edge while the concrete is still plastic (soft enough to tool). Leaves a V-groove in the surface.
- *Saw cutting:* Cut with a concrete saw (dry diamond blade or wet saw) after the concrete has hardened enough to not ravel (typically 4-12 hours after finishing). The advantage: saw cuts produce a clean, straight joint. The disadvantage: you must saw before the concrete cracks on its own, and timing depends on weather and mix.

**Isolation joints (expansion joints):** Different from control joints. Isolation joints fully separate the slab from adjacent structures (building foundation, curbs, drain pipes). Use 1/2" foam isolation joint material along the foundation wall before pouring the slab. This allows the slab to move independently from the structure.

## Floating

Float the surface after the bleed water is gone and the edging and jointing are complete. Floating brings the cream (fine paste) to the surface, embeds aggregate, and removes small imperfections from the screed and bull float.

**Hand float (Magnesium float):** A flat magnesium or aluminum plate with a handle. Work in arcs across the surface, keeping the leading edge slightly raised. Use moderate pressure. The goal is a level, slightly rough surface — not smooth yet.

**Power float (walk-behind float):** For large commercial slabs, walk-behind power trowels with float pans speed up the process. These are typically rented equipment.

**Timing:** Float when your knee prints are no more than 3/8" deep. Too early and you work up bleed water. Too late and the concrete resists the tool.

---

## Troweling

Steel troweling produces the hard, smooth, dense surface characteristic of interior floors, garage floors, and high-traffic surfaces. Each successive troweling pass is done later and with more pressure, consolidating and densifying the surface.

**First trowel pass:** After floating, use a steel trowel to smooth the surface. The trowel face is kept nearly flat, with only a very slight angle. This first pass closes the surface and removes float marks.

**Second and third passes:** As the concrete stiffens further, additional passes with increasing pressure and trowel angle produce a harder, denser surface. A final pass with the trowel nearly vertical (the blade edge pressed hard into the surface) produces a hard, shiny finish.

**Don't over-trowel too early.** Troweling while bleed water is still present works water into the surface paste, creating a weak, dusty surface that dusts under foot traffic. Wait for the right timing.

**Burnished steel finish:** A very smooth, very hard surface. Beautiful for basements and interior commercial floors. Slippery when wet — apply a non-slip sealer or texture for areas that get wet.

## Texturing

For exterior concrete (driveways, sidewalks, pool decks), a textured surface provides slip resistance. Texturing replaces the final trowel pass.

### Broom Finish

The most common exterior texture. After hand floating, drag a concrete broom across the surface in parallel lines to create a striated texture.

**Coarse broom:** Heavy bristles create a rougher texture with more traction. For areas with vehicle traffic or where maximum slip resistance is needed.

**Fine broom:** Lighter texture, better appearance. For walkways and areas with mostly foot traffic.

**Direction:** Broom lines should run perpendicular to the direction of travel (foot traffic), so people step across the ridges rather than along them. On ramps, broom across the ramp width.

**Technique:** Pull the broom in a straight line with consistent pressure. Lift, return to the start, and broom the next strip overlapping slightly. Don't scrub back and forth.

### Exposed Aggregate

The surface paste is removed to expose the coarse aggregate, creating a textured surface with visible stone. Attractive and durable.

**Method 1 (surface retarder):** Apply a chemical surface retarder immediately after the bull float, before the concrete sets. Cover with plastic. The next day, pressure wash the surface — the retarder kept the surface paste from hardening while the concrete below hardened, allowing the paste to wash away and reveal the aggregate.

**Method 2 (seeding):** Broadcast decorative aggregate (colored stone, glass, river pebbles) over the freshly screeded surface. Float it in with a bull float. After the concrete sets, pressure wash to expose the seeded aggregate.

**Slip resistance:** Exposed aggregate is naturally slip-resistant due to the textured stone surface.

### Stamped Concrete

Rubber stamp mats are pressed into the concrete while it's still plastic to imprint patterns — cobblestone, brick, slate, wood plank. Integral or surface-applied color can match stone or brick appearance.

**Timing is critical:** Stamp too early and the pattern blurs. Too late and the mats won't leave an impression and may tear the surface.

**Requires experience.** Color application, stamp placement, corner patterns, and release agent application all require practice. For residential decorative concrete, hiring an experienced contractor is worth it — the mistakes look very obvious.

## Sealing

Concrete sealers protect the surface from moisture, deicing salts, oil stains, and abrasion. They're applied after curing is complete (typically 28 days, though some sealers allow application at 7 days).

### Types of Sealers

**Penetrating sealers (silicates, siloxanes, silanes):** React with the concrete matrix to form water-repellent compounds within the concrete. No visible surface film. Don't change the appearance significantly. Excellent for driveways and exterior concrete — protect against freeze-thaw damage from water absorption.

**Acrylic sealers (topical):** A thin film on the concrete surface. Provide a wet-look sheen (high-gloss or low-gloss versions). Moderate protection, must be reapplied every 2-5 years. Common choice for stamped and decorative concrete.

**Epoxy and polyurethane coatings:** Thick film coatings with excellent abrasion resistance and chemical resistance. Used on garage floors. Epoxy requires careful surface prep — the floor must be clean, dry, and free of any previous sealer. Diamond grinding or acid etching opens the concrete surface to allow mechanical bonding.

### Surface Preparation for Sealers

**Clean and dry:** Sealers applied to dirty or wet concrete fail quickly. Pressure wash, let dry fully (minimum 24 hours, longer in humid conditions).

**Previous coatings:** Test for existing coatings before applying a new sealer — a new sealer applied over an incompatible existing sealer peels. Test by applying a drop of muriatic acid — if it foams immediately, the surface is clean concrete. If not, a coating is present.

**Profile for epoxy:** Acid etch with muriatic acid (10% solution, scrub, rinse thoroughly) or diamond grind to provide mechanical bond for epoxy. This step is not optional — epoxy applied to a smooth, dense surface will peel.

### Application

**Rollers and sprayers:** Penetrating sealers are sprayed. Topical sealers are rolled or sprayed. Use the rate specified by the manufacturer — applying too thick causes milky appearance and peeling.

**Temperature:** Apply when surface and air temperature are between 50-90°F. Never apply to a surface in direct sun that's hot to the touch — the sealer flashes before penetrating.

**Recoat window:** If applying two coats, apply the second coat while the first is still slightly tacky (check the manufacturer's time window). If the first coat fully cures, the second coat may not bond well.

## Common Concrete Problems and Causes

**Dusting (surface deteriorates under traffic):** Worked the surface while bleed water was present, or concrete was allowed to dry too quickly. The surface layer is weak. Treatment: apply a chemical hardener (sodium silicate or lithium silicate), which reacts with calcium in the concrete to form harder compounds in the surface.

**Crazing (network of fine surface cracks):** Rapid drying of the surface paste. Prevented by curing promptly and protecting from sun and wind.

**Scaling (surface layers flaking off):** Freeze-thaw damage combined with deicing salts. Use 3,500+ PSI air-entrained concrete for exterior slabs, seal properly, and avoid sodium chloride (table salt / rock salt) deicers — use calcium magnesium acetate or sand instead.

**Map cracking / D-cracking:** Aggregate that absorbs water and expands during freeze-thaw. A material selection issue — use non-absorptive aggregate in cold climates.

**Efflorescence (white deposits on surface):** Soluble salts carried to the surface by water. Apply efflorescence cleaner (dilute acid), then seal to prevent water from continuing to carry salts through.
