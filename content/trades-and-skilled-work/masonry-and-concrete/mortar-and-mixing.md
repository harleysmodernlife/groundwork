## Mortar: Types, Mixing, and Application

Mortar is the binding material that holds masonry units — brick, block, and stone — together. Unlike concrete, mortar isn't designed to carry structural load on its own. It's designed to bond units, accommodate slight imperfections in unit size, and distribute load evenly. Choosing the right mortar type and mixing it properly determines how long masonry work lasts and how well it performs.

---

## What Mortar Is

Mortar is a mixture of:
- **Portland cement:** The binding agent (same cement used in concrete)
- **Masonry sand (mason's sand):** Fine aggregate, free of organic material and clay
- **Water:** For workability and hydration
- **Hydrated lime (in most mortars):** Improves workability, water retention, and flexibility

The ratio of these components determines the mortar type. More cement = stronger but stiffer mortar. More lime = more flexible but weaker mortar.

Mortar joints are deliberately weaker than the masonry units they connect. When a masonry wall must absorb movement (thermal expansion, settlement), it's far better for the mortar joints to crack than for the bricks or blocks to crack — mortar joints can be repointed, but cracked bricks and blocks are expensive to replace.

---

## Mortar Types

ASTM C270 classifies mortar into four types based on strength, flexibility, and use:

### Type S

**Compressive strength:** ~1,800 PSI
**Use:** Below-grade masonry (foundations, retaining walls), exterior at-grade applications, any masonry subject to lateral loads (wind, seismic, soil pressure).

Type S provides high bond strength and good durability in demanding conditions. The standard choice for:
- Foundation walls
- Retaining walls in contact with soil
- Exterior below-grade block work
- Brick pavers and hardscaping

**Not recommended for:** Re-pointing older brick buildings — too stiff, can crack old soft brick.

### Type N

**Compressive strength:** ~750 PSI
**Use:** General-purpose above-grade exterior masonry. The most widely used type for non-load-bearing exterior applications.

Type N provides a good balance of strength and flexibility. Suitable for:
- Exterior chimneys
- Non-structural brick veneer
- Natural stone (stone requires flexibility — harder mortars crack stone)
- Above-grade exterior walls not subject to severe lateral loads

**The standard choice for most exterior brick and block projects.**

### Type M

**Compressive strength:** ~2,500 PSI
**Use:** Below-grade foundations and masonry in contact with earth. High compressive strength, but lower flexibility.

Less common than Type S for most applications. Sometimes used for stone foundations and masonry in severe soil contact.

### Type O

**Compressive strength:** ~350 PSI
**Use:** Interior, non-load-bearing applications. Very flexible.

For historic restoration on soft, old brick where any harder mortar would damage the brick. For interior tile and block work where movement must be accommodated without cracking the units.

**Not for exterior or structural use.**

## Mixing Mortar

### Pre-bagged Mortar

Available as Type S, N, or M. Add water only — the cement, lime, and sand are pre-proportioned. For small jobs, pre-bagged is convenient and consistent.

**How to mix:**
1. Pour 3/4 of the required water into a clean bucket or mortar box
2. Add the dry mortar mix
3. Mix with a drill and mixing paddle or by hand with a margin trowel
4. Add remaining water gradually to achieve the correct consistency
5. Let the mix "slake" for 5 minutes — the lime needs time to hydrate fully
6. Remix and adjust consistency if needed

**Consistency:** Mortar should hold its shape when squeezed in your hand and not slump off the trowel, but should be plastic enough to work easily. "Peanut butter" consistency is the common description.

### Site-Mixed Mortar (Proportioned from Components)

**For Type N mortar (1:1:6 mix):**
- 1 part Portland cement
- 1 part hydrated lime
- 6 parts masonry sand (by volume)

**For Type S mortar (1:0.5:4.5 mix):**
- 1 part Portland cement
- 1/2 part hydrated lime
- 4.5 parts masonry sand

Measure by volume using a consistent container (e.g., a 5-gallon bucket).

**Mixing sequence:**
1. Dry-mix the cement, lime, and sand in the mortar box or mixer until uniform in color
2. Add water gradually while mixing
3. Mix for 5 minutes minimum
4. Rest for 5 minutes (slake)
5. Remix, adjust water to final consistency

**Mortar life:** Mix only what you can use in 2 hours. Mortar begins to set and loses workability. If mortar stiffens in the bucket, add a small amount of water and retemper once — but never add water more than once, and never use mortar that has begun to set.

## Mortar Consistency for Different Applications

**Too wet (soupy):** Mortar sags off the trowel, joints slump, units don't stay in position. Will have poor strength.

**Too dry (stiff):** Hard to apply, doesn't bond well to units, tears rather than spreading smoothly.

**Correct for brick/block:** Holds a furrow cut with a trowel edge. Doesn't slump when placed on top of a brick. Sticks to the trowel when turned sideways (doesn't slump off).

**For stone:** Stone is often smoother than brick and requires slightly wetter mortar for adhesion. Stone also has variable thicknesses that require more adjustment.

**For tuckpointing (repointing):** Stiffer mortar than for new work. The mortar is packed into existing joints rather than applied with a trowel, and stiffer mortar holds in the joint during compaction.

## Applying Mortar: The Basic Technique

### Setting Bed (Horizontal Joints)

The mortar bed that goes below each masonry unit:

1. Load the trowel with mortar from the mortar board
2. **Throw a line:** Slap the mortar onto the block or brick top in a line the length of 2-3 units. The motion is a flick of the wrist that spreads the mortar in a furrow along the center.
3. **Furrow the bed:** Draw the point of the trowel through the center of the mortar line to create a shallow furrow. The furrow ensures the mortar will spread evenly when the next unit is pressed down — without a furrow, you get hollow spots in the joint.

### Buttering the Head Joint (Vertical Joints)

Head joints are the vertical mortar joints between units in the same course:

1. Pick up the unit to be placed
2. Apply mortar to the end of the unit (or to both ends — the unit being set and the unit it's set against)
3. Apply generously — you need enough to fill the joint and squeeze out slightly when the unit is pressed into place

**The squeeze-out:** Mortar should squeeze out of both the bed joint and head joint slightly as the unit is pressed into place. This squeeze-out confirms you have full mortar contact. If nothing squeezes out, there's probably a void.

### Placing and Tapping

Press the unit down into the bed joint with a slight rocking motion. Check for level and alignment. Tap with the handle of the trowel to set the unit. Excess mortar that squeezed out is scraped off with the trowel. Don't tap a unit that's already nearly set — you'll break the mortar bond without compressing the joint.

## Joint Types

The mortar joint profile affects drainage, appearance, and weather resistance:

**Concave (rodded) joint:** Tooled with a rounded jointing tool (jointer) while mortar is still green (thumb-print hard but not fully set). Creates a slightly inset, concave profile. The tooling compresses the mortar against the units, improving weather resistance. The standard joint for most exterior masonry.

**V-joint:** A V-shaped profile. Similar to concave in weather resistance. Common on stone work.

**Flush joint:** The mortar is cut flush with the face of the unit with a trowel. Less durable than a concave joint because tooling doesn't compress the mortar. Used on block that will be painted or plastered.

**Raked joint:** The mortar is raked back 3/4" or more from the face. Dramatic shadowed look. Poor weather resistance — water collects in the joint. Not for exposed exterior masonry in wet or cold climates.

**Weathered joint:** Sloped outward at the top to shed water. Requires skill to produce consistently. Good weather resistance.

### Tooling Timing

Tool joints when the mortar is "thumb-print hard" — it holds an impression when you press your thumb into it, but doesn't smear. Too early and the mortar smears. Too late and the tool drags and tears.

On warm days, you may need to tool frequently to keep up with the mortar set. On cool days, you have more time. Start testing the first joints laid every 30-45 minutes.

## Masonry Cement vs. Portland-Lime Mortar

**Masonry cement:** Pre-blended Portland cement with lime and air-entraining agents. Just add sand and water. Simpler to mix.

**Portland-lime mortar:** Mix Portland cement, lime, and sand separately. Allows more control over proportions. Generally considered to have better bond than masonry cement.

The performance difference is minimal for most applications. Masonry cement is convenient for most residential work. Portland-lime is preferred by experienced masons for critical work and restoration.
