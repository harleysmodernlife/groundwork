## How Concrete Works: Mix Design and Curing

Concrete is the most widely used construction material in the world. Understanding what it's made of, how it gets strong, and what can go wrong explains why concrete work has so many specific practices — the water ratios, the curing requirements, the temperature restrictions. None of it is arbitrary.

---

## What Concrete Is

Concrete is a composite material: aggregates (sand and gravel) bound together by a hardened paste of cement and water. The cement is the active ingredient — it reacts with water to form a hard, stone-like material through a chemical process called hydration.

**Components:**

**Portland cement:** A finely ground powder of limestone, clay, and other minerals that have been heated to very high temperatures (calcined) and then ground to a fine powder. When mixed with water, it undergoes hydration — a chemical reaction that produces calcium silicate hydrate crystals, forming a rigid binding matrix.

**Aggregates:** Fine aggregate (sand) fills spaces between the coarse particles. Coarse aggregate (gravel or crushed stone) is the bulk filler, typically 3/8" to 1-1/2" diameter. Aggregates are inert — they don't participate in the chemical reaction, but their size, shape, and gradation directly affect the workability and finished strength of concrete.

**Water:** Reacts with cement. Excess water doesn't hydrate more cement — it sits in the mix and eventually evaporates, leaving voids. Every extra gallon of water added to a concrete mix reduces compressive strength.

**Admixtures:** Added in small quantities to modify properties:
- *Water reducers (plasticizers):* Make concrete more workable without adding water
- *Air-entraining agents:* Introduce microscopic air bubbles that improve freeze-thaw resistance
- *Accelerators:* Speed up set time (calcium chloride) — useful in cold weather
- *Retarders:* Slow set time — useful in hot weather or long pours

## The Water-to-Cement Ratio

The water-to-cement (w/c) ratio is the single most important factor in concrete strength. It's the ratio of water weight to cement weight in the mix.

**Lower w/c = stronger, less permeable concrete.**
**Higher w/c = weaker, more permeable concrete.**

| w/c Ratio | Typical Compressive Strength (28 days) | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 0.35-0.40 | 5,000-7,000 PSI | High-performance, low permeability |
| 0.45 | ~4,500 PSI | Structural concrete, exposed conditions |
| 0.50 | ~4,000 PSI | Standard residential slabs and footings |
| 0.55-0.60 | 3,000-3,500 PSI | Less demanding applications |
| 0.70+ | <2,500 PSI | Very weak — adding too much water on site causes this |

**Why contractors add water on site:** Wet concrete is easier to work — it flows around rebar, levels out, and finishes more easily. Adding water makes their job easier. It also reduces the final strength significantly. Adding a single 5-gallon bucket of water to a standard ready-mix truck can reduce strength by 20%.

**Slump:** The measure of concrete workability. A high-slump mix (5-7") is wet and flowable. A low-slump mix (2-3") is stiff. Specify appropriate slump for the application — not maximum slump because it's easier to work.

## Concrete Mix Design

### Proportions

A typical residential concrete mix:
- 1 part cement
- 2 parts sand (fine aggregate)
- 3 parts gravel (coarse aggregate)
- Water to achieve target w/c ratio

The "1-2-3 mix" is a basic rule of thumb. Professional mixes are designed by a concrete mix engineer who accounts for aggregate properties, cement type, admixtures, and target strength.

### Compressive Strength Specification

Concrete is specified by its 28-day compressive strength in PSI (pounds per square inch):

**2,500 PSI:** Sidewalks, interior slabs not exposed to freeze-thaw.

**3,000 PSI:** Standard residential footings and foundations in moderate climates.

**3,500-4,000 PSI:** Driveways, garage slabs, any exterior concrete subject to freeze-thaw cycles and deicing chemicals. Minimum for most exposed slabs per ACI recommendations.

**5,000 PSI+:** High-strength structural applications, bridge decks, prestressed concrete.

Ready-mix concrete is ordered by strength specification: "Give me a yard of 4,000 PSI mix." The concrete plant designs the mix to achieve that strength.

### Aggregate Size

Maximum aggregate size affects mix design:
- 3/8" aggregate: For thin sections, rebar-congested pours
- 3/4" aggregate: Standard for footings, slabs, most structural concrete
- 1-1/2": Massive pours (dams, large foundations) where rebar spacing allows

Maximum aggregate size should not exceed 1/3 the narrowest dimension of the form or 3/4 of the minimum clear spacing between rebar.

## Hydration and Curing

### The Hydration Reaction

Hydration is not drying — it's a chemical reaction between cement and water that produces heat (exothermic) and forms the crystalline structure that gives concrete its strength. The reaction continues for weeks and months, but the rate decreases dramatically after the first 28 days.

Strength gain over time (approximate, for normal Portland cement):
- 1 day: ~30% of 28-day strength
- 3 days: ~50%
- 7 days: ~70%
- 14 days: ~85%
- 28 days: ~100% (the design strength)
- 1 year: ~115-120%

Concrete continues to gain strength for years if moisture is available for continued hydration.

### What Curing Means

Curing means maintaining moisture and temperature so hydration can continue. If concrete dries out too quickly, hydration stops — the surface layer is weak and dusty, and the concrete won't reach its design strength.

**Minimum curing period:** 7 days for normal Portland cement. 3 days for rapid-hardening cement. 14 days for fly ash or slag cements.

**Curing methods:**
- *Wet curing:* Keep the surface continuously wet — spray water, cover with wet burlap, or flood with water. The best method for achieving full strength.
- *Curing compounds:* Chemical sprays applied immediately after finishing that form a membrane, trapping moisture. Convenient for large slabs but less effective than wet curing.
- *Plastic sheeting:* Cover the surface with polyethylene film, sealed at the edges. Traps moisture. Simple and effective for slabs.

### Temperature Effects

**Hot weather:** Accelerates hydration — the concrete sets faster, reducing the working time. Rapid evaporation from the surface causes plastic shrinkage cracks (cracks that form before the concrete hardens). Mitigate by:
- Pouring in the early morning (cooler, less wind)
- Pre-wetting forms and subgrade
- Adding retarder admixture
- Using sunshades and evaporation retarder sprays
- Curing aggressively immediately after finishing

**Cold weather:** Hydration slows significantly below 50°F and essentially stops below 40°F. Freshly placed concrete that freezes can be permanently damaged — ice crystal formation disrupts the developing hydration matrix.

Protect fresh concrete in cold weather:
- Don't place on frozen ground (ground temperature must be above 32°F)
- Pre-heat aggregate and water to bring the mix temperature above 55°F
- Use accelerating admixtures
- Insulate with curing blankets
- Use heated enclosures for very cold conditions

**Never add hot water to speed things up** in cold weather without temperature calculation — concrete that's too hot at placement loses workability rapidly and can have reduced final strength.

## Types of Portland Cement

**Type I/II:** General purpose. The most common. Used for footings, slabs, sidewalks, and most residential work.

**Type III:** High early strength. Gains strength faster (3-day strength comparable to Type I 7-day). Used when formwork must be stripped quickly or cold weather requires faster strength gain.

**Type IV:** Low heat of hydration. Used in massive pours (dams) where heat buildup from hydration causes cracking. Rarely seen in residential work.

**Type V:** Sulfate-resistant. For concrete in contact with soil or water high in sulfate content, which attacks normal concrete. Required in some regions.

**Supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs):** Partially replace Portland cement:
- *Fly ash:* A byproduct of coal combustion. Improves workability, reduces water demand, extends set time, and often improves long-term strength. Very common in ready-mix. Reduces cost.
- *Slag:* Ground blast furnace slag. Similar benefits to fly ash, often better long-term strength. Slower early strength gain.
- *Silica fume:* Very fine pozzolan — dramatically increases strength and reduces permeability. Used in high-performance mixes.

## Reading a Ready-Mix Ticket

When ordering ready-mix concrete, the delivery ticket specifies:

- **Mix designation:** The ordered strength (e.g., 4000 PSI)
- **Slump:** Ordered vs. delivered
- **Cement content:** Bags per yard
- **Water added:** Gallons added at the plant (may increase if more water is added on site)
- **Admixtures:** Types and dosages
- **Volume:** Cubic yards ordered and delivered
- **Time of departure and arrival**

The ticket is a quality document. If you receive concrete that doesn't match the specification (wrong slump, wrong mix), you have the right to reject it.

**Making a test cylinder:** For critical pours, sample the concrete midway through the pour (not from the beginning or end of the truck). Fill 4"×8" plastic cylinders in three lifts, rod each lift 25 times to consolidate. Cap and cure under controlled conditions. Send to a lab for compression testing at 7 and 28 days. This is standard practice for structural concrete — it confirms you got what you ordered.
