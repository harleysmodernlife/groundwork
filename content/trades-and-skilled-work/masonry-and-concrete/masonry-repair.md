## Tuckpointing and Masonry Repair

Old masonry doesn't fail because bricks and blocks wear out — it fails because the mortar joints deteriorate. Mortar joints are the intentionally weaker element in masonry construction, and they're expected to need maintenance every 25-50 years. Tuckpointing (also called repointing) replaces deteriorated mortar joints with fresh mortar, restoring weather resistance and structural integrity. It's one of the most common and important masonry repair tasks.

---

## Why Mortar Joints Fail

Mortar is a porous material. Water enters through the pores and migrates through the joint. In climates with freeze-thaw cycles, this water expands as it freezes, mechanically breaking the mortar apart over time. Mortar that's already degraded — due to weathering, mismatched mortar type, or original poor quality — deteriorates faster.

**Signs of joint failure:**
- Mortar is soft, crumbly, or sandy (rub it — good mortar is hard; failing mortar rubs off)
- Mortar has receded 1/4" or more from the face
- Mortar is cracked through (you can see a gap or probe a crack with a wire)
- Mortar is missing in sections
- Water staining on the interior face of walls (mortar failure allowing water infiltration)

Failing mortar isn't just cosmetic. Water entering through failed joints causes:
- Efflorescence (salt staining)
- Freeze-thaw spalling of the brick face
- Mold growth behind veneer
- Water damage to interior framing and finishes
- In severe cases, structural weakening of block and stone walls

---

## Mortar Type Selection for Repointing

This is critical and frequently wrong: **the repointing mortar must not be stronger than the original mortar.**

Older buildings (pre-1920s) were built with lime-only mortar (no Portland cement) or very low-Portland content mortar. Lime mortar is softer and more flexible than modern mortars.

**Why this matters:** When a harder mortar is placed in joints of a softer-mortar wall, and the masonry moves (thermal expansion, settlement), the stiff repointing mortar doesn't give — the brick gives, spalling and cracking. The repair damages the brick to protect the mortar, which is backwards.

**Rule:** Repointing mortar should have similar or lower compressive strength than the original mortar.

- Historic buildings with soft, old brick: Type O or K mortar (lime-rich, low Portland)
- Older brick from the 1920s-1970s: Type N (general purpose)
- Modern brick and block: Type N or Type S depending on location and exposure

**Testing original mortar:** Send a sample to a masonry lab for chemical analysis (ASTM C1324). This is worth doing on historic buildings before repointing thousands of square feet with the wrong mortar. Field testing: try to gouge the old mortar with a nail — if it scratches easily, it's a softer historic mortar.

## Tools for Tuckpointing

**Grinder with tuckpointing blade:** The tuckpointing blade is a thin, hard disc used to cut mortar out of joints without damaging the brick. Usually 4" or 4-1/2" angle grinder. Provides more control than standard grinding discs. Run along the horizontal (bed) joints first, then the vertical (head) joints.

**Cold chisel and hammer:** The traditional method. A narrow chisel (1/4" to 3/8") for head joints, wider for bed joints. Requires more skill to avoid chipping brick faces.

**Oscillating tool with mortar removal blade:** Useful for small areas and areas where the grinder is too large. Slower than a grinder.

**Hawk:** A flat platform held in one hand, which holds a supply of mortar for troweling.

**Pointing trowel:** Narrow trowel (about 3/4" wide) for packing mortar into joints.

**Tuckpointing trowel (joint filler):** A long, narrow trowel specifically for filling horizontal bed joints.

**Joint jointer / raker:** For tooling the finished joint to the correct profile.

## The Tuckpointing Process

### Step 1: Remove Deteriorated Mortar

Remove all failing mortar to a minimum depth of 3/4" (more is better — ASTM standard is 3/4"). Shallow repointing doesn't bond adequately and fails within a few years.

**Grinding:** Run the tuckpointing blade centered in the joint. It removes the mortar without the disc reaching the brick. Always wear a dust mask or respirator rated for silica — mortar removal generates fine silica dust. Eye protection is mandatory. Ear protection for extended work.

Work bed joints first, then head joints.

**Chiseling:** For head joints (where a grinder can't reach) and for touch-up, use a narrow chisel. Keep the chisel centered in the joint. Angling toward the brick will chip the brick face.

**Blow out dust** with compressed air or brush thoroughly after removal. All loose material and dust must be removed — it will prevent bond.

### Step 2: Wet the Joints

Before applying mortar, dampen the joint with a brush and water. Dry masonry absorbs water from the mortar too quickly, causing the mortar to dry before it bonds properly (a condition called "suction").

Don't saturate — you want damp, not wet. The surface should look damp but have no standing water.

### Step 3: Mix the Mortar

Mix repointing mortar to a stiffer consistency than laying mortar. Repointing mortar is packed into joints rather than placed with a trowel, so it needs to hold its shape under compaction.

Consistency: firm enough to hold a thumbprint, stiff enough to hold its shape when squeezed in a ball.

Pre-bagged repointing mortar is available (Type N is common). For historic work or large projects, mix from components per the analysis results.

**Colored mortar:** Add mortar pigments to match the existing joint color. Mix small test batches, let dry, and compare to the existing joints before committing. The color shifts when wet — always compare dried samples.

### Step 4: Pack the Joints

Apply mortar in layers, not all at once:
- First layer: fill to about halfway. Pack firmly with the pointing trowel.
- Let the first layer firm up slightly (15-30 minutes in warm weather).
- Second layer: fill to within 1/4" of the face.
- Let firm up.
- Final layer: bring to the correct profile and depth.

The purpose of layers: thick mortar applications shrink more as they cure, causing the new joint to pull away from the brick. Thin layers allow each layer to begin to stiffen before the next is added.

**Packing technique:** Hold the hawk with mortar under the joint. Use the pointing trowel to force mortar from the hawk into the joint. Work it firmly against both brick faces to ensure contact. The mortar should be packed, not just placed.

Work the bed joints first (horizontal), then the head joints (vertical). Keep mortar off the brick faces — clean it off immediately if it gets on the brick, before it dries.

### Step 5: Tool the Joint

When the mortar is thumb-print hard (holds an impression but doesn't smear), tool the joint to match the profile of the existing joints. Concave is the most weather-resistant and the most common.

Tool the bed joints first (the sled jointer runs continuously along the bed joint), then the head joints.

Tooling compresses the mortar against the brick faces, improving the bond and weather resistance. Don't skip this step.

### Step 6: Clean Up

After the mortar has cured but before it's fully hardened (typically 4-8 hours after tooling), scrub the wall with a stiff brush to remove any mortar smears. Wet the area first.

If mortar has already hardened on the brick face, a dilute muriatic acid solution (1:10 acid:water) will clean it. Apply carefully (protect skin, eyes, and any vegetation at the base of the wall). Rinse thoroughly.

## Brick Replacement

Spalled or cracked bricks must be replaced, not just repointed over. Spalling brick allows water infiltration that undermines the surrounding mortar and causes adjacent bricks to deteriorate.

### Removing a Damaged Brick

1. Use a grinder or chisel to remove mortar from all four joints surrounding the brick
2. Drill into the center of the brick with a masonry bit and break it up in sections using a chisel — don't try to remove it whole
3. Clear all old mortar from the opening. The back of the cavity (the back wythe or the CMU structure) must be clean
4. Wet the opening

### Inserting the Replacement Brick

1. Find a matching brick — color, texture, and size must match. Old brick is often available from salvage yards and demolition projects. A mismatched brick is obvious and permanent.
2. Wet the replacement brick (dry brick absorbs too much water from the mortar)
3. Butter all five faces that will contact mortar (four sides and the back face)
4. Slide the brick in and press firmly until all mortar joints are at the correct width
5. Tool when the mortar is thumb-print hard

## Crack Repair

Not all cracks should be filled and forgotten. The cause of the crack determines the repair.

**Differential settlement cracks (stair-step or diagonal cracks):** The foundation has moved. Repointing without addressing the foundation movement will result in the crack reopening. Get a structural engineer assessment first.

**Shrinkage cracks (hairline, random):** Normal in new masonry and concrete. Fill with masonry caulk that matches mortar color. Doesn't require full tuckpointing.

**Control joint cracks (at designed joints):** These joints should be filled with a flexible sealant (polyurethane or silicone caulk), not mortar. They're designed to move — mortar is rigid and will crack again.

**Through-the-unit cracks:** If the crack runs through the brick or block (not just the mortar joint), the unit is damaged and must be replaced. A crack through a unit is a plane of weakness that will continue to propagate.

## Chimney Repointing

Chimneys are among the highest-priority repointing jobs because deteriorated chimney mortar allows water into the structure, often without obvious interior symptoms until significant damage has occurred.

**Special considerations:**
- Chimney mortar is exposed on all sides and experiences severe temperature swings — it deteriorates faster than wall mortar
- Water that enters a chimney can run down the flue liner, into the firebox, and onto the hearth or surrounding framing
- Crown (the concrete cap at the top of the chimney) cracks allow significant water entry — repair or replace cracked crowns with elastomeric crown sealant

**Safety:** Working on a chimney requires roof access and ladder work. Use proper safety equipment — a roof ladder (chicken ladder) that hooks over the ridge, a safety harness if near the edge. Do not work on a wet or frosty roof.
