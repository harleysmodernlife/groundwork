## Laying Brick and Block

Masonry is one of the oldest construction skills. The technique hasn't changed much — you're still placing units in mortar, keeping them level and plumb, and building up courses in an interlocking pattern. The details of how you do that efficiently and correctly are where the craft lives.

---

## Before You Start

### Layout and Planning

**Count your units.** Calculate brick or block quantities before ordering. For a wall: count the number of units per course (length ÷ (unit length + joint width)) and multiply by the number of courses. Add 5-10% for breakage and cuts. For brick veneer on a house, use the modular calculation based on wall area.

**Dry stack first.** For anything more complex than a simple straight wall, lay out your first course dry (without mortar) on the footing to work out spacing and pattern. Identify where cuts will be needed. Adjust the starting position to minimize cuts.

**Plan the bond pattern.** The pattern in which units are laid — the bond pattern — determines appearance and structural integrity. Units must interlock so vertical joints don't stack up continuously (continuous vertical joints = planes of weakness).

Common bond patterns:
- **Running bond:** Each course offsets from the one below by half a unit. The simplest and most common pattern. Used for single-wythe walls.
- **Flemish bond:** Alternating headers (brick turned to show the short end, penetrating the wall depth) and stretchers (brick turned to show the long face) in each course. Traditional and attractive, used for multi-wythe structural walls.
- **Common (American) bond:** Five courses of stretchers followed by a course of headers. Traditional structural pattern.
- **Stack bond:** Units aligned in continuous vertical joints. Structurally weak — requires reinforcement. Primarily decorative.

**Establish the lead.** Professional masons begin by building the corners (leads) of a wall first, then filling in between them using a line. The leads establish height, plumb, and bond. For simple walls without corners, set up temporary corner guides.

## Tools

**Brick trowel:** The primary mortar-placing tool. London pattern (with a heel) for brick work; Philadelphia pattern (no heel) for block work.

**Pointing trowel:** A smaller, narrow trowel for detail work and tooling joints in tight spaces.

**Level:** 4-foot level for checking courses level; torpedo level for checking individual units plumb.

**Mason's line (string line) and blocks or pins:** A string stretched between corner leads or line blocks to guide the height and alignment of each course. Every unit in the course should align with the string on its face and top.

**Story pole:** A straight 2x4 with course heights marked on it at the correct spacing. Use it to check that courses are rising at the correct rate. If you're building an 8" high block wall, each course marks 8" (unit height + mortar joint).

**Jointing tools:** Round or V-shaped jointers for tooling horizontal joints. A sled jointer runs along the bed joints in one continuous motion; a smaller jointer does the head joints.

**Brick hammer and chisel:** For cutting brick. A brick set (wide cold chisel) or bolster is the traditional method. Score around all four faces, then strike to split.

**Angle grinder or masonry saw:** For precise cuts and curves. A 4-1/2" angle grinder with a segmented diamond blade handles most residential cutting. A wet masonry saw (miter-saw style with a water-cooled blade) makes cleaner cuts.

## The First Course

The first course is the most important — it determines alignment, level, and bond pattern for everything above. Take more time here.

**Clean the footing.** Sweep and dampen the concrete footing. Dry concrete sucks moisture from the mortar before it bonds properly.

**Snap a chalk line** for the face of the wall. This is your alignment reference for the first course and any brick that touches the footing.

**Set the corners first.** Lay mortar at each end of the wall. Set the corner units (or lead units) in position. Level in all directions — fore-aft, side-to-side. Plumb the face of the unit. Align with the chalk line.

**Stretch a line** between the corner units at the top of the first course. Every unit in between should just kiss the line on its face and top.

**Lay the remaining units.** Throw a mortar bed, butter the head joint on each unit, press it into place, check alignment with the string, and tap to adjust. Work from both ends toward the middle.

**The closure unit:** The last unit to fill the gap in the middle. Butter both ends of the closure unit and both adjacent units, then slide it in. Mortar must fill both head joints. If mortar falls out, pack it back in from the face.

**Check the first course for:**
- Level along the length of the wall
- Consistent joint width (typically 3/8" for brick, 3/8"-1/2" for block)
- Proper alignment to the chalk line
- Bond pattern beginning correctly

## Building Up Courses

### Maintaining Level

A 4-foot level checks each unit for level (left-right tilt) and every few units to confirm the course is running level cumulatively. Small errors in individual units compound over many courses — check often.

**Adjusting height:** If a unit is too high, press it down and remove mortar. Too low, lift it out and add more mortar to the bed. A unit that's already partially set shouldn't be moved — mortar that's disturbed after it begins to bond won't bond again. Remove the unit, clean off the mortar, apply fresh mortar, reset.

### Maintaining Plumb

Every few courses, check the wall face for plumb. Hold the level vertically against the face. The bubble must be centered.

A wall that's plumb at the base can still lean at the top if you're not checking plumb regularly. Plumb is about the vertical relationship between the bottom course and the top course — not just individual units.

### The Mason's Line

Tie a mason's line to corner pins or line blocks. The line is positioned so it runs along the face of the wall and at the top of the current course. Every unit in the course should be:
- Face flush with the string (don't let units push the string out — maintain 1/16" clearance)
- Top aligned with the string

**Why clearance matters:** If units touch the string and push it out, you're actually building a curved wall that appears straight when looking along the string. Keep the face of each unit just barely clear of the string.

Move the line up as you complete each course.

### Running Bond Offset

In running bond, each course offsets by half a unit. On a standard brick, that's 3-3/4" offset. Every other course starts with a half brick at each end (cut units), or the bond reverses for every other course with a full brick at the end.

**Maintain the offset.** If your offset drifts — 3/8" on one course, 5/8" on another — the wall will look off-pattern. Use a consistent reference mark to verify the bond alignment every few courses.

## Laying Concrete Block

Concrete masonry unit (CMU) work follows the same principles as brick but at larger scale. Standard block is 8"×8"×16" (nominal — actual 7-5/8"×7-5/8"×15-5/8") in the US.

**Block is heavy.** An 8" block weighs 35-45 lbs. Use proper lifting technique and work with a partner for long runs or tall walls.

**Mortar placement:** For hollow CMU, mortar is placed only on the face shells (the two face walls of the block) — not across the webs. The mortar "shell bedding" leaves the web areas open for grouting reinforcement. For the first course on the footing, full bedding (mortar across the full block bottom face) is used.

**Vertical reinforcement:** For structural block walls, a rebar is placed vertically in the hollow cores at regular intervals (typically every 32" or 48" on center, per the engineer's specification). The cores are then filled with grout (a high-slump concrete mix sized to flow around the rebar). Grouting done in lifts (maximum 5 feet per lift to prevent the block from blowing out from hydrostatic pressure).

**Horizontal reinforcement:** Ladder or truss wire (joint reinforcement) is placed in mortar bed joints at every other course (16" intervals for 8" block) or per specification. This provides horizontal steel and ties the wythes together in multi-wythe walls.

**Control joints:** Similar to concrete slabs, block walls need control joints to control cracking from thermal movement and shrinkage. Typically every 20 feet maximum, and at changes in wall height or thickness.

## Cutting Masonry Units

**Score and break (brick):** Mark the cut line on all four faces with a pencil. Set the brick on a firm surface. Place a brick set (bolster chisel) on the score line and strike sharply with a heavy hammer. The brick should split cleanly. With practice, this is fast and produces reasonable cuts.

**Angle grinder with diamond blade:** For more precise cuts or partial cuts (notches for pipes, electrical boxes). Use a segmented diamond blade. Cut dry or with water for a cleaner cut and blade life. Always wear eye protection and a dust mask — cutting brick and block generates significant silica dust. (Silica dust is a serious long-term health hazard — repeated exposure causes silicosis, an irreversible lung disease. Wet cutting or a respirator rated for silica (N95 minimum, P100 preferred) is not optional.)

**Masonry saw (tub saw):** A stationary wet saw with a continuous-rim diamond blade, used for precise cuts with consistent dimensions. Produces very clean cuts with minimal chipping. Standard for tile work and finish masonry.

## Common Problems

**Efflorescence:** White salt deposits on the face of masonry. Soluble salts carried to the surface by water. Causes: excessive water in the mortar, water entering through the wall (missing flashing, no weep holes), or salts in the units. Remove with a dilute muriatic acid solution (1:10 acid:water), scrub, rinse thoroughly. Prevent by proper detailing of flashings, weep holes, and drainage.

**Brick spalling:** The face of the brick pops off or flakes. Caused by freeze-thaw damage — water infiltrates the brick, freezes, and expands. Choose brick rated for your climate (SW — Severe Weathering — in freeze-thaw zones). Repoint deteriorated joints to stop water entry.

**Stair-step cracks:** Cracks running diagonally through mortar joints in a stair-step pattern. Caused by differential settlement — one part of the foundation settling more than another. A structural concern that requires a foundation engineer assessment, not just repointing.

**Horizontal cracks in block walls:** Often indicate excessive lateral (soil) pressure on a retaining or below-grade wall. Serious structural issue. Get a structural engineer involved.
