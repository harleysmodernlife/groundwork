## What Makes a Contract

A contract is an agreement the law will enforce. Not every agreement is a contract. Understanding what separates an enforceable contract from an unenforceable promise is the foundation of contract law.

---

## The Four Required Elements

For a contract to be enforceable, it needs four things:

**1. Offer**

One party proposes clear terms. "I will paint your house for $2,000" is an offer. "I might be able to help you with your house" is not — it's too vague to be an offer. The offer has to be specific enough that accepting it creates a clear agreement.

**2. Acceptance**

The other party agrees to the exact terms of the offer. Acceptance must match the offer. If someone says "I'll paint your house for $2,000" and you say "I'll pay you $1,800," that's not acceptance — it's a counteroffer, which rejects the original offer.

Acceptance can be verbal, written, or sometimes through action (starting the work when you knew an offer was on the table).

**3. Consideration**

Each party has to give something. Consideration is what each side is exchanging — a promise for a promise, or a promise for an action. "I'll pay you $2,000 if you paint my house." Money is consideration. The painting is consideration.

A promise to give someone a gift is generally not enforceable — there's no consideration from the recipient. "I promise to give you $500 because I like you" isn't a contract. There's nothing the recipient is doing or giving in return.

**4. Mutual Assent**

Both parties must genuinely agree. Sometimes called "meeting of the minds." If one party was deceived, coerced, or fundamentally misunderstood what they were agreeing to, there may not be true mutual assent — and the contract may be voidable.

---

## Additional Requirements

Beyond the four elements, some contracts are only enforceable if they're in writing (the Statute of Frauds). These include:
- Contracts for the sale of real estate
- Contracts that can't be completed within one year
- Contracts for the sale of goods over $500 (under the Uniform Commercial Code)
- Contracts to pay someone else's debt

If you're in one of these categories, a handshake deal isn't legally reliable — even if both parties remember agreeing to it.

---

## Capacity and Legality

**Capacity:** Both parties must have legal capacity to contract. Minors (under 18 in most states) generally can't be bound by contracts — they can void most contracts they enter. People who are severely mentally incapacitated may lack capacity. Contracts made while intoxicated can sometimes be voided.

**Legality:** The contract's purpose must be legal. A contract to pay someone to commit a crime is unenforceable. Contracts that violate public policy can be void.

---

## What Contracts Actually Do

A contract gives each party a legal remedy if the other side doesn't perform. If you pay a contractor $2,000 and they don't paint your house, you can sue for breach of contract. The court can order the contractor to pay you back or pay you for damages you suffered.

Without a contract — or with a contract that doesn't clearly establish what was promised — that legal remedy gets much harder to exercise.

The goal of a contract isn't to describe a dispute. It's to prevent one by creating shared, documented expectations.
