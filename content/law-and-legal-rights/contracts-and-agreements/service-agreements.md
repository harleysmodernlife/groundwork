## Service Agreements

A service agreement is a contract where one party agrees to perform services for another in exchange for payment. Hiring a plumber, signing with a web designer, retaining a cleaning service, engaging a consultant — these are all service relationships that benefit from a written agreement.

Service agreements protect both sides. The customer knows what they're paying for. The provider knows what they're expected to deliver and when they'll get paid.

---

## What Should Be in a Service Agreement

**Parties:** Full legal names (or business names) of everyone involved.

**Scope of services:** The most critical section. What exactly will be done? The more specific, the better. "Website development" invites disputes. "Ten-page website including homepage, about page, five service pages, contact form, and mobile responsiveness, built in WordPress, delivered by March 15" does not.

**Deliverables:** Specific outputs the provider will produce. List them.

**Timeline:** When work starts, key milestones, and completion date. What happens if the timeline slips? Is there a penalty? Is there a provision for extensions?

**Payment terms:** How much, when it's due, and how it's paid. Common structures:
- Fixed price (all or nothing, or milestone-based)
- Hourly rate with a cap or estimate
- Retainer (monthly fee for a defined level of service)

Also: what happens if payment is late? Interest? Service termination?

**Change orders:** What happens when the client asks for work outside the original scope? Ideally, the contract specifies that scope changes require a written change order with an agreed additional price before work proceeds.

**Ownership of work product:** Who owns what's created? In most service relationships, the client assumes they own what they paid for. But without a written assignment of intellectual property, the provider may retain rights to their work. Freelancers and contractors especially — clarify this.

**Confidentiality:** If the provider will have access to sensitive business information, an NDA or confidentiality provision protects the client.

**Termination:** How can either party end the agreement? With how much notice? What's owed if it's terminated early? Does the client owe for work completed to that point?

**Dispute resolution:** How will disagreements be handled?

---

## From the Client's Perspective

What you want the agreement to guarantee:
- Clear description of what you're getting
- A timeline with consequences if missed
- Payment triggered by completion, not just by time passing
- Ownership of the work product
- Right to terminate if quality isn't acceptable

Watch for: vague deliverables ("consulting services as needed"), payment due upfront with no milestones, no acceptance criteria, no termination provision.

---

## From the Provider's Perspective

What you want the agreement to guarantee:
- Scope is locked — changes require approval and additional payment
- Payment is due on a clear schedule, not dependent on subjective client approval
- Work is accepted in a defined timeframe — clients can't sit on a completed project for months then complain
- You're not liable for client-caused delays
- You own your tools, methodologies, and pre-existing intellectual property

Watch for: open-ended scope, payment due "on final approval" with no defined acceptance criteria, broad liability for downstream problems.

---

## Small Business Reality

Many small service transactions happen on a handshake. For small, one-time jobs this may be fine. But for anything involving:
- More than a few hundred dollars
- Work over multiple weeks or months
- A customer you've never worked with
- Work where misunderstandings about scope are likely

— a written service agreement prevents costly disputes.

A simple one-page agreement is better than nothing. Email confirmation of terms is better than a pure handshake. A professional contract protects both parties and signals that you operate professionally.

## When Things Go Wrong

If a service provider doesn't deliver:
1. Document everything — what was promised, what was delivered, when
2. Put your concerns in writing (email is fine) to create a record
3. Give them a reasonable opportunity to fix it if the issue is fixable
4. If you can't resolve it directly, your options include small claims court (for smaller amounts), mediation, or hiring an attorney

If a client doesn't pay:
1. Send a written demand for payment with a deadline
2. Small claims court is designed for exactly this situation — no attorney needed for most cases
3. Mechanics' lien laws protect certain contractors and tradespeople — if you've done work on real property, look into whether a lien is available in your state
