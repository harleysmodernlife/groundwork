## Verbal vs. Written Contracts

Verbal contracts are real. In most situations, a spoken agreement that meets the four elements of a contract is legally enforceable. But "legally enforceable" and "practically enforceable" are different things.

---

## Verbal Contracts: Legal but Risky

A verbal contract is binding when it has offer, acceptance, consideration, and mutual assent — same as any other contract. If you hire someone to mow your lawn for $50 and they do it, you owe them $50. If you pay in advance and they don't show up, you can sue.

The problem is proof.

In a dispute, you need to establish what the agreement was. With a verbal contract, you're asking a court to figure out what two people said to each other — possibly months ago, when both parties remember it differently, and neither has documentation.

"He said he'd finish by Friday." "She agreed to the lower price." "They promised to include cleanup." These disputes are nearly impossible to resolve fairly when there's no record.

---

## Why Written Contracts Win

A written contract creates a record at the time of agreement, when both parties are on the same page. It:

- Establishes exactly what was promised
- Sets deadlines, payment terms, and conditions clearly
- Forces both parties to think through the details before problems arise
- Gives a judge something to interpret if there's a dispute

When a written contract says "completion by March 15" there's no argument about what was agreed. When it's verbal, you have two people under oath giving different accounts.

---

## The Statute of Frauds: When Writing Is Required

Some contracts are only enforceable in writing. You can't sue to enforce them based on verbal agreement alone, no matter how clearly both parties remember what was said.

These include:
- Real estate sales or transfers
- Leases longer than one year
- Contracts that won't be completed within one year from signing
- Contracts to pay someone else's debt (a guarantee)
- Sale of goods over $500 (in most states)
- Marriage contracts (prenuptial agreements)

If you're in one of these categories and you don't have it in writing, you may be stuck regardless of what was agreed.

---

## Practical Rules

**Get it in writing whenever:**
- Significant money is involved
- Work will happen over time
- The relationship matters (a friend or family member you don't want to fight with)
- There are specific conditions, deadlines, or deliverables
- One of the Statute of Frauds categories applies

**Verbal is probably fine for:**
- Small, immediate transactions ("$10 for this item, here you go")
- Ongoing relationships where trust is established and stakes are low

**For everything in between:** A simple email confirming what you agreed to creates a written record even if it's not a formal contract. "Just following up on our conversation — you'll complete the work by the 20th for $800, right?" A reply confirming those terms is evidence.

---

## After the Fact

If you had a verbal agreement and something went wrong, write down everything you remember immediately. Date, time, who was there, what was said. Text messages, emails, or other written communications that reference the agreement are valuable evidence. Witnesses who heard the agreement are relevant.

You may still win in court with strong evidence even without a written contract. But you've made your case significantly harder than it needed to be.
