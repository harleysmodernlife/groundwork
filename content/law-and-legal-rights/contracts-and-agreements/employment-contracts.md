## Employment Contracts

Most employees never read their employment contract carefully — or sign offer letters without realizing they contain binding terms. Employment agreements can shape your career for years: when you can leave, where you can work next, and who owns what you create.

---

## What an Employment Contract Covers

Employment agreements vary widely. A simple offer letter might just state your title, salary, and start date. A more complete employment contract typically covers:

- Compensation (salary, bonus structure, equity if any)
- Benefits (health insurance, retirement plan, PTO)
- Job title and responsibilities
- At-will status or term of employment
- Termination conditions
- Confidentiality obligations
- Non-compete or non-solicitation agreements
- Intellectual property ownership
- Dispute resolution

Any of these can become significant issues. Know what's in your agreement before you start.

---

## At-Will Employment

Most U.S. employment is "at-will," meaning either party can end the relationship at any time for any reason (or no reason), as long as it's not an illegal reason. Even with an employment contract, you may be at-will unless the contract specifies otherwise.

What at-will doesn't mean: the employer can fire you for illegal reasons — discrimination based on protected characteristics, retaliation for protected activity (like whistleblowing or filing a workers' comp claim), or violations of other employment laws.

Some states have additional protections that narrow at-will employment. Montana, for example, requires good cause for termination after a probationary period.

## Non-Compete Agreements

A non-compete clause says you won't work for a competitor (or start a competing business) for a certain period after leaving. These are among the most important clauses to understand before signing.

**Enforceability varies enormously by state.** California essentially bans non-competes — they're unenforceable against most employees. Other states enforce them if they're "reasonable" in scope, duration, and geographic area. Some states require separate compensation for signing a non-compete.

**What to look at:**
- Duration (6 months? 1 year? 2 years?)
- Geographic scope (your city? your state? nationwide?)
- Definition of "competitor" — is it narrow (your specific employer's direct competitors) or broad (anyone in the same industry)?

A non-compete that prevents you from working in your field nationwide for two years is not the same as one preventing you from working for three named direct competitors for six months. These are functionally different agreements.

**Try to negotiate:** Ask to narrow the scope, shorten the duration, or limit it to specific named competitors. Or ask to remove it entirely — some employers will.

---

## Non-Solicitation Agreements

Different from non-competes: a non-solicitation clause prevents you from recruiting your former colleagues or soliciting your former clients/customers after you leave. These are more commonly enforced than broad non-competes, and harder to argue are unreasonable.

Know who you can and can't contact when you leave.

---

## Intellectual Property Clauses

Most employment contracts include a clause assigning intellectual property created during your employment to the employer. Anything you create using company time or resources belongs to them.

Watch for clauses that go further: some claim ownership of everything you create during employment, even on your own time, even if unrelated to your job. Some states limit how broad these clauses can be.

If you have a side project, startup, or invention you're working on before you start a job, document it carefully. Some contracts allow you to carve out pre-existing work — ask specifically about this before signing.

---

## Confidentiality and NDAs

Non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) are standard and usually reasonable — don't share proprietary business information with competitors or the public. But watch for:

- Overly broad definitions of "confidential information" that cover essentially everything
- Provisions that survive employment for very long periods (or forever)
- Clauses that prevent you from discussing your own compensation with coworkers (this is generally illegal under the National Labor Relations Act)
- Provisions that could be read to prevent you from discussing workplace harassment or discrimination (also legally constrained in many states)

---

## Severance

Most employment contracts don't guarantee severance. It's often offered at the employer's discretion when they lay people off. But some executive contracts and some company policies do establish severance terms.

If severance is offered when you're terminated, it almost always comes with a separation agreement you must sign to receive it. That agreement typically releases all claims against the employer. Don't sign a separation agreement without reading it carefully — and for significant amounts, consider having an attorney review it.

---

## What to Do Before Signing

1. Read the entire document, not just the offer letter summary
2. Look specifically for: non-compete, non-solicitation, IP assignment, confidentiality, dispute resolution
3. Ask questions about anything unclear
4. If you have significant leverage (a competing offer, specialized skills), negotiate on terms that matter to you
5. For executive-level or equity-heavy agreements, an employment attorney's review is worth the cost
