## When Contracts Are Void or Voidable

Not all contracts are enforceable. Some are void — meaning they have no legal effect at all, as if they never existed. Others are voidable — valid unless one party chooses to cancel them. Knowing the difference matters when you're deciding whether an agreement is binding.

---

## Void Contracts

A void contract is legally dead from the start. No one can enforce it, and it creates no rights or obligations.

**Illegal purpose:** A contract to do something illegal is void. "Pay me $500 to break someone's windows" — that's a contract for criminal damage, and courts won't enforce it. If you pay upfront and the person doesn't do the illegal thing, you can't sue to get your money back. You entered into an illegal agreement.

**Fundamental impossibility:** A contract to do something genuinely impossible is generally void. (If the subject matter doesn't exist and both parties were mistaken about it, the contract may be void.)

**Violation of public policy:** Some contracts aren't technically illegal but courts refuse to enforce them because doing so would harm public interests. Non-compete agreements, for example, are often unenforceable if they're too broad — courts won't enforce a clause saying you can never work in your industry again anywhere in the country.

---

## Voidable Contracts

A voidable contract is valid and enforceable — unless the party who has grounds to void it chooses to cancel it. That party has the option, not an obligation.

**Minor's contracts:** In most states, a contract signed by someone under 18 is voidable by the minor. The minor can choose to honor it or cancel it. The adult party doesn't get the same option. (Some exceptions: contracts for necessities like food and shelter may be enforceable against minors.)

**Lack of mental capacity:** If a person lacked the mental capacity to understand what they were agreeing to — due to mental illness, dementia, or severe intoxication — the contract may be voidable by that person or their representative.

**Fraud:** If one party lied about material facts to induce the other to sign, the defrauded party can void the contract. You don't have to honor an agreement you entered based on lies.

**Duress:** A contract signed under threat or coercion is voidable. "Sign this or I'll hurt you" — the person who signed under threat can cancel the contract.

**Undue influence:** If one party used a position of power or trust to improperly pressure the other into an agreement, the influenced party may void it. Common in cases involving elderly people pressured by caregivers.

**Mistake:** In narrow circumstances, a mutual mistake about a material fact (both parties were wrong about something fundamental) can make a contract voidable.

---

## The Difference in Practice

**Void:** Neither party can enforce it. It doesn't matter if both parties wanted to honor it. Courts won't help either side.

**Voidable:** The contract is valid until the protected party decides to cancel. If they choose not to cancel — or if they wait too long and "ratify" the contract by continuing to perform — it becomes fully enforceable.

If a 17-year-old signs a lease and then turns 18, they might be considered to have ratified it by continuing to pay rent as an adult. The window to void it may have closed.

---

## What to Do If You Think a Contract Is Void or Voidable

If you're questioning whether you're bound by an agreement:

1. Stop performing if you can safely do so
2. Don't make statements that look like you're accepting the terms
3. Consult an attorney — the legal analysis matters here, and the facts determine which category applies
4. Act quickly — delays can undermine voidable claims

The burden is on you to assert the defect. A court won't automatically void a contract because it technically qualifies — you have to raise the issue.
