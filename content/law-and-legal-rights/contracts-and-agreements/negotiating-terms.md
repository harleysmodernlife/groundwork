## Negotiating Contract Terms

Most people accept contracts as presented. They assume the terms aren't negotiable, or they don't want to seem difficult, or they're embarrassed to ask. This leaves money on the table and rights unprotected.

The reality: many contracts are negotiable, even when they appear to be standard forms. Knowing how to negotiate — and what to ask for — is a practical skill that applies throughout your life.

---

## Start with the Right Assumption

Assume terms can be changed until you're told otherwise. Don't disqualify yourself before trying. The worst they can say is no, and you're no worse off than accepting the original terms.

Some contracts genuinely aren't negotiable — large platforms (app terms of service, standard consumer subscriptions) rarely bend for individual users. But service contracts, employment agreements, leases, contractor agreements, and business contracts often are negotiable.

---

## Read First, Then Identify Your Priorities

Before negotiating, know what you're asking to change. Read the contract and mark:
- Terms you won't accept under any circumstances (dealbreakers)
- Terms you'd like to change but could accept as-is
- Terms you don't understand and need explained

Prioritize. Going into negotiations with 20 changes signals inexperience and strains the relationship. Pick the things that actually matter to you.

---

## How to Ask

Be direct and non-adversarial. You're not accusing anyone of bad faith — you're protecting your interests, which is normal and expected.

**For changes:** "I'd like to change the payment terms to net-30 rather than net-15. Is that something you can work with?"

**For explanations:** "I want to make sure I understand the indemnification clause in section 8. Can you walk me through what that would mean in practice?"

**For removal:** "I'd like to remove the automatic renewal clause. I'd rather opt in each year than have it auto-renew."

Frame requests as questions when possible. "Is there flexibility on X?" is easier to respond to than "I'm not signing unless you change X."

---

## Common Things Worth Negotiating

**Payment terms:** When payment is due, whether a deposit is required, what happens if payment is late.

**Scope of work:** Adding specifics that are currently vague. "Website redesign" becomes "ten-page website with specific features, delivered by a specific date."

**Deadlines and timelines:** Can the deadline move? What happens if it's missed?

**Termination notice:** If you need to cancel, how much notice is required? Can you reduce it?

**Automatic renewal:** Ask to remove it or change it to opt-in renewal.

**Arbitration clauses:** In consumer contracts, these are often non-negotiable. In business contracts, sometimes you can negotiate mutual arbitration terms or remove them entirely.

**Limitation of liability:** If you're the customer, you want to know their liability isn't capped at a number that doesn't cover your actual exposure.

**Personal guarantee:** If you're a business owner signing on behalf of your company, try to avoid personally guaranteeing the contract (making yourself personally liable if the company can't pay).

---

## What to Do When They Say No

If a term isn't negotiable, you have to decide: is this a dealbreaker, or can you live with it?

Before walking away from a good deal over one bad term, consider:
- How likely is it that this provision will actually affect you?
- What's the practical worst-case if it does?
- Is the deal good enough overall that accepting this risk is worth it?

Sometimes the answer is no — a contract that buries huge liability on you for a routine service isn't worth it. Sometimes it's yes — a minor limitation that probably won't apply is not worth losing a good opportunity over.

---

## Get Changes in Writing

Whatever you negotiate, get it documented in the contract itself — not in an email, not in a verbal promise.

"We agreed we can cancel with 30 days notice" is worthless if the written contract says 90 days and the other party later denies the agreement. Every change to the written contract should appear in writing, either in the contract document itself or in a signed amendment.

Cross out and initial changes directly on paper contracts. Use tracked changes or version control on digital contracts. Make sure both parties have the same final version.

---

## A Note on Power Dynamics

Negotiating leverage depends on what each party has to offer. If they need your business or your skills more than you need the deal, you have more leverage. If you're one of many applicants for a job or vendors for a contract, you have less.

Know your leverage. Use it when you have it. Don't overplay it when you don't.

Negotiating once with good results builds confidence for the next time. Most people who start negotiating contracts find that it works far more often than they expected.
