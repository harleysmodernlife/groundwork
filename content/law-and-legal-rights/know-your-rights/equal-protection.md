## Equal Protection Under the Law

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment says: "No State shall... deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws."

It's one sentence that has driven some of the most consequential legal battles in American history — from school desegregation to same-sex marriage.

---

## What It Means

The government must treat similarly situated people equally. Laws can make distinctions between groups of people, but those distinctions must meet certain legal standards depending on how fundamental the distinction is.

**The critical point:** Not all unequal treatment is unconstitutional. The question is what level of justification the government needs to make a distinction.

---

## The Three Levels of Scrutiny

Courts apply different levels of scrutiny to different types of classifications:

**Rational Basis Review (lowest standard):**

Applies to most laws and government action. The law must have a rational relationship to a legitimate government interest. This is an easy test for the government to meet — almost any plausible justification will do.

Example: A law requiring drivers to be at least 16. Some 15-year-olds might be better drivers than some 30-year-olds, but the age distinction has a rational basis. The law stands.

**Intermediate Scrutiny:**

Applies to classifications based on sex and some other characteristics (like legitimacy of birth). The law must be substantially related to an important government interest.

This is more demanding. The government has to show a closer fit between the distinction it's making and the goal it's pursuing.

Example: The government can exclude women from combat roles only if it can show the exclusion is substantially related to military effectiveness. Courts have narrowed the acceptable exclusions significantly over time.

**Strict Scrutiny (highest standard):**

Applies to "suspect classifications" — race, national origin — and fundamental rights (voting, travel, marriage). The law must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest.

This is an extremely demanding standard. Very few laws survive strict scrutiny. The government has to show not just that the goal is important, but that this particular distinction is the only way to achieve it.

Example: Racial segregation in public schools couldn't survive strict scrutiny — there's no compelling government interest in separating children by race. *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954).

---

## Landmark Equal Protection Cases

**Brown v. Board of Education (1954):** Racial segregation in public schools violates equal protection. Overturned *Plessy v. Ferguson*'s "separate but equal" doctrine.

**Loving v. Virginia (1967):** Laws prohibiting interracial marriage violate equal protection (and due process).

**Reed v. Reed (1971):** The first Supreme Court case striking down a sex-based classification under equal protection — Idaho's rule preferring men over women as estate administrators had no rational basis.

**United States v. Virginia (1996):** Virginia Military Institute's all-male admissions policy violated equal protection — the state's justification didn't survive intermediate scrutiny.

**Obergefell v. Hodges (2015):** Laws excluding same-sex couples from marriage violated equal protection (and due process).

---

## What Equal Protection Does and Doesn't Cover

**What it covers:**
- Government discrimination based on race, sex, national origin, religion, and other protected characteristics
- Laws that treat similarly situated people differently without adequate justification
- Government action in any context — criminal justice, education, public services, voting

**What it doesn't cover:**
- Private discrimination (that's addressed by civil rights statutes, not the Constitution directly)
- All differences in outcomes — unequal *impact* is not the same as unequal *treatment* under the law without discriminatory intent
- Discrimination by private employers (Title VII and other laws address this separately)

---

## Discrimination by Private Actors

The Equal Protection Clause limits government action. But Congress has passed statutes that prohibit private discrimination in many contexts:

- **Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964):** Prohibits employment discrimination based on race, color, sex, national origin, and religion.
- **Fair Housing Act:** Prohibits housing discrimination based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability.
- **Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA):** Prohibits discrimination against people with disabilities in employment and public accommodations.

These statutes have their own enforcement mechanisms and don't require you to prove a constitutional violation — just a violation of the statute.
