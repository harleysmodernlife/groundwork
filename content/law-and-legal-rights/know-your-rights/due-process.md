## What Due Process Actually Means

Due process is one of the most important concepts in American law — and one of the most misused phrases in casual conversation. People say "that's not due process" about situations that have nothing to do with it, and miss genuine due process violations because they don't know what to look for.

---

## The Constitutional Basis

Due process appears twice in the Constitution:

**Fifth Amendment:** "No person shall... be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." (Applies to the federal government.)

**Fourteenth Amendment:** "...nor shall any State deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." (Applies to state governments.)

Together, they establish that before the government takes something fundamental from you — your life, your freedom, your property — it must follow a process that's fair and legally established.

---

## Two Types of Due Process

**Procedural due process:** The government must follow fair procedures before depriving you of life, liberty, or property.

The basic requirements: notice (you must be informed of what's happening and why) and a hearing (you must have a meaningful opportunity to be heard and contest the government's action).

What "fair procedure" means varies by context. For a criminal conviction that results in imprisonment, you're entitled to an elaborate set of procedural protections — jury trial, right to confront witnesses, right to counsel, proof beyond a reasonable doubt. For a welfare benefit termination, the process is less formal — but some notice and opportunity to respond is still required.

**Substantive due process:** Some rights are so fundamental that the government cannot infringe them regardless of what procedures it follows.

This is more controversial and has been interpreted to protect rights not explicitly listed in the Constitution — privacy, marriage, contraception, intimate relationships. *Roe v. Wade* was based on substantive due process (the right to privacy). So was *Obergefell v. Hodges* (the right to same-sex marriage) and *Griswold v. Connecticut* (the right to contraception). These rights can expand or contract based on Supreme Court interpretation.

---

## Due Process in the Criminal System

The criminal process is where due process protections are most robust:

- **Notice:** You must be formally charged and told what crime you're accused of
- **Speedy trial:** You can't be held indefinitely without being brought to trial
- **Neutral decision-maker:** The judge (or jury) cannot have a personal stake in the outcome
- **Right to be heard:** You have the right to present your defense
- **Right to confront witnesses:** You can cross-examine the people testifying against you
- **Standard of proof:** The government must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt
- **Right to counsel:** You have the right to an attorney

---

## Due Process Outside Criminal Court

**Administrative hearings:** Government agencies that revoke licenses, terminate benefits, or impose fines must provide some form of due process. The amount of process required scales with what's at stake — terminating Social Security benefits requires more process than denying a parking variance.

**Public school discipline:** Students facing suspension have due process rights — at minimum, notice of the charges and an opportunity to respond, per *Goss v. Lopez* (1975). Longer suspensions or expulsions require more process.

**Government employment:** Public employees with a "legitimate expectation of continued employment" (usually established by contract, tenure, or civil service rules) have due process rights before termination — typically notice and a hearing. At-will public employees have fewer protections.

**Civil asset forfeiture:** When the government seizes property allegedly connected to a crime, due process requires some procedure for the owner to contest the seizure. In practice, civil forfeiture procedures have been criticized as inadequate, and many states have reformed them.

---

## What Due Process Does NOT Cover

Due process is a limit on **government** action. Private actors — employers, landlords, universities, companies — are not subject to constitutional due process requirements. A private employer can fire you without notice. A private university can discipline students under its own procedures (though may be bound by contract/handbook). A private company can terminate services without a hearing.

When people say "that's not due process" about a private company's decision, they're usually misapplying the concept. Constitutional due process only constrains governments.
