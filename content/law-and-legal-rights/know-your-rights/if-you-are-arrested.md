## If You Are Arrested: Step by Step

An arrest is one of the most stressful things a person can go through. Your reactions in the first moments — what you say, how you behave — can significantly affect what happens next. Knowing the process before it happens is the best preparation.

---

## The Moment of Arrest

An arrest occurs when police take you into custody — when you're no longer free to leave and they're exercising control over your person.

**What to do:**
- Do not physically resist, even if you believe the arrest is unlawful. Resisting arrest is a separate criminal charge and can escalate physical danger. The place to challenge an unlawful arrest is in court.
- Clearly state: "I am invoking my right to remain silent. I want to speak with an attorney."
- Then stop talking about anything related to the alleged crime. Completely.

**What not to do:**
- Argue about whether the arrest is legal
- Explain yourself or tell your side of the story
- Try to convince the officer you didn't do it
- Answer questions without an attorney present

People genuinely believe that if they just explain things, the situation will be resolved. This almost never works. Statements made at arrest are recorded and used in court. Explanations become inconsistencies. "I was there but I didn't do anything" is now you placing yourself at the scene.

---

## Booking and Processing

After arrest, you'll be taken to a police station or jail for booking:

- **Identification:** You'll be photographed and fingerprinted.
- **Personal property:** Your belongings will be inventoried and held.
- **Health:** Any medical needs should be disclosed.
- **Phone call:** You typically have the right to at least one phone call. In practice, you may get more. Use it to call an attorney or a trusted person who can find one.

During booking, officers may attempt further questioning. Repeat your invocation: "I am exercising my right to remain silent. I want an attorney." You don't have to answer anything.

---

## Arraignment and Bail

Within a set time period (usually 24-72 hours for most charges), you'll have an arraignment — a court appearance where:
- You're formally told the charges against you
- You enter a plea (almost always "not guilty" at this stage regardless of the facts — your attorney will advise you)
- Bail is determined

**Bail** is money paid to the court to secure your release pending trial. The court holds it as assurance you'll appear; you get it back when the case concludes if you appear.

If you can't afford bail, you may stay in custody until trial — which can be weeks or months. A bail bondsman will post bail for a fee (typically 10% of the bail amount, non-refundable) if you qualify.

Many jurisdictions also use **released on recognizance (ROR)** — release without bail based on your promise to appear, used for lower-risk defendants.

---

## Getting an Attorney

**This is the most important step.**

If you cannot afford an attorney, the Sixth Amendment guarantees that one will be appointed for you. At your arraignment, if you don't have an attorney, request the public defender's office.

Public defenders are real attorneys with law degrees and bar licenses. Many are experienced and committed. They're also often overworked — caseloads can be very high. Being cooperative, organized, and communicative with your public defender matters.

If you can afford a private attorney, hire one who specializes in criminal defense in your jurisdiction. Criminal law varies by state and county, and local knowledge matters.

**Do not talk to police before speaking with your attorney.** Even after invoking the right to counsel, police may continue trying to engage with you. Politely and consistently refuse: "I would like to speak with my attorney before answering any questions."

---

## While You're in Custody

- Assume all conversations are monitored. In jail, they are. Don't discuss your case with cellmates, on the phone, or in person visits without your attorney.
- Don't accept a deal or sign anything without your attorney reviewing it.
- If you're mistreated physically, inform your attorney immediately and document everything you can.

---

## What Comes Next

The criminal process after arrest typically involves:
- Preliminary hearing or grand jury (depending on the charge and state)
- Pre-trial motions (your attorney may challenge evidence, the arrest, or the search)
- Negotiation over possible plea deals
- Trial if no deal is reached
- Sentencing if convicted

Most criminal cases resolve through plea deals, not trials. Your attorney will advise you on whether a deal makes sense given the evidence, the charges, and the likely outcome of trial.

The single most valuable thing you can do after an arrest: stay silent, get an attorney, and follow their advice.
