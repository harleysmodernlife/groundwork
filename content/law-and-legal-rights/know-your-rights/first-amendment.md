## Free Speech: What It Does and Doesn't Protect

The First Amendment is both the most celebrated and most misunderstood right in the Constitution. People invoke it constantly in situations where it doesn't apply — and are sometimes unaware of it in situations where it does.

---

## What the First Amendment Actually Says

The government cannot:
- Establish an official religion or favor one religion over others
- Prevent you from practicing your religion (with limits)
- Punish you for your speech
- Censor the press
- Break up peaceful assemblies
- Prevent you from petitioning the government

---

## The Critical Limitation: Government Action Only

The First Amendment is a limit on the **government**. It does not apply to:

**Private employers.** Your boss can fire you for things you say at work, on social media, or in public. Nothing about this violates the First Amendment — your employer isn't the government.

**Private platforms.** Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, and any other private company can moderate, ban, or remove content they don't want. They're not the government. No First Amendment issue.

**Private individuals.** A person can call you a liar, kick you out of their house for what you said, or refuse to do business with you because of your views. Private actors aren't bound by the First Amendment.

When does the First Amendment apply? When a government entity — a city, a state agency, a public university, the federal government — is attempting to punish, silence, or restrict your speech.

---

## What Speech Is Protected

The protection is remarkably broad. Protected speech includes:
- Political speech, including speech the majority finds offensive
- Unpopular opinions
- Criticism of public officials
- Symbolic speech (flag burning, for example — confirmed in *Texas v. Johnson*, 1989)
- Artistic and creative expression
- Commercial speech (with some regulation allowed)

---

## What Speech Is NOT Protected

There are recognized categories of speech outside First Amendment protection:

**True threats:** Statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious intent to commit violence. "I will kill you" in a genuine threatening context.

**Incitement to imminent lawless action:** Speech specifically designed to produce immediate illegal action and likely to do so. Not just inflammatory speech — the standard is high.

**Defamation:** False statements of fact presented as true that damage someone's reputation. Libel (written) and slander (spoken). Public figures have a higher bar — they must prove "actual malice" (the speaker knew it was false or was reckless about the truth).

**Obscenity:** Under the *Miller* test, material that: appeals to prurient interest, depicts conduct in a patently offensive way, and lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value.

**Fraud and perjury:** Lying under oath, lying to defraud people.

**"Fighting words":** Words that by their very utterance tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace. A narrow, rarely used category.

**Child pornography:** Not protected.

---

## Public vs Private Employees

Government employees have some First Amendment protections related to their jobs — but they're more limited than civilians. A government employee speaking on a matter of public concern (as a citizen, not in their official role) has some protection. Speech made as part of their official job duties has much less.

A police officer can't be fired for voting for a political candidate. They can be disciplined for insubordination in the line of duty.

---

## Common Misconceptions

**"You can't say that — it's illegal."** In most cases, saying something offensive or even hateful is protected. The U.S. has much broader speech protection than most other democracies.

**"That's hate speech, which isn't protected."** The U.S. does not have a "hate speech" exception to the First Amendment. Offensive, hateful, or bigoted speech is generally protected unless it falls into one of the unprotected categories above.

**"The company banned me — my rights were violated."** Private companies aren't the government. They can set their own content rules.

**"I got fired for what I said online — First Amendment violation."** Almost certainly not, unless you work for a government employer.

Understanding the actual scope of First Amendment protection — what it covers and what it doesn't — is how you know when you have a genuine legal claim and when you don't.
