## Search and Seizure: The Fourth Amendment

The Fourth Amendment protects you from unreasonable government searches and seizures. It's the primary legal barrier between your private life and law enforcement — and it has important limitations and exceptions you need to understand.

---

## The Core Requirement: Warrants

The Fourth Amendment generally requires law enforcement to obtain a **warrant** before searching your home, car, papers, or person.

A warrant must:
- Be issued by a neutral judge or magistrate
- Be supported by **probable cause** — specific facts that would lead a reasonable person to believe a crime has been or is being committed
- Describe with particularity the place to be searched and the things or persons to be seized

A warrant signed by a judge authorizing a search of your home for evidence of a specific crime must say what they're looking for and where they're searching. They can't show up with a warrant for your kitchen and rifle through your bedroom.

---

## What "Reasonable Expectation of Privacy" Means

Courts determine whether the Fourth Amendment applies based on whether you have a **reasonable expectation of privacy** in what's being searched.

- Your home: very strong protection. Police generally cannot enter without a warrant.
- Your car: lower expectation of privacy. More exceptions apply.
- Your phone (content): strong protection — *Riley v. California* (2014) required warrants for phone content.
- Items in plain view: no protection. If a cop can see it from a place they're allowed to be, no search occurs.
- Things you share with others: reduced expectation. What you tell a friend, put in the trash, or share on a public social media profile has less protection.
- Public spaces: generally no expectation of privacy. Your movements in public can be observed and recorded.

---

## Major Exceptions — When Police Don't Need a Warrant

The warrant requirement has significant exceptions. Understanding them is as important as understanding the rule.

**Consent:** If you voluntarily agree to a search, no warrant is needed. You do not have to consent. If you do consent, it's much harder to challenge the search later.

**Search incident to arrest:** When you're lawfully arrested, officers can search your person and the area within your immediate reach. This prevents you from accessing weapons or destroying evidence.

**Automobile exception:** If police have probable cause to believe your car contains contraband or evidence of a crime, they can search it without a warrant. Courts have greatly expanded this exception.

**Exigent circumstances:** Emergency situations where getting a warrant isn't practical — someone is being chased into a building, evidence is about to be destroyed, there's an imminent threat to life.

**Plain view:** Police can seize evidence they see in plain view from a place they're legally allowed to be.

**Hot pursuit:** If police are chasing a suspect, they can follow them into private property.

**Terry stop (stop and frisk):** Police can stop and briefly detain you if they have **reasonable suspicion** (a lower standard than probable cause) that criminal activity is afoot. They can frisk (pat down) your outer clothing for weapons if they believe you may be armed. This is not a full search.

---

## When Fourth Amendment Rights Are Violated

If evidence is obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment, it may be suppressed under the **exclusionary rule** — it can't be used against you at trial.

This is enforced through a motion to suppress evidence, filed by your attorney before trial.

Exceptions to the exclusionary rule exist: good faith (officers relied reasonably on a warrant later found to be deficient), inevitable discovery (evidence would have been found anyway), and attenuation (the connection between the illegal search and the evidence is too remote).

---

## What This Means Practically

- You can decline a search without a warrant. Say clearly: "I do not consent to this search."
- Refusing consent cannot be used as probable cause for a search.
- If you do consent, you've waived your Fourth Amendment rights for that search.
- If police have a warrant, compliance is required — but any illegal aspects of the search can be challenged later.
- Document everything you can: badge numbers, what was said, what was searched. This matters if you challenge the search.

The Fourth Amendment is powerful but porous. Knowing where its protections hold and where they don't helps you make better decisions when law enforcement interacts with you.
