## Civil Law vs. Criminal Law: The Difference

Most people have a vague sense that some legal matters involve jail and others involve lawsuits. That's the core distinction — but understanding the details helps you recognize which system you're dealing with and what the stakes are.

---

## The Basic Split

**Criminal law** involves the government prosecuting someone for behavior that society has decided is harmful enough to criminalize. The case is brought by the state — the prosecutor represents "the people," not a private victim. Punishment can include incarceration, probation, fines paid to the government, and a criminal record.

**Civil law** involves private parties resolving disputes. One person or entity sues another. The court resolves who owes what to whom. Punishment is almost never jail — it's money (damages) or court orders requiring or prohibiting specific actions.

---

## Who Brings the Case

**Criminal:** The government. If someone assaults you, you don't file the criminal charge — the prosecutor decides whether to bring charges. You're a witness or victim, not the party. You can report the crime; you can't control whether charges are filed.

**Civil:** The injured party. If someone breaches a contract, defrauds you, or injures you through negligence, you file the lawsuit. You're the plaintiff. You control whether to sue, settle, or drop the case.

---

## The Standard of Proof

This is the most important practical difference.

**Criminal:** Proof beyond a reasonable doubt. The highest standard in law. The jury must be convinced to a near-certainty. This standard exists because the consequences — loss of liberty — are so severe.

**Civil:** Preponderance of the evidence. More likely than not. More than 50%. The party with the slightly stronger case usually wins.

This is why O.J. Simpson was acquitted criminally (not proven guilty beyond a reasonable doubt) but found liable civilly (more likely than not that he caused the deaths). Same facts, different standards, different outcomes.

---

## Consequences

**Criminal:**
- Incarceration (jail or prison)
- Probation
- Fines paid to the court/government
- Criminal record
- Loss of certain rights (voting in some states, firearms)
- Deportation risk for non-citizens

**Civil:**
- Compensatory damages (money to make the plaintiff whole)
- Punitive damages (extra money to punish egregious conduct)
- Injunctions (court orders to do or stop doing something)
- No jail in most civil cases (contempt of court is an exception)

---

## The Same Act Can Trigger Both

Criminal and civil proceedings are separate. The same conduct can result in both a criminal prosecution and a civil lawsuit.

Example: A drunk driver hits and injures someone. The state prosecutes for DUI (criminal). The injured person sues for medical bills and pain and suffering (civil). These are independent proceedings with different standards and different outcomes.

The criminal case can acquit and the civil case can still find liability — because different standards apply.

---

## Practical Implications

**If you're a victim:** You can report to police (criminal) and consult a civil attorney (civil). You don't have to choose one.

**If you're accused criminally:** You have constitutional protections — right to counsel, right to remain silent, right to jury trial, proof beyond a reasonable doubt. These don't apply in civil cases the same way.

**If you're sued civilly:** You have no right to a court-appointed attorney (the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is criminal only). You need to hire one or represent yourself.

**For most everyday disputes** — a contractor doesn't finish the job, a landlord wrongfully keeps your security deposit, someone owes you money — the civil system (small claims court or regular civil court) is how you get resolution. Police generally won't respond to contract disputes because they're not criminal matters.

## Quick Reference

| | Criminal | Civil |
|---|---|---|
| Who files | Government/prosecutor | Private party (plaintiff) |
| Standard of proof | Beyond reasonable doubt | Preponderance (51%) |
| Primary consequence | Jail, probation, criminal record | Money damages, injunctions |
| Right to appointed attorney | Yes | No |
| Jury trial | Usually yes | Sometimes |
| Can result from same act as civil | Yes | Yes |
