## The Right to Counsel

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that in all criminal prosecutions, the accused has the right to the assistance of counsel. *Gideon v. Wainwright* (1963) extended this to state courts and established that if you can't afford an attorney, the state must appoint one for you.

This is one of the most important constitutional rights. It also, in practice, works imperfectly.

---

## When the Right Attaches

The right to counsel exists at all "critical stages" of a criminal prosecution:

- After formal charges are filed
- At arraignment
- At preliminary hearings
- During any custodial interrogation (the Miranda right to counsel)
- At trial
- At sentencing
- On first appeal of right

**Before formal charges:** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel hasn't attached yet. But Miranda gives you the right to have an attorney present before answering police questions during custodial interrogation. These are related but distinct rights.

---

## Invoking Your Right

You must clearly invoke your right to counsel. You cannot simply remain silent and assume an attorney will appear. You must say it:

**"I want an attorney."** or **"I am invoking my right to counsel."**

Once you invoke clearly, all questioning must stop until an attorney is present (per *Edwards v. Arizona*). Police are not allowed to continue interrogating you or try to get you to change your mind.

Ambiguous requests — "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer" or "Do you think I need a lawyer?" — may not trigger the right under some court decisions. Be clear and unambiguous.

---

## Public Defenders

If you cannot afford an attorney, the court appoints a public defender. Public defenders are:

- Licensed attorneys with law degrees and bar membership
- Often experienced in criminal law — many try far more cases than private attorneys
- Familiar with local courts, judges, and prosecutors in ways that matter

The challenges:
- **Caseload:** Public defenders are chronically underfunded and often carry hundreds of cases at once. The national average exceeds what professional standards recommend for adequate representation.
- **Time:** The caseload reality means less time per case — less investigation, less motion practice, less client contact
- **Resources:** Private defense attorneys can hire investigators and expert witnesses; public defenders often have limited resources for this

The quality of public defense varies enormously by jurisdiction. Some public defender offices are well-funded and provide excellent representation. Others are critically under-resourced.

---

## Getting the Most from Your Public Defender

You cannot choose your public defender, but you can work effectively with them:

- **Communicate early and completely.** Tell your attorney everything relevant. They can't effectively represent you without accurate information about what happened.
- **Be organized.** Bring documents, write down what you remember, keep track of court dates.
- **Ask questions.** If you don't understand what's happening or why, ask. You have the right to understand your case.
- **Be responsive.** Return calls and show up to appointments. Public defenders juggling hundreds of cases will spend their time on clients who respond.
- **Request a meeting before accepting any plea.** Don't accept a deal without discussing it with your attorney.

If you have serious concerns about your representation — your attorney isn't responding, isn't meeting with you, seems unprepared — ask to speak with a supervising attorney in the public defender's office.

---

## Waiving the Right to Counsel

You can represent yourself (*pro se*) by waiving your right to counsel. The court must ensure the waiver is knowing and intelligent — the judge will typically ask whether you understand you're giving up this right and the dangers of self-representation.

Representing yourself in a criminal case is almost always a mistake. The legal system is complex, prosecutors are experienced, and the rules of evidence and procedure take years to learn. Even attorneys in legal trouble typically hire other attorneys to represent them.

---

## Ineffective Assistance of Counsel

Even with an attorney, if representation was so poor that it denied you a fair trial, you may have a constitutional claim. *Strickland v. Washington* (1984) established the standard:

1. The attorney's performance was deficient (fell below an objective standard of reasonableness)
2. The deficient performance prejudiced the outcome (there's a reasonable probability the result would have been different with adequate counsel)

This is a high bar. Many attorney failures don't meet it. But egregious errors — failing to investigate an obvious defense, missing a critical deadline, sleeping through trial — can support a claim.

Ineffective assistance claims are typically raised on appeal or in post-conviction proceedings (habeas corpus), not during the original trial.
