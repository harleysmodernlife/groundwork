## Crimes and Their Classifications

Criminal offenses aren't all treated equally. The law organizes crimes into categories based on severity, which determines how they're prosecuted, what procedures apply, and what punishment is possible. Understanding how crimes are classified helps you understand what's actually at stake in a criminal case.

---

## The Basic Split: Felonies and Misdemeanors

**Felonies:** The most serious crimes. Punishable by more than one year in prison, typically in a state or federal prison (not county jail). Examples: murder, rape, robbery, burglary, arson, grand theft, serious drug offenses.

Felony convictions carry significant collateral consequences beyond the sentence: loss of voting rights in many states, prohibition on firearm possession, difficulty obtaining employment, ineligibility for certain housing and professional licenses, and more.

**Misdemeanors:** Less serious offenses. Punishable by up to one year in jail (county jail, not prison). Examples: simple assault, petty theft, first-offense DUI, disorderly conduct, minor drug possession.

Misdemeanor convictions also have consequences, though generally less severe than felonies. Employment background checks, professional licensing, and housing applications often ask about misdemeanors as well.

**Infractions:** The least serious category. Traffic violations, code violations, and minor regulatory offenses. Typically punishable only by fines. Generally no jail time and no criminal record in the traditional sense. Infractions are usually not considered "criminal" convictions.

---

## Felony Classifications

Most states further divide felonies into classes by severity:

**Class A (or First Degree) Felonies:** The most serious. Murder, rape, kidnapping. Often carry maximum sentences of life in prison or even the death penalty in states that retain it.

**Class B (Second Degree), Class C (Third Degree), etc.:** Less serious felonies with lower sentencing ranges. A Class C felony might carry a maximum of 5-10 years; a Class A might carry 25 to life.

The specific classes and their ranges vary by state. Look up your state's felony classification system if specific details matter.

---

## Misdemeanor Classifications

Similarly, misdemeanors are often classified:

**Class A (or First Class) Misdemeanors:** Up to one year in jail, larger fines. Example: simple assault, first-offense DUI in many states.

**Class B (Second Class), Class C:** Less serious, with smaller fines and shorter potential jail time.

**Petty misdemeanors / violations:** The bottom tier, sometimes treated more like infractions.

---

## State vs. Federal Crimes

Most crimes are prosecuted under state law. Federal crimes involve violations of federal statutes — often involving interstate activity, crimes on federal property, crimes against federal officials, drug trafficking at scale, white-collar fraud, immigration violations, and similar.

State and federal systems are separate. The double jeopardy clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits being tried twice for the same offense, but under the "dual sovereignty" doctrine, being prosecuted by the state doesn't bar a federal prosecution for the same conduct (and vice versa). This is how people can face both state murder charges and federal civil rights charges for the same killing.

Federal sentences are often harsher than state sentences for comparable conduct, and federal prisons are separate from state facilities.

---

## Specific Intent vs. General Intent

Many crimes require proving what the defendant intended:

**Specific intent crimes:** The prosecution must prove the defendant intended not just the act but the specific result. Burglary traditionally requires entering a building with intent to commit a crime inside — the specific intent element.

**General intent crimes:** Only requires that the defendant intended to do the act, not necessarily its consequences. Battery often only requires intent to make contact, not intent to cause harm.

**Strict liability crimes:** No intent required — just doing the act is enough. Traffic violations, statutory rape (in states where it's strict liability), certain regulatory crimes. You can violate these laws accidentally.

Intent matters because it's what prosecutors must prove and what defenses can challenge.

---

## Inchoate Crimes

"Inchoate" means incomplete — crimes that involve planning or attempting to commit a crime, even if the underlying crime wasn't completed.

- **Attempt:** Taking a substantial step toward committing a crime with intent to complete it
- **Conspiracy:** Agreeing with another person to commit a crime, plus at least one overt act in furtherance
- **Solicitation:** Asking or inducing another person to commit a crime

These are separate crimes from the underlying offense, and can be charged even if the planned crime never happened.
