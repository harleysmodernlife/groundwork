## From Arrest to Trial: How Criminal Cases Move

Most people have a vague picture of how criminal cases work, drawn from TV — arrest, courtroom drama, verdict. The reality is more procedural, slower, and more dependent on decisions made long before any trial. Understanding the stages helps you know what's actually happening and when it matters.

---

## Step 1: Arrest

Police arrest you when they have probable cause to believe you've committed a crime. You're taken into custody, transported to a police station, and booked (photographed, fingerprinted, belongings inventoried).

At this point: say nothing beyond providing identification if required. Request an attorney. Then stop talking.

---

## Step 2: Initial Appearance / Bail Hearing

Within 24-72 hours (depending on jurisdiction and charges), you appear before a judge for the first time. The judge:
- Informs you of the charges
- Determines whether there's probable cause to hold you (in some jurisdictions)
- Sets bail or determines whether you're released on your own recognizance (ROR)

At this stage, if you don't have an attorney, you should request a public defender. Don't say anything substantive about the case.

---

## Step 3: Formal Charges

How formal charges are filed depends on the severity of the case and the jurisdiction:

**Information:** For misdemeanors and some felonies, a prosecutor files a document called an information directly with the court.

**Grand jury indictment:** For serious felonies in federal court (constitutionally required) and in many state courts, a grand jury of citizens reviews evidence and decides whether there's probable cause to formally charge. Grand jury proceedings are secret, and only the prosecution presents evidence. Grand juries indict in the vast majority of cases they consider — the standard is low.

---

## Step 4: Arraignment

You appear in court and formally enter a plea to the charges:

- **Not guilty:** The universal initial plea in virtually all cases, regardless of the facts. This preserves all options and starts the process. Your attorney will advise you on this.
- **Guilty:** Admits the charges. Usually happens as part of a plea deal negotiated before arraignment or after.
- **No contest (nolo contendere):** Not admitting guilt but not contesting the charge. Has the same criminal consequence as a guilty plea but can't be used against you in subsequent civil litigation. Only available in some states.

---

## Step 5: Pre-Trial Motions and Discovery

This is often the most consequential phase, though it happens mostly out of public view.

**Discovery:** The prosecution must share evidence they have with the defense. Your attorney reviews police reports, witness statements, forensic evidence, video, and other materials. Brady material — evidence favorable to the defense — must be disclosed. Prosecutors who hide exculpatory evidence commit serious misconduct.

**Pre-trial motions:** Your attorney may file motions to:
- Suppress evidence obtained through illegal searches (Fourth Amendment)
- Dismiss charges for insufficient evidence or procedural violations
- Change venue if a fair trial isn't possible locally
- Suppress statements made in violation of Miranda

A successful suppression motion can gut the prosecution's case. Evidence obtained unconstitutionally can't be used against you, and without key evidence, charges are sometimes dropped.

---

## Step 6: Plea Negotiations

The overwhelming majority of criminal cases — over 90% in federal court, similar in state court — resolve through plea deals, not trial.

A plea deal typically involves pleading guilty to a lesser charge or to the original charge in exchange for a recommended sentence (which the judge may or may not accept). Plea bargaining allows the system to function — trials are expensive and time-consuming, and would overwhelm courts if every case went to trial.

The decision to accept or reject a plea deal is ultimately the defendant's — not the attorney's. Your attorney advises you on the strength of the evidence, the likely outcome at trial, and the risk of a worse sentence if convicted at trial. You decide.

---

## Step 7: Trial

If no plea deal is reached:

**Jury selection:** The process of selecting 12 jurors (for felonies; often fewer for misdemeanors) from a pool. Both sides can strike jurors for cause (specific bias) and use a limited number of peremptory challenges (no reason required, but not based on race or sex).

**Opening statements:** Both sides outline what they expect the evidence to show.

**Prosecution's case:** Witnesses and evidence presented. Your attorney cross-examines each witness.

**Defense's case:** Your attorney may present witnesses and evidence. You have the right not to testify (Fifth Amendment), and the prosecutor cannot comment on that choice.

**Closing arguments:** Each side summarizes the case.

**Jury instructions:** The judge instructs the jury on the law.

**Deliberations and verdict:** The jury deliberates privately. A verdict must be unanimous in most jurisdictions for felonies. Hung jury (unable to agree) results in a mistrial; the prosecution can retry the case.

---

## Step 8: Sentencing

If convicted (by plea or trial), a separate sentencing hearing determines the punishment. The judge considers:
- Sentencing guidelines (advisory ranges based on offense and criminal history)
- Victim impact statements
- Your attorney's arguments for leniency
- The defendant's statement (allocution)
- Pre-sentence investigation report

**Sentencing after trial vs. plea:** Going to trial and losing sometimes results in a harsher sentence than a negotiated plea — the "trial penalty" is real. This is one factor (among many) in the decision about whether to accept a plea.
