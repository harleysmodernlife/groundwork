## Criminal Records: What They Are and What You Can Do

A criminal record follows you. It shows up on background checks, affects employment and housing applications, can restrict professional licenses, and in some states affects voting rights. Understanding what's on your record, who can see it, and whether you can clear it gives you more control over your own situation.

---

## What a Criminal Record Contains

A criminal record (also called a rap sheet) includes:

- Arrests (even without conviction)
- Charges filed
- Court dispositions (guilty pleas, verdicts, dismissals)
- Sentences (incarceration, probation, fines)
- Parole or probation status

**Arrests without conviction appear on records.** This surprises many people. Being arrested, charged, and then having the case dismissed or being acquitted doesn't erase the arrest record unless you take steps to seal or expunge it. Employers and landlords running background checks may see arrests regardless of outcome.

---

## Who Can See Your Record

**Criminal background checks** are run by employers, landlords, licensing boards, and others. Depending on the type of check:

- **Basic commercial background checks:** Typically search court records and databases. May miss arrests or court records in jurisdictions that don't contribute to national databases. May contain errors.

- **FBI fingerprint-based checks:** More comprehensive. Used for certain jobs (childcare, law enforcement, financial services, federal positions).

- **State criminal history repositories:** Maintained by each state. The most complete record of in-state criminal history.

**Public access:** Most court records are public. The public can look up cases by name at court websites. Background check services compile these records.

---

## Employment Consequences

Title VII prohibits using criminal records in a way that has a disparate impact on protected classes without business justification. The EEOC has issued guidance that blanket "no felony" policies may violate Title VII because criminal records disproportionately affect Black and Hispanic applicants.

The **ban the box** movement has resulted in laws in many states and cities prohibiting employers from asking about criminal history on initial job applications. The inquiry gets delayed until later in the hiring process — but it doesn't disappear.

**Background check laws vary.** Some states limit how far back checks can go (often 7 years for non-conviction information). Some limit what employers can consider. Know your state's rules.

---

## Expungement and Sealing

Many states allow people to clear or limit access to their criminal records through expungement or sealing. These are different:

**Expungement:** The record is destroyed or erased. It no longer exists in the official record. In most states, you can legally say you were not convicted of the expunged offense on most applications.

**Sealing:** The record still exists but is hidden from most background checks. Government agencies and law enforcement can still see sealed records. The general public and most employers cannot.

**What can be expunged or sealed varies by state:**
- First-time offenses
- Non-violent misdemeanors
- Juvenile records
- Arrests without conviction (important and often overlooked)
- Some felonies (after a waiting period and completion of sentence)
- Drug offenses (many states have created pathways for marijuana convictions)

Waiting periods apply — usually a minimum number of years after completing your sentence (including probation) with no new offenses.

---

## How to Pursue Expungement

1. **Check eligibility.** Look up your state's expungement laws. "Expungement [your state]" will get you to official resources. Legal aid organizations often publish plain-English guides.

2. **Obtain your complete criminal record.** You can request your official record from the state's criminal history repository. Know exactly what you're working with.

3. **File a petition with the court.** Expungement typically requires filing papers with the court that handled the original case, paying a filing fee, and attending a hearing.

4. **Get legal help.** Many legal aid organizations offer free or low-cost expungement assistance. Some jurisdictions hold expungement clinics where you can get help on the spot.

5. **Follow up.** After an expungement order is granted, check that background check databases have actually updated. Sometimes commercial databases lag behind or contain errors.

---

## Restoration of Rights

Beyond expungement, some states have processes to restore rights lost due to criminal conviction:

**Voting rights:** Most states restore voting rights after completion of sentence. Some restore rights immediately upon release. A few restrict rights only during incarceration. Maine and Vermont have no voting restrictions even for incarcerated people. Check your state's current rules.

**Firearms:** Federal law prohibits felons from possessing firearms. Some states allow restoration of firearm rights after a waiting period. This is a complex area — consult an attorney if relevant.

**Professional licenses:** Some licenses are restricted by criminal history. Many states have "fair chance" licensing laws that limit automatic disqualifications and require individualized assessment.

---

## Errors in Background Checks

Background checks contain errors more often than people realize. If you're denied a job or housing based on a background check:

- You have the right to see the report (Fair Credit Reporting Act)
- You have the right to dispute errors
- Background check companies have obligations to maintain accurate records
- Errors that cause harm can be the basis for legal action against the screening company

If you're denied and think the information was wrong, act quickly — you have a short window to dispute and potentially delay the adverse decision.
