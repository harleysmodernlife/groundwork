## Security Deposits: Your Rights

Security deposit disputes are one of the most common landlord-tenant conflicts. Landlords improperly withhold deposits. Tenants don't know their rights. Money that should be returned gets kept.

This is an area where knowing the law — and documenting your tenancy carefully — directly translates to money in your pocket.

---

## What a Security Deposit Is

A security deposit is money paid upfront to protect the landlord from unpaid rent or damage beyond normal wear and tear. It's your money held in trust. At the end of your tenancy, it should be returned to you minus legitimate deductions — with documentation.

---

## State Law Controls Security Deposits

Every state has its own rules governing security deposits. These vary significantly but typically address:

- **Maximum amount:** Many states cap deposits at one or two months' rent
- **Storage:** Some states require the deposit be held in a separate escrow account
- **Return timeline:** Usually 14 to 30 days after move-out (varies by state)
- **Itemization:** Landlords must typically provide an itemized statement of any deductions
- **Penalties:** Many states penalize landlords who wrongfully withhold deposits — sometimes double or triple the withheld amount plus attorney's fees

Look up your state's specific rules. "Security deposit laws [your state]" will get you there quickly.

---

## Normal Wear and Tear vs. Damage

This is the core dispute in most security deposit cases. Landlords can deduct for **damage**. They cannot deduct for **normal wear and tear**.

**Normal wear and tear** (landlord cannot deduct):
- Small nail holes from hanging pictures
- Faded paint or wallpaper from sunlight
- Worn carpet from regular use over time
- Minor scuffs on walls
- Worn finish on wood floors from foot traffic

**Damage** (landlord can deduct):
- Large holes in walls
- Burns on carpet or counters
- Broken fixtures, doors, or windows
- Pet damage (stains, chewing)
- Excessive filth requiring professional cleaning beyond normal
- Unauthorized alterations

The line isn't always clear, but "this carpet is old and worn" is different from "you spilled something and left a stain."

## How to Protect Your Deposit

**Before move-in:**
- Do a walk-through with the landlord and document every pre-existing condition
- Take date-stamped photos and video of every room
- Note everything: scuffs, stains, marks, broken blinds, condition of appliances
- Get a move-in inspection report signed by the landlord if possible
- Keep a copy of everything

**During tenancy:**
- Report maintenance issues in writing (email or text) so there's a record
- Don't make unauthorized alterations
- Document repairs the landlord makes or fails to make

**At move-out:**
- Do a walk-through with the landlord and get their acknowledgment of the unit's condition
- Take date-stamped photos and video again
- Clean thoroughly — professional cleaning prevents disputes
- Get a receipt for your keys

**After move-out:**
- Keep your forwarding address on file with the landlord in writing
- Know your state's return timeline
- If you don't receive the deposit or an itemized statement by the deadline, send a written demand

---

## If the Landlord Withholds Improperly

1. Send a written demand letter stating the amount you're owed and giving a deadline to respond (usually 10-14 days)
2. If they don't respond or dispute it, file in small claims court — most security deposit disputes are small enough for small claims, and you don't need an attorney
3. Bring your documentation: photos, the lease, your move-in inspection, your move-out documentation
4. Many states have penalty provisions — if the landlord wrongfully withheld, you may recover more than the deposit amount

Small claims court is straightforward and designed for exactly this type of dispute. Don't write off the money because court sounds complicated.

---

## The Documentation Rule

Every dollar you get back from a security deposit is won or lost based on documentation. A landlord who claims you damaged the carpet but has no move-in photos and no evidence the damage wasn't pre-existing will lose in court. A tenant who took photos on move-in and move-out and documented everything has a strong case.

The work you do on day one determines what happens on day last.
