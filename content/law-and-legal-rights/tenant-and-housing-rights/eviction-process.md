## The Eviction Process

Eviction — the legal process by which a landlord removes a tenant — has specific rules. Landlords cannot simply change locks, throw out your belongings, or shut off utilities to force you out. They must follow a legal process. Knowing that process protects you.

---

## Self-Help Eviction Is Illegal

A landlord cannot:
- Change the locks without going through court
- Remove your belongings from the unit
- Shut off utilities (heat, electricity, water) to pressure you to leave
- Physically remove you or threaten you to leave
- Remove doors or windows to make the unit uninhabitable

These "self-help eviction" tactics are illegal in virtually every state. If a landlord does any of these, you may have a claim for damages, and in some states, the landlord can be ordered to let you back in and pay penalties.

If a landlord changes your locks, contact an attorney immediately and consider calling the police to document the illegal lockout.

## The Basic Eviction Timeline

**Step 1: Notice**

The landlord must give you written notice before filing for eviction. The type of notice depends on the reason:

- **Pay or quit notice:** For non-payment of rent. You have a specified number of days (typically 3-14 depending on state) to pay the full amount or vacate. If you pay within the deadline, the eviction is stopped.

- **Cure or quit notice:** For lease violations other than non-payment. You have a certain number of days to fix the violation (cure) or vacate. Example: unauthorized pet — remove the pet within X days.

- **Unconditional quit notice:** For serious violations (significant property damage, criminal activity, repeat violations). No opportunity to cure — you must vacate.

- **No-fault / non-renewal notice:** In states without strong tenant protections, landlords may be able to end a month-to-month tenancy with 30-60 days notice for any reason. In cities with rent control or just-cause eviction laws, this is much more restricted.

**Step 2: Filing in Court**

If you don't comply with the notice, the landlord files an eviction lawsuit (called an unlawful detainer action in many states). You'll receive a summons requiring you to respond by a certain date.

**Step 3: Hearing**

You have the right to appear at the eviction hearing and present your defense. Common defenses include:
- Procedural defects (improper notice, wrong notice period, notice not served correctly)
- Landlord accepted rent after giving notice, waiving the eviction
- Retaliation — the eviction is because you exercised protected rights
- Habitability — you withheld rent because of uninhabitable conditions (following proper procedure)
- The alleged lease violation didn't happen or was cured

If you don't appear, the landlord wins by default. Show up.

**Step 4: Judgment**

If the landlord wins, the court issues a judgment for possession. You'll have a short window (usually a few days to a few weeks) to vacate voluntarily.

**Step 5: Writ of Possession**

If you still don't leave, the landlord can get a writ of possession. Law enforcement (a sheriff or marshal) posts notice and then physically removes you and your belongings on a set date.

## How Long It Takes

The eviction process typically takes 3-8 weeks from first notice to physical removal in most states, though it can take longer in tenant-friendly jurisdictions with backlogged courts. This is why self-help eviction is tempting for landlords — going through the courts is slower. But it's the only legal option.

---

## What You Should Do If Facing Eviction

**Don't ignore notices or court papers.** Missing deadlines or not appearing at hearings results in automatic losses.

**Respond to the lawsuit.** If you receive a summons, file a written answer raising your defenses by the deadline. Even if you can't stop the eviction, filing an answer buys time and creates a record.

**Seek legal help.** Many areas have legal aid organizations that provide free assistance to tenants facing eviction. Find one quickly — timelines are short.

**Pay what you owe if you can.** For non-payment evictions, paying the full amount owed (including fees, if required by your state) during the notice period typically stops the eviction.

**Negotiate.** Sometimes landlords will work out a payment plan or agree to additional time to move if you communicate with them. Get any agreement in writing.

**Know your defenses.** Procedural defects in how notice was served or the wrong notice period can defeat an eviction — even if you otherwise owe rent. The landlord must follow exact procedures.

---

## After Eviction

An eviction judgment on your record makes it harder to rent in the future. Some landlords will not rent to anyone with a prior eviction. Understand this consequence when deciding whether to fight an eviction versus negotiate a move-out agreement that avoids a court judgment.

Sometimes agreeing to leave on a specific date in exchange for the landlord not filing — a "cash for keys" arrangement — is better for your long-term housing prospects than fighting an eviction you're likely to lose.
