## Landlord Entry: Your Right to Privacy

Paying rent doesn't mean giving up privacy in your home. Landlords have the right to enter rental units — but that right is limited. Most states require advance notice, and unauthorized entry can be a violation of your rights.

---

## The General Rule: Notice Required

In most states, landlords must give **24 to 48 hours advance notice** before entering a rental unit for non-emergency purposes. Some states require 24 hours; others require 48. A few require "reasonable notice" without specifying a number.

The notice requirement applies even if the landlord owns the property. You have a possessory interest in the rental unit for the term of your lease — that's part of what rent buys.

---

## Permitted Reasons to Enter

Even with proper notice, landlords can only enter for legitimate purposes:

- Making repairs or inspections (after a repair request, before providing access to repair workers)
- Showing the unit to prospective tenants or buyers
- Responding to complaints or checking for lease violations
- Annual or periodic inspections (where allowed by the lease and local law)
- Emergency situations (see below)

Landlords cannot enter just to check on you, snoop through your belongings, or harass you.

---

## Emergency Entry

In a genuine emergency — burst pipe, fire, gas leak, or a situation where waiting would cause significant damage or injury — landlords can enter without notice. This is the major exception to the notice requirement.

"Emergency" means a real emergency. A landlord cannot call a non-urgent situation an emergency to circumvent the notice requirement.

---

## What Qualifies as Harassment

Repeated, unannounced entries — even for ostensibly legitimate reasons — can constitute harassment or a violation of your right to quiet enjoyment. This is especially relevant in disputes where a landlord is trying to pressure a tenant to leave.

Patterns of behavior: excessive inspection requests, last-minute "notice" that doesn't allow you to prepare, entering at odd hours, bringing multiple people for a "routine" repair — these can add up to constructive harassment even if each individual entry was technically legal.

---

## Your Right to Quiet Enjoyment

Most leases include, and most state laws imply, a covenant of quiet enjoyment — your right to use the rental unit without interference from the landlord. This doesn't mean no noise; it means the landlord can't interfere with your peaceful use and possession of the property.

Unauthorized entries, harassment, cutting off utilities, and removing property all violate quiet enjoyment. Depending on the state, violations can entitle you to damages or give you grounds to break the lease.

---

## What to Do About Unauthorized Entry

**Document it:** Write down when the entry happened, what was done, who entered, whether notice was given.

**Send a written notice:** Inform the landlord that entry without proper notice violates your rights and ask them to comply with notice requirements going forward. Keep a copy.

**Know your state law:** Look up what notice is required in your state. If the landlord is violating it, cite the specific statute.

**If it continues:** Options depending on state include:
- Filing a complaint with housing authorities
- Seeking a court order requiring proper notice
- In extreme cases, terminating the lease for breach of quiet enjoyment
- Seeking damages

---

## Your Lease May Have Additional Terms

Some leases specify entry procedures. Read your lease for any provisions about entry — what's required, how notice must be given, whether specific methods (email, text) are acceptable.

If the lease is more protective than state law (requires more notice, limits reasons for entry), the lease terms generally govern. If the lease is less protective than state law (waives your right to notice entirely), the legal minimum usually still applies — landlord-tenant laws are often not waivable by contract.

---

## Practical Tips

- If you receive a notice of entry and the time doesn't work for you, communicate that to the landlord and propose an alternative
- If you want to be present during repairs or inspections, you generally have that right — coordinate a time that works
- If you find evidence that someone entered without your knowledge or notice, document it immediately and address it in writing
