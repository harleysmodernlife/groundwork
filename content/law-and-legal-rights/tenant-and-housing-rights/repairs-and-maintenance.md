## Repairs and Maintenance: Who's Responsible

Maintenance disputes are common in rentals. The water heater breaks. The heat doesn't work in February. The faucet drips. Who fixes it, how fast, and what happens if it doesn't get fixed?

---

## The Basic Framework

**Landlord's responsibility:** Structural repairs, essential systems (heating, plumbing, electrical), and anything necessary for habitability. The landlord must maintain the rental in a livable condition regardless of whether the lease explicitly says so — this obligation comes from the implied warranty of habitability.

**Tenant's responsibility:** Minor maintenance, keeping the unit clean, not causing damage. Some leases assign specific maintenance tasks to tenants (changing light bulbs, unclogging drains). These must be clearly specified in the lease to be enforceable.

**Damage you caused:** If you broke it, you typically pay for it. Accidental damage (you dropped something and cracked a tile) is still damage you caused. Wear and tear from normal use is not.

---

## Requesting Repairs

**Always request in writing.** Email or text creates a record. Verbal requests can be denied; written ones can't.

Your written request should:
- Describe the specific problem
- Note when you first noticed it
- Give a reasonable timeline for response or repair
- Be clear about urgency (a non-working heater in winter is more urgent than a slow-draining sink)

Keep copies of all communications.

---

## What "Reasonable Time" Means

Landlords are generally given a reasonable amount of time to address repair requests. What's reasonable depends on severity:

**Emergency (24-48 hours or sooner):**
- No heat in winter
- No hot water
- Sewage backup
- Major plumbing leak
- Gas leak (call the utility company directly and evacuate — don't wait for the landlord)
- Security issues (broken door locks, broken windows)

**Urgent (within a week):**
- Non-functional refrigerator
- Broken appliances the landlord is responsible for
- Significant pest problems
- Leaky roof

**Non-urgent (within 30 days):**
- Minor repairs that don't affect habitability
- Cosmetic issues

If a landlord ignores an urgent request, you have escalating options.

---

## When the Landlord Doesn't Fix It

**Follow up in writing.** Send a second notice with a firm deadline: "If this is not repaired by [date], I will [next step]." Be specific about what the next step is.

**Contact housing code enforcement.** Your city or county has a housing inspection or code enforcement office. File a complaint. An inspector can issue a violation notice, which compels the landlord to act and creates an official record. This doesn't cost you anything and is often very effective.

**Repair and deduct.** In states that allow it: hire someone to fix the problem and deduct the cost from rent. This remedy has specific rules — you typically must give the landlord advance notice and an opportunity to fix it first, and there are dollar caps. Look up your state's specific law before doing this. Done incorrectly, it can give the landlord grounds to pursue you for unpaid rent.

**Withhold rent.** Some states allow tenants to stop paying rent (or pay into escrow) until habitability problems are fixed. This remedy is powerful but carries risk — if you don't follow your state's exact procedure, you may face eviction for nonpayment. Do this only after research or legal consultation.

**Break the lease.** If the problem significantly impairs habitability and the landlord refuses to fix it after proper notice, many states allow tenants to terminate the lease without penalty. This is constructive eviction — the landlord's failure has effectively made the unit unlivable.

---

## Emergency Repairs

If there's an immediate safety emergency (gas leak, burst pipe flooding the unit, structural collapse), protect yourself first. Call the relevant emergency services (fire department, gas company, emergency plumber). Then notify the landlord.

For genuine emergencies, you generally don't have to wait for the landlord to act. Get the problem stabilized first. Notify the landlord as soon as possible and document your actions.

---

## Documentation Throughout

Photograph every maintenance issue when you report it. Photograph after repair. Keep a log of when you reported issues, when the landlord responded (or didn't), and when repairs were made. This documentation is your evidence if the situation escalates to legal action or affects your security deposit.
