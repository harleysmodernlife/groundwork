## Dying Without a Will: What Happens

If you die without a valid will, you've died "intestate." Your state's intestacy laws determine what happens to your property. These laws establish a default distribution formula that applies when there's no will — and that formula may have nothing to do with what you would have wanted.

---

## How Intestacy Works

Every state has a statutory scheme for distributing assets when there's no will. The rules vary by state, but the general principle is that assets pass to your closest surviving relatives in a defined order of priority.

**Typical intestacy hierarchy:**

1. Spouse and children (often split between them according to a formula)
2. If no spouse or children: parents
3. If no parents: siblings
4. If no siblings: more distant relatives (grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins)
5. If no relatives: the state takes the property (called "escheat")

---

## Common Surprises in Intestacy

**Unmarried partners receive nothing.** If you lived with a partner for 20 years but never married, they have no inheritance rights under intestacy. Your assets go to your relatives — not them. This is one of the most important reasons unmarried couples need wills (and beneficiary designations).

**Stepchildren are typically not covered.** Unless you legally adopted them, stepchildren usually have no intestacy rights. A will is the only way to leave assets to stepchildren.

**Your share of joint property passes outside intestacy.** A house you own jointly with a right of survivorship, a joint bank account, a life insurance policy with a named beneficiary — these pass to whoever is designated, regardless of intestacy laws. Your will doesn't control these either.

**Your specific wishes don't apply.** Maybe you wanted your sister to get your grandmother's ring. Maybe you wanted $5,000 to go to your best friend who helped you through a difficult time. Intestacy doesn't accommodate personal relationships or specific intentions — it follows a formula.

---

## The Probate Process Without a Will

Without a will naming an executor, the court appoints an "administrator" to manage the estate. The court picks from a priority list (usually the surviving spouse or a close relative). This person may or may not be who you would have chosen.

The administrator has to post a bond (insurance), manage the estate according to state law, pay debts and taxes, and distribute assets as the intestacy laws direct. Without a will providing clear direction, this process can be slower, more expensive, and more contentious.

---

## Children and Guardianship

If you have minor children and die without a will, you've left no expression of who should raise them. A court decides — applying a best interests standard, but without knowing your wishes.

This doesn't mean your children will go to strangers. The court will typically look at close family members. But:
- You may have had a specific person in mind who the court wouldn't naturally choose
- You may have wanted to exclude a specific relative (an estranged parent, a sibling with substance abuse issues)
- You may have wanted to specify conditions — how your children's money is managed, at what age they receive it

Without a will, none of these wishes have legal weight.

---

## Assets That Don't Go Through Intestacy

It's worth repeating: a significant portion of most people's assets don't go through the will or intestacy process at all. These pass by:

- **Beneficiary designation:** Life insurance, retirement accounts (401k, IRA, pension), annuities, some bank accounts (POD — payable on death)
- **Joint tenancy with right of survivorship:** Property held jointly passes to the survivor
- **Transfer on death deeds:** Some states allow real estate to pass by a TOD deed without probate
- **Trusts:** Assets held in trust pass according to the trust terms

If you want these assets to go to a specific person, name them as beneficiary. No will required for these. But if you don't name a beneficiary and the default is your "estate," the asset falls into probate and intestacy.

---

## What to Do Instead

Getting a will doesn't have to be complicated or expensive:
- For simple situations, online services (LegalZoom, Trust & Will) can produce a valid will for $100-200
- Legal aid organizations sometimes offer free will clinics
- State bar referral services can connect you with an attorney for a reasonable consultation fee

The will itself can be short. "I leave everything to [person]" with a named executor and guardian is legally sufficient. Perfect shouldn't be the enemy of done.

Also update your beneficiary designations. Your will doesn't control retirement accounts or life insurance — your beneficiary designations do. Review them whenever you have a significant life change.

The cost of not having a will isn't something you'll feel. But the people you leave behind will.
