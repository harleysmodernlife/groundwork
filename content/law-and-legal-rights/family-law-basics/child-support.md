## Child Support

Child support is money paid by one parent to the other to help cover the costs of raising a child. It's not a punishment or a payment from one ex-spouse to another — it's a child's legal right to financial support from both parents, regardless of the parents' relationship.

---

## The Basic Principle

Both parents have a legal obligation to financially support their children. Child support is the mechanism for enforcing that obligation when parents live separately. The non-custodial parent (or lower-custody parent) typically pays support to the primary custodial parent.

Child support is distinct from alimony (spousal support). Alimony is between spouses. Child support belongs to the child — parents cannot waive child support on the child's behalf.

---

## How Child Support Is Calculated

Every state has child support guidelines — formulas that calculate a support amount based on objective factors. Courts are required to follow these guidelines unless there are exceptional circumstances.

Two main approaches:

**Income shares model (most states):** Combines both parents' incomes and calculates the total amount parents would theoretically spend on a child if they lived together. That total is then split proportionally based on each parent's income, with the non-custodial parent's share paid as support.

**Percentage of income model (a few states):** Support is calculated as a percentage of the non-custodial parent's income, regardless of the other parent's earnings.

**Key inputs to any calculation:**
- Both parents' gross or net incomes (wages, salaries, self-employment income, rental income, etc.)
- Number of children
- The custody arrangement (how many overnights with each parent)
- Health insurance costs
- Childcare costs
- Sometimes: extraordinary expenses (special needs, education)

---

## What Child Support Covers

Support is meant to cover a child's basic needs: housing, food, clothing, and similar ordinary expenses. Many expenses beyond basics are addressed separately in the support order:

- **Health insurance:** Which parent provides coverage, how costs are divided
- **Uninsured medical expenses:** Usually split proportionally between parents
- **Childcare costs:** Work-related childcare is typically shared
- **Extraordinary expenses:** Extracurricular activities, private school, special needs — may be specified or left to negotiation

---

## Duration

Child support generally continues until the child:
- Turns 18 (the age of majority in most states)
- Graduates from high school (some states)
- Becomes emancipated

Some states require support for disabled adult children who remain dependent. A few states address college expenses. These specifics vary significantly by state.

---

## Enforcement

Child support is one of the most actively enforced areas of family law. The federal government has created strong enforcement mechanisms:

- **Wage garnishment:** The most common enforcement tool. Support is automatically withheld from the paying parent's paycheck and sent to the other parent.
- **Tax refund interception:** State and federal tax refunds can be seized for unpaid support.
- **License suspension:** Driver's license, professional licenses, and recreational licenses can be suspended for non-payment.
- **Passport denial:** The State Department can refuse to issue or renew a passport if you owe more than $2,500 in unpaid support.
- **Credit reporting:** Child support arrears are reported to credit bureaus.
- **Contempt of court:** Willful non-payment can result in jail time.

Unpaid child support (arrears) is legally significant debt that doesn't go away. It can't be discharged in bankruptcy. It accrues interest. It follows the paying parent.

---

## Modifying Child Support

Support orders can be modified when there's been a substantial change in circumstances:
- A significant change in either parent's income
- A change in custody arrangement
- A change in the child's needs

Most states allow modification when income changes by 15-20% or more. The change must be ongoing, not temporary.

If you lose your job or experience a significant income drop, file for modification immediately. Support continues to accrue at the current amount until a court modifies the order. Falling behind while waiting to modify creates debt that compounds.

---

## If You're Not Receiving Support

Contact your state's child support enforcement agency. Each state has one (typically within the Department of Health and Human Services or similar). These agencies have the power to locate a non-paying parent, establish paternity if necessary, and enforce the order using all the mechanisms above — at no cost to you.

You can also hire a private attorney for enforcement, but the state agency is a free resource specifically for this.

---

## Taxes

Child support payments are not deductible by the paying parent and not taxable income to the receiving parent. (This is different from alimony, which has its own tax rules.)
