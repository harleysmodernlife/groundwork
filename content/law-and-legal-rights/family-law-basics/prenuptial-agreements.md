## Prenuptial Agreements

A prenuptial agreement (prenup) is a contract signed before marriage that specifies how assets and debts will be divided if the marriage ends. It can also address financial expectations and rights during the marriage.

Prenups have a reputation as something only wealthy people need, or as a sign of distrust. Neither framing is accurate.

---

## What Prenups Can Do

**Protect separate property:** If you're bringing significant assets into the marriage — a house, a business, an inheritance, a retirement account — a prenup can establish that those assets remain yours if the marriage ends.

**Address debt:** Specify that each person is responsible for their own pre-marital debt and debt incurred in their own name during marriage.

**Protect business interests:** If you own a business, a prenup can prevent a divorce from forcing a business sale or requiring you to hand over a share to a former spouse.

**Set terms for children from prior relationships:** If you have children from a previous relationship, a prenup can ensure specific assets are preserved for them.

**Establish or waive spousal support:** A prenup can specify whether one spouse will pay alimony to the other, how much, and for how long — or waive it entirely. (Courts in some states limit the ability to fully waive spousal support, especially if it would leave one spouse in poverty.)

**Simplify a potential divorce:** Couples who've worked out property division in advance have simpler, less expensive divorces if it comes to that.

---

## What Prenups Cannot Do

- **Govern child custody or child support:** Courts determine what's in the best interest of children at the time of divorce. Parents can't pre-determine this in a contract.
- **Address non-financial issues:** Lifestyle clauses ("you must keep the house clean") are generally unenforceable.
- **Encourage divorce:** Courts won't enforce provisions that provide incentives to divorce.
- **Waive rights to government benefits** (Social Security spousal benefits, for example, are governed by federal law)
- **Violate state law or public policy**

---

## When to Consider a Prenup

- You or your partner have significant assets
- You or your partner have significant debt
- You own or are a partner in a business
- You're expecting a substantial inheritance
- You have children from a prior relationship
- One of you will leave the workforce to care for children (creating financial dependence)
- One of you earns significantly more than the other
- You're entering a second or subsequent marriage

A prenup doesn't have to mean "I don't trust you." It can mean "Let's be clear about expectations so we don't fight about this later" — the same reason contracts exist in business.

---

## Requirements for a Valid Prenup

Prenup requirements vary by state, but generally:

**In writing:** Verbal prenups are not enforceable.

**Signed voluntarily:** Both parties must sign without coercion or duress. A prenup handed to someone the night before the wedding with no time to review can be challenged.

**Full financial disclosure:** Both parties must fully disclose their assets and debts. A prenup can be voided if it was based on incomplete or false disclosure.

**Independent legal counsel:** Not always legally required, but strongly advisable. A prenup where one party had no attorney is much more vulnerable to challenge. Many attorneys won't draft a prenup for one side without insisting the other side also get independent counsel.

**Reasonable terms:** Courts occasionally refuse to enforce prenups that are extremely one-sided or unconscionable.

---

## The Process

1. Raise the conversation early — at least several months before the wedding. Last-minute prenups are scrutinized and can be challenged.
2. Each party hires their own attorney (using the same attorney for both is a conflict of interest and weakens enforceability).
3. Each party fully discloses their assets and debts.
4. Attorneys draft and negotiate the agreement.
5. Both parties sign, ideally with witnesses or notarization depending on state requirements.

---

## Challenging a Prenup

Prenups can be challenged in divorce proceedings. Common grounds:
- Signed under duress (pressure, threat, no time to review)
- No meaningful financial disclosure
- Lacked independent legal counsel
- Unconscionable terms
- Improper execution (not signed, not witnessed if required)

A well-drafted prenup with independent counsel, adequate time, full disclosure, and fair (not necessarily equal) terms is much harder to challenge than a hastily drafted one.

---

## Postnuptial Agreements

Similar to a prenup but signed after marriage. Same general purpose — defining property rights in the event of divorce. Courts scrutinize postnups somewhat more carefully because the power dynamics of marriage may differ from those at engagement. But they're valid in most states.
