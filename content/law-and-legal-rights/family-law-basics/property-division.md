## Property Division in Divorce

One of the most significant practical consequences of divorce is dividing what you owned together. How property is divided depends on your state's system, what counts as marital property, and — if you can't agree — what a judge decides is fair.

---

## The Two Systems

**Community property (9 states):** California, Arizona, Texas, Nevada, Washington, Idaho, New Mexico, Louisiana, Wisconsin.

In community property states, most property acquired during marriage is owned 50/50 by both spouses. At divorce, community property is divided equally. There's less judicial discretion — it's a mathematical split of what's in the marital estate.

**Equitable distribution (remaining states):**

Property is divided fairly — not necessarily equally. A judge (or the spouses through negotiation) divides marital property in whatever way seems equitable given the specific circumstances. "Equitable" might result in 50/50, 60/40, or another split depending on the facts.

Factors courts consider in equitable distribution:
- Length of the marriage
- Each spouse's income, earning capacity, and financial situation
- Contributions each spouse made (including non-financial contributions like homemaking and childcare)
- Conduct of the parties (in some states, fault affects division)
- Separate property owned by each spouse
- The standard of living established during marriage
- Age and health of each spouse

---

## Marital Property vs. Separate Property

**Marital property:** Generally, everything acquired during the marriage — income earned, accounts funded, property purchased, retirement benefits accrued, debt incurred.

**Separate property:** Property owned before marriage. Gifts or inheritances received by one spouse (even during marriage). Property specifically excluded by a prenuptial agreement.

Separate property is generally not divided in divorce. It stays with the spouse who owns it.

**But:** Separate property can become marital property through commingling or transmutation. If you inherited $50,000 and deposited it into a joint account that both spouses drew from, it may have lost its separate character. If you owned a house before marriage and your spouse contributed to improvements and the mortgage for 15 years, a court may find a marital interest in the property.

Keeping separate property truly separate — in your own name, in separate accounts, without mixing it with marital funds — preserves its separate character.

---

## What Gets Divided

**Bank and investment accounts:** Divided according to their marital vs. separate character. Often split by agreement or court order.

**Real estate:** The marital home is often the most contested asset. Options include:
- Sell it and split the proceeds
- One spouse buys out the other's interest (requires refinancing in most cases)
- Deferred sale (especially with minor children, sometimes the custodial parent stays until children finish school)

**Retirement accounts:** Benefits accrued during marriage are marital property. The portion accrued before marriage is separate. Dividing retirement accounts requires a special court order called a QDRO (Qualified Domestic Relations Order) for most employer plans. IRAs can be divided by transfer pursuant to a divorce decree. Retirement division is technically complex — errors can create tax consequences.

**Business interests:** If one spouse owns a business, the marital portion of its value must be determined and divided. This often requires a business valuation by a forensic accountant. One of the most contentious areas of divorce litigation.

**Debt:** Marital debt is also divided. Credit cards, mortgages, car loans incurred during marriage are generally marital obligations. Important note: a divorce decree saying your spouse is responsible for a joint debt doesn't bind the creditor — if your spouse doesn't pay a joint account, your credit still suffers. Try to either pay off or refinance joint debts out of one person's name.

---

## Negotiating vs. Litigating

Spouses can negotiate property division themselves (especially in uncontested divorces), through their attorneys, or through mediation. Most property is divided by agreement, not court order.

When you can't agree, a judge decides. Judges apply the legal standard (community property or equitable distribution) to the facts. The outcome can be unpredictable, and litigation is expensive.

In most cases, both spouses are better off negotiating than fighting in court — the legal fees alone can consume a significant portion of the assets you're fighting over.

---

## Hidden Assets

Some spouses try to hide assets to reduce what they share. This is illegal. Discovery processes in divorce require full financial disclosure — tax returns, bank statements, retirement accounts, business records.

If you suspect hidden assets:
- Hire a forensic accountant
- Subpoena financial records
- Look for lifestyle that doesn't match disclosed income
- Examine tax returns for income not matching bank deposits

Courts take hidden assets seriously. Hiding assets in divorce can result in the court awarding more than 50% to the other spouse as a sanction.
