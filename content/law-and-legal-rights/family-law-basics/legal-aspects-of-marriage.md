## The Legal Aspects of Marriage

Marriage is a personal and cultural institution, but it's also a legal contract with the government. Entering a marriage changes your legal rights and obligations in ways most people don't fully think through. Understanding what changes — and doesn't change — helps you make informed decisions.

---

## What Marriage Legally Creates

When you marry, you and your spouse become legal partners with rights and obligations toward each other that didn't exist before:

**Property rights:** In most states, property acquired during marriage becomes marital property — jointly owned regardless of whose name is on the account or title. What this means at divorce varies by state, but the default is that you both have claims to marital property.

**Spousal support obligations:** You may be legally obligated to support your spouse. This can affect decisions during marriage and if you separate.

**Inheritance rights:** A surviving spouse has legal rights to a portion of the deceased spouse's estate, even if not mentioned in the will (the "elective share" in common law states).

**Medical decision-making:** Spouses are typically recognized as next-of-kin for medical decisions if you're incapacitated, ahead of parents or siblings.

**Tax status:** You file federal taxes as married. This creates both potential tax advantages (married filing jointly) and potential disadvantages (the "marriage penalty" in some income combinations).

**Immigration:** Marriage to a U.S. citizen or permanent resident can establish a path to immigration status.

**Benefits:** Spousal eligibility for health insurance, pension survivor benefits, Social Security spousal benefits.

**Privilege:** Spousal privilege prevents either spouse from being forced to testify against the other in most criminal proceedings.

## Requirements for a Valid Marriage

To be legally married, you generally need:
- A marriage license (from the county clerk)
- A ceremony (civil or religious) that meets your state's requirements
- A licensed officiant (clergy, judge, government official, or in some states, someone ordained online)
- Witnesses in most states
- Filing of the marriage certificate after the ceremony

**Age:** All states now require both parties to be 18 to marry without parental or court approval. (This has changed recently — some states previously allowed marriage at younger ages with parental consent, and reforms have been enacted in many states.)

**Not currently married:** You cannot marry if you're already legally married to someone else. You must divorce or become widowed first.

**Not closely related:** Marriage between close blood relatives is prohibited (the specific prohibitions vary by state).

## Common-Law Marriage

A small number of states recognize common-law marriage — where a couple lives together, holds themselves out as married, and intends to be married, without a formal ceremony or license. States that recognize it include Colorado, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Oklahoma, Rhode Island, South Carolina, Texas, Utah, and D.C.

If you're in one of these states and meet the requirements, you may be legally married even without a ceremony. Common-law marriages recognized by the state where they were formed are generally recognized by other states.

The significance: common-law spouses have the same legal rights as formally married spouses — including at death or divorce. "We lived together for 10 years" doesn't create common-law marriage in states that don't recognize it.

---

## What Marriage Doesn't Do

- It doesn't merge your pre-marital assets automatically — property you owned before marriage is typically still separate property
- It doesn't affect debts your spouse incurred before marriage — you're not responsible for their pre-marital debt (though community property states have nuances)
- It doesn't mean you can't have separate financial accounts or independent finances
- It doesn't change the fundamental rule that you can't be forced to testify against yourself (Fifth Amendment)

---

## The Practical Reality

Most couples marry without giving much thought to the legal structure they're entering. That's fine for most — the default rules work adequately when a marriage is happy and stable.

But understanding the legal framework matters:
- If you're bringing significant assets into a marriage (property, a business, inheritance)
- If one partner has significant debt
- If you're on a second marriage with children from a prior relationship
- If one partner earns significantly more than the other

These situations may warrant a prenuptial agreement to establish rules that differ from your state's defaults.
