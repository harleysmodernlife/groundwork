## Wills: The Basics

A will is a legal document that says what happens to your property after you die and who takes care of any minor children. Most people don't have one — partly because thinking about death is uncomfortable, and partly because people assume they don't have enough assets to need one.

Both assumptions are costly mistakes.

---

## What a Will Does

**Distributes your property:** A will specifies who gets what when you die. Without a will, your state's intestacy laws determine distribution — a default formula that may not match your wishes at all.

**Names a guardian for minor children:** If you have children under 18 and you die, someone needs to care for them. A will is where you designate who that is. Without a will, a court decides. This alone is reason enough for every parent to have a will.

**Names an executor:** The person responsible for carrying out the will — collecting assets, paying debts, filing final taxes, and distributing what remains. The executor has a legal duty to follow the will's instructions and act in the beneficiaries' interests.

**Minimizes family conflict:** Without a will, family members may dispute who gets what. A clear, legally valid will reduces (though doesn't eliminate) the possibility of conflict.

---

## What a Will Doesn't Do

**Doesn't cover everything:** Assets that pass by beneficiary designation or joint ownership aren't governed by your will:
- Life insurance proceeds go to the named beneficiary
- Retirement accounts (401k, IRA) go to the named beneficiary
- Joint bank accounts pass to the surviving joint owner
- Property held in joint tenancy with right of survivorship passes to the survivor

These pass outside of probate regardless of what your will says. Keep beneficiary designations updated — an ex-spouse listed as beneficiary on a life insurance policy still gets the money if you die.

**Doesn't avoid probate:** In most cases, a will must go through probate — the court-supervised process of validating the will and overseeing distribution. Probate takes time (months to over a year), costs money (court fees, attorney fees), and is public record. Trusts are used in part to avoid probate.

---

## Requirements for a Valid Will

**In writing:** An oral will is almost never valid for real property. Wills must be written.

**Signed by the testator:** You (the person whose will it is) must sign or acknowledge the signature.

**Witnessed:** Most states require two adult witnesses who watch you sign and then sign themselves. In many states, witnesses cannot be beneficiaries of the will (or their inheritance is at risk).

**Mental capacity:** You must be of sound mind when you sign — you understand what a will is, what property you have, and who your family members are.

**Notarization:** Not required in most states, but a "self-proving affidavit" (a notarized declaration by the witnesses) allows the will to be admitted to probate without the witnesses having to appear in court.

---

## Holographic Wills

About half of states recognize holographic wills — entirely handwritten and signed by the testator, but not witnessed. They're better than nothing if there's no other option, but they're also more frequently contested and more likely to be found invalid. A properly witnessed will is far more reliable.

---

## Basic Will Provisions

A simple will typically covers:
- Who gets your property (specific bequests or a general residuary clause leaving "everything else" to a named person)
- Who gets specific items of personal significance
- Who is the executor
- Who is guardian of any minor children
- Alternate beneficiaries if your primary beneficiary predeceases you

---

## Keeping a Will Updated

A will is a point-in-time document. It should be reviewed and potentially updated when:
- You marry or divorce
- You have children or grandchildren
- A named beneficiary or executor dies
- Your assets change significantly
- Your wishes change

Marriage and divorce affect wills in most states — marriage sometimes revokes a prior will; divorce may revoke provisions for an ex-spouse. But don't rely on automatic rules — update your will.

---

## Where to Keep Your Will

The original signed will matters. Copies are generally not sufficient for probate.

- Keep the original in a safe, accessible place — a fireproof home safe, a bank safety deposit box (but tell your executor where), or with your attorney
- Tell your executor where it is
- Do not keep it somewhere only you know about

A will that can't be found after you die may as well not exist.

---

## Do You Need an Attorney?

For simple situations (no minor children, relatively straightforward assets, clear wishes), online will services (LegalZoom, Trust & Will, state bar referral services) can produce a valid will at modest cost.

For more complex situations — blended families, minor children, significant assets, business ownership, desire to avoid probate — an estate planning attorney is worth the investment. A will drafted by an attorney that fits your actual situation is less likely to be challenged and more likely to accomplish what you intend.

Either way, something beats nothing. Most adults should have at least a basic will.
