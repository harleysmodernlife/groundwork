## Filing a Workplace Complaint

When your employer violates the law — failing to pay wages, discriminating, retaliating, creating unsafe conditions — you have multiple avenues for making a complaint. Each agency handles different types of violations. Knowing where to go is the first step.

---

## The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC)

**What it handles:** Employment discrimination based on race, color, sex, national origin, religion, age (40+), disability, pregnancy, and genetic information. Also handles retaliation for engaging in protected activity.

**When to use it:** Before you can sue an employer in federal court for discrimination under Title VII, the ADA, or the ADEA, you must first file a charge with the EEOC. This is a mandatory step.

**Time limits:** You must file within 180 days of the discriminatory act (300 days in states that have their own fair employment agency — most states). Missing this deadline means you can't sue federally. This is one of the most commonly missed procedural requirements.

**What happens:**
1. You file a charge (online, by mail, or in person at an EEOC office)
2. The EEOC notifies your employer
3. The EEOC may investigate, attempt mediation, or find "no reasonable cause" to investigate further
4. If the EEOC doesn't resolve it, they issue a "right to sue" letter giving you 90 days to file suit in federal court

**Important:** The EEOC investigates a small percentage of charges fully. Receiving a "no cause" determination doesn't necessarily mean your case is weak — the EEOC is overloaded. Many attorneys will still take cases that received "no cause" determinations.

---

## Department of Labor: Wage and Hour Division

**What it handles:** Violations of the Fair Labor Standards Act — minimum wage, overtime, and child labor. Also administers the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA).

**When to use it:** If you haven't been paid minimum wage, aren't receiving overtime you're owed, or have been denied FMLA leave you're entitled to.

**How to file:** Online at the Department of Labor website, or call the Wage and Hour Division. Complaints are confidential.

**What happens:** The WHD may investigate your employer, interview you and coworkers, review payroll records, and if violations are found, order back pay and penalties.

**Time limits:** 2 years for regular FLSA violations, 3 years for willful violations. You can also sue directly without going through the agency.

---

## OSHA: Occupational Safety and Health Administration

**What it handles:** Workplace safety violations. Employers must provide a workplace free from recognized hazards.

**When to use it:** Unsafe working conditions, exposure to dangerous materials, lack of required safety equipment, or if you've been retaliated against for reporting safety concerns.

**How to file:** Online, by phone, or by mail. Complaints can be confidential.

**Time limits for retaliation claims:** 30 days from the retaliatory act for most industries (some industries have longer windows). This is extremely short — act quickly if you've been retaliated against for a safety complaint.

---

## State Labor Agencies

Every state has its own labor department that handles:
- State minimum wage and overtime violations (often with stronger protections than federal law)
- State-specific discrimination claims
- Wage theft and unpaid wages
- Unemployment insurance
- Workers' compensation

State agencies often have broader coverage, shorter deadlines to resolve cases, and sometimes stronger penalties than federal agencies. File with both federal and state agencies when possible — they handle claims independently.

---

## National Labor Relations Board (NLRB)

**What it handles:** Rights under the National Labor Relations Act — the right to organize, form unions, engage in collective bargaining, and take "concerted action" (two or more employees acting together to improve working conditions).

**Important:** Protected concerted activity applies even in non-union workplaces. If two employees complain to management together about working conditions, or employees discuss wages with each other, that's protected. Retaliation for this is an unfair labor practice.

**Time limits:** 6 months from the unfair labor practice.

---

## Internal Complaints

Before or alongside external filings, report violations internally through your HR department or the company's reporting hotline. Reasons:

- Some legal claims require showing you used the employer's complaint procedures first
- Internal reporting can sometimes resolve the issue faster
- It creates a record that you put the employer on notice
- Retaliation after an internal complaint is separately illegal

Document your internal complaint in writing (email to HR) and keep a copy.

---

## What to Prepare

Before filing any complaint:
- Write down a detailed factual account of what happened, with dates
- Gather any supporting documents (pay stubs, emails, texts, performance reviews, written policies)
- Identify witnesses who can support your account
- Know the relevant time limits for your type of claim

You don't need an attorney to file administrative complaints — they're designed to be accessible to individuals. But for anything you intend to litigate, a consultation with an employment attorney is valuable.
