## Protected Classes in Employment

Federal law prohibits employment discrimination based on certain characteristics. These characteristics are called "protected classes." Employers cannot make hiring, firing, compensation, promotion, or other employment decisions based on them.

Knowing what's protected — and what isn't — helps you recognize when discrimination may be occurring and what you can do about it.

---

## Federal Protected Classes

**Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964):**
- Race
- Color
- National origin
- Sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation, and gender identity — confirmed by *Bostock v. Clayton County*, 2020)
- Religion

**Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA):**
- Age (protects workers 40 and older)

**Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA):**
- Disability (physical or mental impairment that substantially limits a major life activity)
- Also protects people with a history of disability and people regarded as disabled

**Pregnancy Discrimination Act:**
- Pregnancy, childbirth, and related medical conditions (treated as sex discrimination under Title VII)

**Equal Pay Act:**
- Prohibits pay discrimination based on sex for substantially equal work (though enforcement can be complex)

**Genetic Information Non-Discrimination Act (GINA):**
- Genetic information (cannot use genetic testing or family medical history in employment decisions)

---

## State and Local Protected Classes

Many states and cities add additional protected classes that go beyond federal law. Common additions include:
- Marital status
- Sexual orientation and gender identity (now federal too, but state laws predate the federal rule)
- Military or veteran status
- Source of income
- Political affiliation
- Citizenship status (beyond what federal law covers)
- Height and weight (in some jurisdictions)

Look up your state's specific anti-discrimination laws — they may offer broader protections than federal law.

---

## What Discrimination Looks Like

Employment discrimination is prohibited in:
- Hiring decisions
- Firing or layoffs
- Pay and compensation
- Promotions and demotions
- Job assignments
- Training opportunities
- Working conditions
- Harassment (severe or pervasive conduct based on a protected class)

**Disparate treatment:** Being treated differently than similarly situated employees because of a protected characteristic. Example: Women are not promoted to management positions even when equally or more qualified than men who are promoted.

**Disparate impact:** A neutral policy that has a disproportionate negative effect on a protected class, without business justification. Example: A height requirement that disproportionately excludes women or certain national origin groups from a job that doesn't actually require height.

---

## What's Not Protected

Federal law doesn't prohibit all unfair treatment — only treatment based on protected characteristics. You can be fired because your boss doesn't like you personally, because you made a mistake, because the company is downsizing, or for any number of reasons that have nothing to do with a protected class.

Favoritism, nepotism, workplace politics, and general unfairness aren't illegal discrimination unless they're connected to a protected characteristic.

---

## Disability: A Closer Look

The ADA deserves special attention because it's more nuanced than the other protected classes.

**Reasonable accommodation:** Employers must make reasonable accommodations for employees with disabilities unless doing so would cause undue hardship. Reasonable accommodations might include modified work schedules, ergonomic equipment, remote work, reassignment to a different role, or leave.

**Interactive process:** When you request an accommodation, the employer must engage in an "interactive process" with you to identify what accommodation would work. You're entitled to this conversation.

**Undue hardship:** Employers don't have to provide accommodations that are excessively costly or burdensome relative to their size and resources. Small employers get more leeway than large ones.

**What qualifies as a disability:** The ADA covers physical and mental impairments that substantially limit major life activities. Courts have interpreted this broadly. Anxiety disorder, ADHD, depression, cancer, diabetes, and many other conditions may qualify.

---

## Religion: Another Nuance

Employers must also make reasonable accommodations for employees' sincerely held religious beliefs, unless it causes undue hardship. Common examples:
- Scheduling accommodations for religious holidays or sabbaths
- Exceptions to dress or grooming policies for religious dress
- Accommodations for prayer time

"Undue hardship" for religious accommodations is a lower standard than for disability — courts have sometimes found even minimal burden to be sufficient. But the obligation to try exists.

## How to Respond to Discrimination

If you believe you've been discriminated against:

1. **Document:** Write down what happened, when, who was involved, and any witnesses. Save any relevant emails, texts, or documents.

2. **Report internally:** Use your employer's complaint procedures. This is often required before you can sue, and it creates a record.

3. **File with the EEOC:** For federal claims, you must file an EEOC charge before suing in federal court. The deadline is typically 180 days from the discriminatory act (300 days in states with their own agencies). Miss this deadline and you lose the right to sue federally.

4. **Consult an attorney:** Employment attorneys often take discrimination cases on contingency. Free consultations are common.

5. **File with your state agency:** May offer broader protections or a more favorable process.
