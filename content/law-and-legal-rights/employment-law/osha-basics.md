## OSHA Basics: Workplace Safety Rights

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) sets and enforces standards for safe working conditions. Employers covered by OSHA are legally required to provide a workplace free from recognized hazards that are likely to cause death or serious physical harm.

This matters regardless of your industry. Construction workers face one set of hazards; office workers face others. The protections exist across the board.

---

## The General Duty Clause

Even where no specific OSHA standard exists, the general duty clause requires employers to:

"Furnish to each of his employees employment and a place of employment which are free from recognized hazards that are causing or are likely to cause death or serious physical harm to his employees."

This is a broad obligation. If there's an obvious danger in your workplace that isn't covered by a specific standard, OSHA can still cite the employer under the general duty clause.

---

## Your Rights Under OSHA

**The right to a safe workplace:** Your employer must follow applicable OSHA standards and fix recognized hazards.

**The right to information:** You have the right to see OSHA injury and illness records kept by your employer. You can also request information about hazardous chemicals in your workplace under the Hazard Communication Standard (right-to-know laws). Safety Data Sheets (SDSs) for all hazardous chemicals must be available to employees.

**The right to training:** For many hazards, OSHA standards require employers to provide training in a language you understand.

**The right to request an inspection:** You can ask OSHA to inspect your workplace if you believe there are safety violations. You can request confidentiality — OSHA won't tell your employer who filed the complaint.

**The right to participate in inspections:** If OSHA inspects, an employee representative has the right to accompany the inspector during the walk-around.

**The right to refuse dangerous work:** You can refuse to do work that poses an imminent danger of death or serious injury if all of the following apply:
- You asked the employer to fix the hazard and they refused
- There wasn't time to file an OSHA complaint and have it investigated
- A reasonable person would believe the danger is real

This right is narrow — it's not a blanket right to refuse any task you consider risky. But in genuine imminent danger situations, it exists.

**Protection from retaliation:** OSHA prohibits employers from retaliating against you for exercising your OSHA rights — filing a complaint, refusing imminently dangerous work, reporting an injury, or participating in an inspection. If you're retaliated against, you must file a complaint within 30 days for most violations. This deadline is short — act quickly.

## Common OSHA Standards

**Hazard Communication:** Employers must inform employees about hazardous chemicals through labels, SDSs, and training.

**Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):** Employers must provide and require appropriate PPE for hazards that can't otherwise be controlled — respirators, gloves, eye protection, hard hats, hearing protection.

**Electrical safety:** Standards for wiring, grounding, lockout/tagout procedures.

**Fall protection:** In construction, fall protection is required at 6 feet; in general industry, at 4 feet.

**Machine guarding:** Moving parts of machinery must be guarded to prevent contact.

**Confined spaces:** Entry into confined spaces (tanks, silos, crawl spaces) requires specific procedures, atmospheric testing, and rescue planning.

**Emergency action plans:** Many employers are required to have written emergency action plans.

---

## Recordkeeping

Employers with 10 or more employees (in most industries) must keep records of workplace injuries and illnesses. The OSHA 300 log records all work-related injuries and illnesses requiring more than first aid.

You have the right to access this log. Reviewing it gives you a sense of your workplace's injury history.

**Reporting:** Employers must report to OSHA within 8 hours of a workplace fatality and within 24 hours of an in-patient hospitalization, amputation, or loss of an eye. These aren't optional.

---

## Limitations

OSHA doesn't cover:
- Self-employed workers (no employees)
- Immediate family members of farm employers
- Employees covered by other federal safety programs (mining, nuclear, airlines have separate agencies)
- State and local government employees (in most states — some states have their own OSHA-approved plans that cover public employees)

---

## Filing a Complaint

If you believe your workplace has a safety violation:
1. Report it to your supervisor or safety officer first if safe to do so
2. File an OSHA complaint online, by phone (1-800-321-OSHA), or by mail
3. Request confidentiality — OSHA won't reveal who filed

OSHA prioritizes complaints based on severity. Imminent danger situations get fastest response. Complaints about non-imminent hazards may be resolved with a letter to the employer rather than an in-person inspection.

Your employer cannot punish you for filing. Document any adverse action immediately and file a retaliation complaint within 30 days.
