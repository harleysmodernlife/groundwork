## The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA)

The Family and Medical Leave Act gives eligible employees the right to take unpaid, job-protected leave for certain family and medical reasons. Your job is protected while you're on leave — you return to the same or equivalent position.

Most workers don't fully understand FMLA until they need it. Knowing the rules in advance helps you assert your rights when it matters.

---

## Who Is Covered

**You must meet all three criteria:**

1. **Your employer must be covered:** Employers with 50 or more employees within 75 miles of your worksite.

2. **You must have worked long enough:** At least 12 months for this employer, and at least 1,250 hours in the 12 months before leave.

3. **Your leave must be for a qualifying reason.**

Small businesses (under 50 employees) are not covered by FMLA. If you work for a small employer, check your state — many states have similar laws with lower employee thresholds.

---

## Qualifying Reasons

FMLA leave is available for:

- **Birth of a child** and bonding during the first year
- **Adoption or foster placement** of a child and bonding during the first year
- **Your own serious health condition** that makes you unable to perform your job
- **Care for a spouse, child, or parent** with a serious health condition
- **Qualifying military exigency:** Certain situations arising from a spouse, child, or parent being called to active duty

**What counts as a "serious health condition":** An illness, injury, impairment, or physical or mental condition that involves inpatient care or continuing treatment by a health care provider. This includes conditions that require periodic treatment (chronic conditions, recurring episodes) even if each episode isn't severe.

---

## How Much Leave

Up to **12 weeks** of unpaid leave in a 12-month period.

**Military caregiver leave:** Up to 26 weeks in a single 12-month period if you're caring for a covered service member with a serious injury or illness.

**Intermittent leave:** FMLA can be taken all at once, in separate blocks, or as reduced-schedule leave (a few hours per week). Intermittent leave is particularly useful for chronic conditions that cause periodic flare-ups.

---

## Pay During FMLA

FMLA itself is unpaid. However, employers can (and often do) require you to use accrued paid leave (vacation, sick days) concurrently with FMLA. Some employers offer paid family leave policies on top of FMLA — know your employer's policy.

Some states have mandatory paid family leave programs (California, New York, New Jersey, Washington, and others). These provide partial wage replacement during qualifying leave, and run concurrently with FMLA.

---

## Job Protection

When you return from FMLA leave, you're entitled to:
- Your same position, or
- An equivalent position with equivalent pay, benefits, and working conditions

Your employer cannot demote you, reduce your pay, or assign you to an inferior position because you took FMLA leave.

**Benefits continuation:** Group health insurance continues during FMLA leave under the same conditions as if you were still working. You still pay your portion of premiums.

---

## How to Request FMLA

**Notify your employer.** For foreseeable leave (planned medical procedure, expected birth), give 30 days advance notice when possible. For unforeseeable leave (medical emergency), notify as soon as practicable.

You don't have to invoke the specific term "FMLA." You just need to give enough information for the employer to understand you may need FMLA-qualifying leave.

**Medical certification.** Your employer can require a medical certification from your (or the family member's) healthcare provider confirming the serious health condition. The certification form is provided by the employer. You typically have 15 calendar days to submit it.

---

## What Employers Cannot Do

- Interfere with your right to take FMLA leave
- Retaliate against you for taking or requesting FMLA leave
- Count FMLA leave against you under attendance policies ("no-fault" attendance policies cannot penalize FMLA absences)
- Deny leave you're entitled to

If your employer interferes with your FMLA rights or retaliates against you, you can file a complaint with the Department of Labor or sue in federal court.

---

## State Leave Laws

Many states have their own family and medical leave laws that are broader than FMLA:
- Cover smaller employers
- Cover additional family relationships (siblings, grandparents, domestic partners)
- Provide longer leave periods
- Offer paid leave benefits

Check your state's laws — you may have rights your employer hasn't told you about.
