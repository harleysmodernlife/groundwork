## Unemployment Insurance

Unemployment insurance (UI) provides temporary income replacement if you lose your job through no fault of your own. It's not welfare — it's an insurance program funded by taxes on employers, specifically to support workers between jobs.

Most people don't think about unemployment until they need it. By then, understanding the rules and applying correctly matters.

---

## Who Can Collect

**Basic eligibility requirements (vary by state, but generally):**

- You must have worked recently (usually a "base period" of roughly the 12-18 months before you became unemployed)
- You must have earned enough wages during that period (minimums vary by state)
- You must be unemployed through no fault of your own
- You must be able to work and available for work
- You must be actively looking for work

---

## "Through No Fault of Your Own": What Qualifies

**Qualifies:**
- Layoff (reduction in force, position eliminated)
- Company closes
- Seasonal work ends
- Being fired for reasons unrelated to your conduct (such as not being the right fit)

**May not qualify:**
- Quitting voluntarily (with significant exceptions — see below)
- Being fired for misconduct

**Quitting:** Voluntary resignation generally disqualifies you, but many states have exceptions. Quitting for "good cause" — which may include intolerable working conditions, safety violations, being forced to relocate, a significant change in job duties or pay, or family circumstances — can preserve eligibility. Each state defines "good cause" differently.

**Fired for misconduct:** If you're fired for misconduct, you may be disqualified. But "misconduct" in the legal sense is more specific than it sounds — it typically means deliberate violation of company policy or behavior showing disregard for the employer's interests. Being fired for poor performance, not being a good fit, or making honest mistakes often doesn't rise to disqualifying misconduct.

If you're denied benefits after being fired, it's worth appealing and understanding exactly what the employer claimed as misconduct.

---

## How Much You Get

UI benefits are a percentage of your previous wages, up to a state-set maximum. Nationally, benefits typically replace 40-50% of prior earnings, capped at a maximum weekly amount that varies widely by state (roughly $200 to $800/week in most states).

Benefits typically last up to 26 weeks (about 6 months). During economic downturns, federal extended benefits programs have sometimes provided additional weeks. Check current availability in your state.

---

## How to Apply

Apply as soon as you become unemployed. Don't wait — there's usually a waiting period (one week in most states) before benefits begin, and you can't retroactively recover that time.

Apply through your state's unemployment agency website or by phone. You'll need:
- Social Security number
- Employment history for the past 18 months (employer names, addresses, dates, pay)
- Reason for separation

After applying, you'll receive a determination letter either approving or denying your claim.

---

## Continuing Requirements

Once approved, you must typically:
- Certify weekly or biweekly that you were available for work and actively job searching
- Report any earnings (part-time work can reduce but not necessarily eliminate benefits)
- Report job offers you refused (refusing a suitable job offer can disqualify you)
- Continue looking for work

---

## If You're Denied

Appeals are common and often successful. If your claim is denied:

1. **Read the denial letter carefully.** It will explain the reason.
2. **File an appeal.** The deadline is short — usually 10 to 30 days from the denial date. Don't miss it.
3. **Request a hearing.** You'll present your case to an appeals referee (a hearing officer).
4. **Gather evidence.** Documentation of how you separated from employment, any communications, and any witnesses who can speak to the circumstances.

Many initial denials are overturned on appeal — especially when employers characterize a separation inaccurately. The appeals process is administrative and accessible without an attorney, though an attorney can help in complex cases.

---

## Taxes on Benefits

Unemployment benefits are taxable income — federal income tax, and sometimes state income tax. You can have taxes withheld from your payments or pay them when you file your tax return.

If you don't withhold and don't set money aside, you may owe taxes on UI benefits when you file.

---

## Special Situations

**Self-employed / gig workers:** Traditionally not covered by UI (state UI is funded by employer payroll taxes, which self-employed people don't pay). During COVID-19, Pandemic Unemployment Assistance temporarily covered self-employed workers. Check whether your state has any coverage for independent contractors.

**Independent contractors misclassified as employees:** If you were actually an employee who was misclassified as a contractor, you may be eligible for UI based on your actual relationship with the employer.
