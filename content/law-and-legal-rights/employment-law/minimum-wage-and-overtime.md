## Minimum Wage and Overtime

Wage theft is one of the most common labor violations. Workers are underpaid, not paid for all hours worked, or denied overtime they're entitled to — often because they don't know the rules that apply to them.

---

## Federal Minimum Wage

The federal minimum wage is $7.25 per hour as of the last federal update. But this floor matters less than it might seem, because most states and many cities have higher minimums.

**The rule:** You're entitled to the higher of federal, state, or local minimum wage.

If your state minimum wage is $15/hour, your employer cannot pay you $7.25 just because that's the federal floor. The state law controls.

Look up your state and city's current minimum wage — it changes. Some states index their minimum wage to inflation and update it automatically each year.

---

## Tipped Workers

For tipped employees (servers, bartenders), federal law allows employers to pay a "tipped minimum wage" of $2.13/hour — but only if tips bring the total to at least $7.25/hour. If tips don't make up the difference, the employer must pay it.

Many states have higher tipped minimums or require full minimum wage before tips. Know your state's rule.

**Tip pooling:** Employers can require tip pooling (sharing tips among certain workers) under specific rules. Tips cannot be taken by managers or owners. Kitchen staff can be included in tip pools under certain conditions (if the employer doesn't take a tip credit).

---

## Federal Overtime: The FLSA

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) requires non-exempt employees to be paid **1.5 times their regular rate** for all hours worked over 40 in a workweek.

Key points:

**Workweek, not per day:** Federal overtime is calculated on a 7-day workweek, not daily. Working 10 hours on Monday doesn't automatically generate overtime — it depends on total hours that workweek. (Some states require daily overtime — California is the most notable, requiring time-and-a-half after 8 hours in a day.)

**Regular rate:** Overtime is calculated on your "regular rate," which can include certain bonuses and other compensation — not just your base wage.

**Off-the-clock work:** If your employer knows you're working, they have to pay you for it. Requiring employees to work before clocking in, after clocking out, during unpaid breaks, or at home without compensation violates the FLSA.

---

## Exempt vs. Non-Exempt

Not everyone is entitled to overtime. The FLSA exempts certain categories of workers.

**The big three exemptions** (executive, administrative, professional — "white collar exemptions"):
- Must be paid a salary of at least $684/week (as of the current federal threshold)
- Must primarily perform exempt duties (management, independent judgment, learned professional work)

Both the salary test AND the duties test must be met. Calling someone a "manager" and paying them $600/week doesn't make them exempt. Just paying a salary doesn't make someone exempt either.

**Other exemptions:** Outside sales employees, certain IT workers, agricultural workers, and others have specific exemption rules.

**Misclassification is common:** Employers sometimes classify workers as exempt when they don't qualify, saving the employer overtime costs. If you're being paid a salary and told you're not eligible for overtime but your job doesn't primarily involve management or professional judgment, you may be misclassified.

---

## Independent Contractors

Independent contractors are not covered by FLSA minimum wage or overtime requirements. This is why misclassifying employees as contractors (covered in a separate lesson) is profitable for employers — it eliminates these legal obligations.

---

## If You Aren't Being Paid Correctly

You have several options:

**File a complaint with the Department of Labor:** The Wage and Hour Division investigates FLSA violations. Complaints can be filed online, are confidential, and can result in back wages and penalties for the employer.

**File a state labor board complaint:** Most states have their own wage and hour agencies with often stronger protections than federal law.

**Sue:** You can sue your employer for unpaid wages. If you win, you're typically entitled to the back wages, an equal amount in liquidated damages (doubling your recovery), and attorney's fees. Class action lawsuits are common in wage theft cases.

**Retaliation is prohibited:** Your employer cannot fire you or punish you for complaining about wage violations or filing a complaint. Retaliation is a separate violation with its own remedies.

The statute of limitations for FLSA claims is typically 2 years (3 years for willful violations). Don't wait long to act.
