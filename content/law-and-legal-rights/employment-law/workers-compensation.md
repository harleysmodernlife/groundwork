## Workers' Compensation

Workers' compensation is a no-fault insurance system that provides benefits to employees who are injured on the job or develop occupational illnesses. You don't have to prove your employer was negligent — you generally just have to show the injury happened at work.

In exchange for this no-fault coverage, workers typically give up the right to sue their employer for most work injuries. Workers' comp is the exclusive remedy in most states.

---

## What Workers' Comp Covers

**Medical expenses:** All necessary medical treatment for a work-related injury or illness — doctor visits, hospital stays, surgery, physical therapy, prescription medications, medical equipment.

**Temporary disability benefits:** If your injury prevents you from working during recovery, workers' comp pays a portion of your lost wages (typically two-thirds of your average weekly wage, up to a state maximum). These benefits last while you're temporarily unable to work.

**Permanent disability benefits:** If your injury results in a permanent impairment, you may be entitled to additional compensation based on the extent of the impairment. This is often calculated using a rating system — a doctor assigns a percentage of impairment, and benefits are calculated from that.

**Vocational rehabilitation:** If your injury prevents you from returning to your former job, some states provide vocational retraining to help you qualify for different work.

**Death benefits:** If a worker dies from a work-related injury or illness, surviving dependents receive death benefits and burial expense coverage.

---

## What Counts as a Work-Related Injury

**Covered:**
- Accidents while performing job duties
- Injuries caused by repetitive motion (carpal tunnel, back injuries from repeated lifting)
- Occupational diseases caused by workplace exposure (asbestosis, hearing loss from chronic noise)
- Injuries during work-related travel (not the commute — during the workday)
- Injuries at employer-sponsored events, in some circumstances

**Often not covered:**
- Injuries during the commute to and from work (the "going and coming" rule — significant exceptions exist)
- Self-inflicted injuries
- Injuries occurring while intoxicated (in many states)
- Injuries from fighting that you started
- Injuries unrelated to work that happen at work

## The Claims Process

**Report immediately.** Most states require you to report a work injury to your employer within a specific timeframe — often 30 days, sometimes shorter. Missing this deadline can jeopardize your claim. Report in writing and keep a copy.

**Seek medical treatment.** Your employer or their insurer may have an approved list of doctors (in states that allow this). Follow the rules for your state — using an unauthorized doctor may mean the employer doesn't have to pay for treatment.

**File a claim.** Reporting to your employer isn't always the same as filing a workers' comp claim. In most states, you must also file with the state workers' compensation board or agency. Your employer should give you the necessary forms.

**Document everything.** Describe the injury in detail. Get copies of all medical records. Keep records of all communications with your employer and the insurance company.

---

## If Your Claim Is Denied

Workers' comp claims are frequently denied initially. Common reasons:
- The employer disputes that the injury was work-related
- The employer claims you missed the reporting deadline
- The insurer disputes the nature or extent of your injury

**You have the right to appeal.** The workers' comp system has an appeals process — hearings before a workers' compensation judge or board. You can present evidence and testimony.

**Consider an attorney.** Workers' compensation attorneys typically work on contingency — they take a percentage of your settlement or award. In contested cases or where significant benefits are at stake, legal representation significantly improves outcomes. Initial consultations are usually free.

---

## Common Problems

**Pressure to return to work early:** Employers and insurers sometimes push injured workers back to work before they're medically ready. Your doctor's opinion controls when you're cleared to return. Don't sign a return-to-work agreement that exceeds your medical restrictions.

**Surveillance:** Insurers sometimes have claimants surveilled or investigate social media to challenge the extent of injuries. Be accurate about your limitations and avoid activity that contradicts your reported restrictions.

**Retaliation:** Employers cannot fire you for filing a workers' comp claim. Retaliation is a separate illegal act with its own remedies.

---

## Independent Contractors

Independent contractors are generally not covered by workers' compensation. Another reason misclassification matters: if you're genuinely an employee but classified as a contractor, you may have no workers' comp coverage if you're injured. If this situation arises, consult an attorney — if you were actually an employee, you may still have a claim.
