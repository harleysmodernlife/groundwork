## Employee vs. Independent Contractor

Whether you're an employee or an independent contractor matters enormously. The classification determines whether you're entitled to minimum wage and overtime, whether the company pays half your Social Security taxes, whether you're eligible for unemployment benefits, whether you're protected by anti-discrimination laws, and much more.

Many companies prefer contractors because it's cheaper and involves fewer legal obligations. And many workers are misclassified as contractors when they should legally be employees.

---

## Why It Matters

**As an employee, you get:**
- Minimum wage and overtime protections (FLSA)
- Half of FICA (Social Security and Medicare) paid by the employer
- Workers' compensation coverage
- Unemployment insurance eligibility
- Anti-discrimination protections (Title VII, ADA, etc.)
- Family and medical leave protections (if employer is large enough)
- Employer may be required to provide benefits

**As an independent contractor, you get:**
- None of the above
- You pay both halves of FICA (self-employment tax — 15.3% vs. an employee's 7.65%)
- You handle your own health insurance, retirement, and taxes
- You're responsible for quarterly estimated tax payments
- No unemployment if the relationship ends
- No workers' comp if you're injured on the job

---

## How Classification Is Determined

There's no single test — different agencies and courts use different frameworks. The IRS, Department of Labor, and individual states all have their own tests. But they look at the same core factors.

**Behavioral control:** Does the company control how you do the work, not just what the result is?
- Do they set your hours?
- Do they tell you where and how to work?
- Do they train you in how to do the work?

More control → more likely employee.

**Financial control:** Does the company control financial aspects of your work?
- Do you have a significant investment in your own tools or facilities?
- Are you at risk of financial loss?
- Can you work for multiple clients?
- Is payment by the hour (employee-like) or by the project (contractor-like)?

More financial independence → more likely contractor.

**Type of relationship:**
- Is there a written contract calling you a contractor?
- Do you receive employee benefits?
- Is the relationship indefinite (employee-like) or for a specific project?
- Is the work you do a core part of the company's business?

---

## The ABC Test

Several states (including California) use an "ABC test" that's stricter for employers. Under this test, a worker is presumed to be an employee unless all three conditions are met:

- **A:** The worker is free from control and direction in performing the work
- **B:** The work performed is outside the usual course of the company's business
- **C:** The worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade or business

Under this test, an Uber driver is probably an employee — they're doing the core business of the company (transportation), under the company's direction. California's enforcement of this test (AB5) has been a major legal battle.

---

## Misclassification

Companies sometimes classify workers as contractors when they legally qualify as employees — either intentionally (to save costs) or through genuine confusion.

Signs you might be misclassified:
- You work exclusively or primarily for one company
- The company sets your hours, location, and methods
- You're doing the same work as employees at the company
- You've worked there for years without a specific project end date
- You have no real independent business outside this relationship

---

## If You're Misclassified

**File a complaint with the Department of Labor:** The Wage and Hour Division handles FLSA misclassification. A finding that you're an employee can result in back pay for unpaid overtime, reimbursement of FICA taxes, and penalties for the employer.

**File with your state labor agency:** Many states have more aggressive enforcement and broader tests.

**Consult an employment attorney:** Misclassification cases can be complex, but if you and others in your situation were systematically misclassified, a class action may be possible.

---

## If You're a Legitimate Contractor

Embrace the reality: you're running a small business. That means:
- Setting aside ~25-30% of income for taxes
- Making quarterly estimated tax payments
- Tracking all business expenses (they're deductible)
- Handling your own insurance and retirement
- Having clear written contracts for your engagements

The flexibility and autonomy of contract work has real value. But so does knowing the financial obligations that come with it.
