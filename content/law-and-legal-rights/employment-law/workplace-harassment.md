## Workplace Harassment

Harassment is a form of employment discrimination. It's illegal when it's based on a protected characteristic and rises to a certain level of severity. Understanding what qualifies — and what doesn't — helps you recognize real violations and respond appropriately.

---

## What Harassment Is

Legal harassment in the workplace is unwelcome conduct based on a protected characteristic (race, sex, national origin, religion, disability, age, etc.) that is either:

**Quid pro quo:** "Something for something." A supervisor makes employment benefits (job, promotion, assignment) conditional on tolerating or submitting to unwelcome conduct. Example: a supervisor implies that a raise depends on sexual favors. One instance can be enough for quid pro quo harassment.

**Hostile work environment:** Conduct that is severe or pervasive enough to create an intimidating, hostile, or abusive work environment for a reasonable person. This requires more than one or a few offhand comments — the conduct must meaningfully alter the conditions of employment.

---

## What Harassment Is Not

This is important to understand to avoid confusion about what the law actually addresses:

- Rude, unfair, or harsh treatment that isn't based on a protected characteristic
- A boss who's difficult, demanding, or unreasonable in general
- One offensive comment (in most cases) — doesn't meet "severe or pervasive"
- General workplace rudeness, favoritism, or unfairness unconnected to protected characteristics
- Conflict between coworkers that doesn't involve protected characteristics

The law doesn't guarantee a pleasant or fair workplace. It prohibits harassment based on protected characteristics that rises to a legal threshold.

---

## Severity and Pervasiveness

A single severe incident (sexual assault, a physical attack combined with a racial slur) can constitute harassment. More commonly, harassment consists of repeated, less severe incidents that collectively create a hostile environment.

Courts look at:
- Frequency of conduct
- Severity of conduct
- Whether it's physically threatening or humiliating vs. offensive utterance
- Whether it unreasonably interferes with work performance

"I get dirty looks sometimes" probably doesn't reach the threshold. Repeated racial slurs, ongoing sexual comments, or persistent threatening behavior probably does.

---

## Who Can Harass

The harasser doesn't have to be a supervisor. Coworker harassment and customer/third-party harassment can all create employer liability if the employer knew or should have known about it and failed to take reasonable corrective action.

**Supervisor harassment:** Employers are automatically liable for quid pro quo harassment by supervisors. For hostile environment harassment by supervisors, employers can sometimes escape liability if they had an effective anti-harassment policy and the employee unreasonably failed to use it.

**Coworker harassment:** Employer is liable if they knew or should have known and didn't respond reasonably.

---

## What to Do

**Document:** Write down what happened, when, who did it, whether anyone witnessed it. Save any emails, messages, or other evidence.

**Report internally:** Use your employer's complaint procedures — typically HR or a designated harassment hotline. Do this in writing (email) so there's a record. Even if you don't expect the company to handle it well, internal reporting is often required before or alongside external reporting, and creates legal protections.

**File with the EEOC:** For federal harassment claims, file a charge with the EEOC within 180 or 300 days (depending on your state) of the harassing conduct. The EEOC will investigate and issue a right-to-sue letter.

**Retaliation is illegal:** Your employer cannot punish you for reporting harassment or filing an EEOC charge. Retaliation is a separate violation.

**Consult an attorney:** Harassment cases have procedural traps (EEOC deadlines) and legal nuances. An employment attorney can advise you on your specific situation, often in a free initial consultation.

---

## Sexual Harassment: Specific Notes

Sexual harassment — the most commonly recognized type — includes:
- Unwelcome sexual advances or comments
- Requests for sexual favors
- Physical conduct of a sexual nature
- Offensive sexual jokes or materials in the workplace

This applies regardless of gender — men can be harassed by women or by other men. Same-sex harassment is prohibited.

**"Welcome" vs. "unwelcome":** The conduct must be unwelcome. Context matters. But the test is not whether you objected in the moment — fear of retaliation or power dynamics can explain silence. What matters is whether the conduct was unwelcome from your perspective.

---

## The Reality

Many people who experience harassment don't report it — fear of retaliation, not being believed, or career damage. These fears are understandable and not always wrong. But:

- Retaliation is separately illegal and can itself be the basis of a claim
- Not reporting can limit your legal options later (some defenses available to employers require showing you didn't use complaint procedures)
- Documentation and internal reporting create the record that supports any future claim

You're not required to endure harassment. The law exists specifically to address it.
