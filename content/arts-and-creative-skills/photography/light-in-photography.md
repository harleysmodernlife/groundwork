## Working with Light

Photography is, literally, writing with light. Every creative decision you make — lens choice, composition, timing — happens in service of capturing a particular quality of light on a sensor. Learning to see light, not just subjects, is the primary perceptual shift that separates photographers from people with cameras.

---

## The Properties of Light

Light has four properties that affect your photographs. All four vary based on the light source, distance, and conditions.

### Quality: Hard vs. Soft

**Hard light** comes from a small, distant source relative to the subject. The sun on a clear day is hard light. Hard light creates:
- Crisp, defined shadows with sharp edges
- High contrast
- Strong texture emphasis (side-lit rock, fabric, skin texture)
- Specular highlights (bright glints on shiny surfaces)

**Soft light** comes from a large source relative to the subject. An overcast sky is a giant soft box. Soft light creates:
- Gentle, diffused shadows with soft edges
- Low contrast
- Flattering skin tones and reduced texture
- Even, wrapping illumination

A bare light bulb is a small, hard source. The same bulb shining through a large white diffusion panel becomes a soft source. The physics: the larger the source relative to the subject, the softer the light. Moving a light source closer (without changing its size) makes it softer; moving it farther makes it harder.

### Direction

**Front lighting:** Light source behind the camera, hitting the subject head-on. Even exposure, minimum shadows. Works for documentation; produces flat, textureless images. Passport photos are front-lit.

**Side lighting (45°, 90°):** Light from the side. Creates the shadow patterns that reveal three-dimensional form. Texture reads strongly because light grazes across the surface. Classic for portrait "Rembrandt lighting," landscape photography emphasizing terrain, macro photography of textured objects.

**Back lighting:** Light source behind the subject, facing the camera. Creates rim light (glowing edges), silhouettes, and separation from the background. The subject may be underexposed unless you expose for them specifically. Atmospheric and emotionally charged.

**Top lighting:** Light directly above (midday sun, overhead studio lights). Creates unflattering shadows under eyes and nose on portraits. Harsh and generally avoided for faces. Sometimes useful for product photography, flat lays, certain fashion looks.

**Low angle lighting:** Light from below, as if from a fire or a light on the floor. Rarely occurs in nature; feels uncanny and dramatic. Usually unnatural and slightly unsettling.

### Color Temperature

Light has color. Measured in Kelvin (K):

- 2000K — candlelight: deep orange-red
- 2700K — incandescent/tungsten: warm yellow-orange
- 3200K — studio tungsten lights
- 4000K — fluorescent (cool white): neutral with green cast
- 5500K — daylight, noon sun: neutral
- 6500K — overcast sky: slightly blue
- 8000K+ — deep shade, open blue sky: distinctly blue

**White balance** tells the camera what neutral looks like under the current light. If you set white balance to "daylight" but shoot indoors under tungsten lights, your images will be orange. Set white balance correctly and the camera adjusts the image to render whites as white.

**Auto white balance (AWB)** works well in most conditions. Where it fails: scenes where the entire frame is one color temperature (the camera has nothing "neutral" to reference), and mixed lighting sources (tungsten room lights + daylight from a window).

**Shooting RAW:** If you shoot RAW, white balance is adjustable in post-processing without any quality loss. White balance is baked in at capture for JPEG.

**Creative use of color temperature:** Sometimes "wrong" white balance creates the right feeling. Letting tungsten light go warm for indoor scenes feels cozy. Letting open shade go blue in portraits feels cool and melancholic. The "correct" white balance is not always the right choice.

### Intensity

More light = more exposure options. Bright conditions let you use faster shutter speeds, narrower apertures, and lower ISOs. Low light forces compromises: slower shutter (motion blur risk), wider aperture (shallower depth of field), or higher ISO (noise).

Intensity changes through the day and changes with weather. Photographers plan around these changes.

## Natural Light Through the Day

### Golden Hour

The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset. The sun is low on the horizon, producing:
- Warm orange-gold color (2500-3500K)
- Long shadows that reveal texture and dimension
- Soft, directional side or back lighting
- Dramatic rim lighting possibilities

This is why almost every landscape photographer wakes before dawn. The light quality is categorically different from midday. The same location at 7am and at noon is, photographically, a different location.

### Blue Hour

The 20-30 minutes before sunrise and after sunset. The sky illuminates a deep blue without the sun visible. Ambient light is balanced with interior and artificial lights — this is when city scenes and interior/exterior combinations look most balanced. Very short window.

### Midday (Harsh Sun)

10am–4pm on a clear day. High, hard light. Generally unflattering for people and landscapes. Options:
- **Find shade.** Open shade (shade next to a bright surface) provides soft, even light with wrap from the bright reflected fill. North-facing doorways on a sunny day are excellent portrait locations.
- **Wait.** If the subject permits, come back at golden hour.
- **Use it intentionally.** Harsh top light can be the right choice for certain subjects — emphasizing heat, harshness, desolation.

### Overcast

An overcast sky is a giant softbox. Even, diffused light from above. Excellent for:
- Portrait photography (no squinting, even skin tones)
- Forest photography (no patches of blown-out sky showing through canopy)
- Detail and product photography (no specular reflections)

Color tends toward neutral-cool. Overcast light is often underrated by beginners who wait for the sun and go home when it clouds over.

## Artificial Light

### Reading Existing Light Indoors

Window light is directional, soft (the window is a large source relative to the subject), and changes with time of day and weather. A north-facing window provides consistent, cool, diffused light. A south or west window produces warm directional light in afternoon.

The distance from the window determines intensity. The falloff follows the inverse square law: doubling the distance from the source reduces the light to one quarter. Move two feet farther from the window, and exposure drops significantly.

### Mixing Artificial Sources

Rooms with multiple light sources at different color temperatures (tungsten ceiling lights + fluorescent kitchen lights + window light) create mixed color casts that no single white balance corrects. Solutions:
- Turn off competing sources
- Match sources with gels on artificial lights
- Choose one dominant source and accept minor casts from others
- Shoot RAW and correct selectively in post

### Flash

On-camera flash is a small, hard source directly in line with the lens — producing flat, harsh, deer-in-headlights light. Used because it works, not because it's flattering.

**Bounce flash:** Aim the flash head at the ceiling or a nearby wall. The large reflected surface becomes the effective light source — big, soft, directional. On-camera bounce flash is dramatically better than direct flash.

**Off-camera flash:** Separate the flash from the lens axis. Even a single flash moved to the side and elevated creates three-dimensional light that reveals form. This is the foundation of studio lighting.

**Fill flash:** A lower-powered flash used to fill in shadows without becoming the dominant light. Useful outdoors to fill face shadows when the sun is behind the subject, or to add a catchlight to eyes in shade.

## Seeing Light

The perceptual shift: stop seeing subjects and start seeing the light on subjects. When you walk into a room, notice the quality of the light before noticing the room's contents. Ask:
- Where is the light coming from?
- Is it hard or soft?
- What color is it?
- Where are the shadows?
- What parts of the scene are brightly lit vs. dark?

Practice observing light everywhere. Notice how a single window lights a room. Notice how the quality changes as a cloud passes over the sun. Notice how the light in a restaurant is different from office light is different from parking garage light.

**The decisive moment for landscape photography** is often about light, not subject. The same mountain looks mediocre at noon and extraordinary at dawn when light rakes across it. Timing is a compositional decision.

## Modifying Light

### Reflectors

A white, silver, or gold reflective surface bounced near the subject redirects ambient or direct light onto shadow areas. A folding 5-in-1 reflector (white, silver, gold, translucent diffusion, black) is the most useful piece of gear for outdoor portrait photography. It requires no power source and weighs almost nothing.

- **White:** Soft fill with neutral color
- **Silver:** Brighter fill, slightly cool
- **Gold:** Warm fill, mimics late-afternoon light
- **Black:** Subtracts light; deepens shadows

### Diffusion

Placing a translucent material between a hard light source and the subject softens the light by effectively enlarging the source. A white sheet, a large piece of translucent acrylic, or a dedicated diffusion panel all work.

### Shade and Natural Diffusion

Trees, buildings, and overhangs create large diffused light areas outdoors. Positioning your subject in open shade — where the sky is the light source rather than direct sun — gives soft, even light for free.

## Exposure for Tricky Light

### High Contrast Scenes

When the range from shadows to highlights exceeds the sensor's dynamic range, something has to give — either shadows go too dark or highlights blow out. Options:
- **Expose for the subject.** Decide what matters: the face of a backlit person (expose for the face and accept a blown sky) or the sky (expose for the sky and accept a silhouetted person).
- **Wait for better light.** Less contrast comes at dawn, dusk, or overcast conditions.
- **HDR bracketing.** Shoot multiple exposures and blend them. Works for static scenes.
- **Graduated ND filter.** A darker-on-top filter that reduces sky brightness relative to the foreground. Used in landscape photography to hold both sky and ground in one exposure.

### Exposing for Backlit Subjects

In backlit conditions, the camera's meter sees the bright background and underexposes the subject. Override with exposure compensation (+1 to +2 stops) or use spot metering aimed at the subject's face.

The result: the background may blow out, but the subject is correctly exposed. This is often the right tradeoff — a slightly bright sky with a well-lit face reads better than a perfectly exposed sky with a dark silhouette of a face.
