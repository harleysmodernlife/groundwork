## Focus and Depth of Field

Focus determines what's sharp in a photograph. Depth of field determines how much of the scene is sharp. These are related but distinct concepts, and controlling them intentionally — rather than accepting whatever the camera decides — is one of the most powerful tools in photography.

---

## How Focus Works

A camera lens focuses light from a specific distance onto the sensor plane. Objects at that exact distance are in focus — rendered with maximum sharpness. Objects in front of or behind that distance are out of focus — their light is spread across multiple sensor points, creating a blur.

**Circle of confusion:** The blur spot created by an out-of-focus point. When the circle of confusion is small enough that it appears sharp to the human eye at normal viewing distance, we consider it "acceptably sharp." Depth of field is defined by the range of distances that fall within this acceptable sharpness threshold.

---

## Depth of Field: The Three Controls

Three factors determine how much or how little of a scene is in acceptably sharp focus.

### 1. Aperture

**The primary depth-of-field control.** Wide aperture (small f-number) → shallow depth of field. Narrow aperture (large f-number) → deep depth of field.

- f/1.4: Very shallow. A portrait might have sharp eyes while the ears are soft.
- f/2.8: Shallow. Common for portraits with separated backgrounds.
- f/5.6: Moderate. Good for small product shots.
- f/8: Moderate-deep. Most landscapes.
- f/16: Very deep. Everything sharp from a few feet to infinity.

### 2. Distance to Subject

**Closer subjects produce shallower depth of field.** This is why macro photography has such extremely shallow depth of field — you're inches from the subject. Photographing someone across the room gives more depth of field than photographing the same person at arm's length, even at the same aperture.

Conversely, far subjects (a mountain range, a cityscape) have deep depth of field naturally — the near/far distances are so far from the camera that both are well beyond the focus point's depth of field range.

### 3. Focal Length

**Longer focal lengths compress depth of field.** A 200mm lens at f/4 has shallower depth of field than a 24mm lens at f/4, for the same framing. This is partly why telephoto lenses are popular for portraits — they compress depth of field, separating subjects from backgrounds.

The relationship is more nuanced: if you maintain the same subject size in frame (moving farther away with a wider lens), the depth of field difference mostly disappears. The practical experience is that a longer lens gives shallower depth of field at the same shooting position.

## Autofocus Systems

Modern cameras have sophisticated autofocus systems. Understanding how they work helps you get more out of them.

### Phase Detection AF

**How it works:** The sensor reads light coming from slightly different angles and detects whether focus needs to move forward or backward, and by how much. This makes it fast and predictive.

**Where it's used:** Modern mirrorless cameras use on-sensor phase detection across most of the frame. DSLRs use phase detection in a dedicated AF sensor (which is why DSLR AF works well only in the dedicated AF point zones).

**Best for:** Action, sports, moving subjects. Phase detection tracks and predicts motion.

### Contrast Detection AF

**How it works:** The camera moves focus while looking for peak contrast — maximum sharpness. It's slower because it has to "hunt" for the right point.

**Where it's used:** Older mirrorless cameras, compact cameras, smartphones (though most modern phones use phase detection now).

**Best for:** Static subjects where speed isn't critical. More accurate than phase detection for stationary subjects on some systems.

### Hybrid AF

Most modern mirrorless cameras use hybrid systems — phase detection for speed, contrast detection to confirm and fine-tune. This gives fast acquisition with accurate final position.

## AF Modes

### Single AF (AF-S / One-Shot)

Camera focuses once when you half-press the shutter, then locks. If the subject moves, focus doesn't follow. Good for static subjects — landscapes, architecture, portraits of still subjects.

### Continuous AF (AF-C / AI Servo)

Camera continuously adjusts focus while you hold down the shutter button (or AF-On button with back-button AF). Predicts where a moving subject will be when the shutter fires. Good for action, sports, birds in flight, any moving subject.

### Back-Button AF

By default, half-pressing the shutter activates both autofocus and the exposure meter, with the shutter button completing the exposure. Many photographers separate these: assign AF activation to a button on the back of the camera (usually AF-On), so the shutter button only triggers the exposure.

The advantage: you can switch instantly between tracking focus (hold AF-On) and locking focus (release AF-On and the focus stays where it was). No mode switching required. Most working photographers use back-button AF once they get used to it.

---

## AF Area Modes

These control how much of the frame the camera uses to find its focus subject.

**Single point:** You tell the camera exactly which point to use. Most reliable for precise control. Use when you have time to place the point correctly.

**Zone / Group:** A cluster of AF points works together. Good for tracking subjects with some erratic movement.

**Wide area / Multi:** The camera uses the full frame and chooses what to focus on. Usually selects the nearest subject, or with eye/face detection, the nearest face. Good for documentary, street photography, situations where you're reacting quickly.

**Eye AF / Subject tracking:** Modern camera AI that identifies people (or animals, vehicles) and keeps focus locked on the eye. Sony, Canon, Nikon, Fuji all have capable implementations. When it works, it's remarkable — tracking a person through a crowd, maintaining eye focus as they turn their head. Use it for portrait and people photography.

## Manual Focus

Sometimes autofocus fails or is too slow:

- Shooting through glass or a fence
- Very low contrast subjects (blue sky, blank wall)
- Macro photography (AF hunts continuously)
- Situations where you want to pre-focus on a specific point

**Manual focus technique:**

**Magnified view:** Live view magnification (digital zoom into the viewfinder or LCD) lets you confirm precise focus on a specific detail. Zoom to 5x or 10x, focus until sharp, zoom back out.

**Focus peaking:** Many cameras highlight the in-focus edges with a colored outline (usually red, white, or yellow) when in manual focus. Fast and usable while looking through the viewfinder.

**Distance scale:** Most manual lenses have a distance scale on the barrel. You can set focus to a specific distance and shoot without looking through the camera. Useful for street photography (zone focus).

---

## Zone Focusing

A technique that trades some precision for speed. Set focus to a specific distance and use a narrow aperture to create enough depth of field that anything within a certain range is acceptably sharp. Shoot without looking through the camera, or pre-focus before the decisive moment.

Example: 28mm lens, f/8, focused at 10 feet. Depth of field at these settings extends from roughly 6 feet to infinity — everything in that range is sharp. Walk around and shoot without focusing, knowing everything beyond a few feet will be sharp.

Used extensively in street photography and photojournalism.

---

## Focus Stacking

For extreme macro photography or landscape work where you need deep depth of field but can't achieve it with aperture alone (without diffraction limits):

Take multiple exposures at the same focal point but focused at different depths. Merge them in software (Photoshop, Helicon Focus, Zerene Stacker) by selecting the sharpest regions from each image. Produces images with sharpness that no single aperture setting could achieve.

## Common Focus Problems

**Front focus / back focus:** The camera consistently focuses in front of or behind the intended point. Usually a lens calibration issue. Modern cameras have AF fine-tune settings in the menu. Mirrorless cameras using on-sensor phase detection have fewer calibration issues than DSLRs.

**Focus hunting:** The lens searches back and forth and can't settle on a focus point. Causes: insufficient contrast (low light, blank surfaces), competing subjects, subject too close for minimum focus distance.

**Missed focus on fast action:** Continuous AF tracks well, but the camera fires between the tracking updates. Use burst mode (continuous shooting) and select the sharpest frame. Also check that your camera's tracking sensitivity matches the situation — higher sensitivity means faster response but can lose the subject on brief obstructions.

**Focus on wrong subject:** The camera chose a background element or the wrong person. Switch to single-point AF to specify exactly what to focus on, or use eye AF with subject selection.

---

## Depth of Field Preview

Most cameras have a depth-of-field preview button. The lens normally stays at its maximum aperture until you shoot (for a bright viewfinder). Pressing depth-of-field preview stops the lens down to your set aperture, showing you the actual depth of field you'll get. The viewfinder darkens, but you can see what will and won't be sharp.

Use it before shooting when depth of field matters — portrait backgrounds, landscapes, close-ups.

---

## Hyperfocal Distance

The hyperfocal distance is the closest focusing distance at which infinity is within the depth of field. Set focus to the hyperfocal distance and everything from half that distance to infinity is sharp.

It varies by focal length and aperture. For a 28mm lens at f/11, the hyperfocal distance is roughly 5 feet — focus there and everything from 2.5 feet to infinity is sharp.

Useful for landscape and documentary photography where you want maximum depth of field with a single focus point. Apps like PhotoPills calculate hyperfocal distance for any lens/aperture combination.
