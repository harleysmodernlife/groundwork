## Finding Your Photographic Eye

Technical competence is necessary but not sufficient. You can expose correctly, focus accurately, and compose by every rule in the book, and still make photographs that say nothing. Developing a photographic eye — a personal vision that makes your images distinctively yours — is the longer, more interesting work.

---

## What a Photographic Eye Actually Is

A photographic eye is not a special talent some people are born with. It's the accumulation of:

- **Visual preferences** — what you're drawn to, what you find compelling
- **Noticing habits** — training yourself to see subjects others walk past
- **Technical fluency** — knowing how the camera will render what you see, so you can see potential images rather than just scenes
- **A point of view** — something you're actually interested in

The last one is the most important and the most neglected. Photographers who are curious about the world — about people, places, light, time, texture, the strange specific details of ordinary life — make better photographs than technically flawless photographers who are pointing the camera at whatever is in front of them.

---

## What You're Actually Photographing

Most photographers spend years looking for subjects without understanding what they're actually after. Here's a useful distinction:

**Subject** — what the image is of: a person, a landscape, a building, a flower.

**Photograph** — what the image is about: time, loss, beauty, humor, connection, scale, isolation, the specific quality of light at that moment.

The subject is a vehicle for the photograph. The best photographers know what they're looking for in terms of meaning or feeling, and they find subjects that carry it.

You don't have to be articulate about this. Many photographers work intuitively, driven by a sense that an image "feels right" without being able to say why. But spending time asking "what am I actually interested in?" is worth it.

---

## Volume and Attention

The fastest way to develop your eye is to make a lot of photographs and pay attention to what works.

**Volume:** Shoot more than you think you need to. The ratio of failures to successes for working photographers is high. Henri Cartier-Bresson — one of the greatest photographers who ever lived — carried one camera and shot selectively, but he was doing this after decades of intensive looking. You're not there yet. Shoot.

**Attention to your own work:** After each session, look at what you made. Not just at whether the images are technically correct, but at what you were drawn to photograph. What subjects? What qualities of light? What kinds of moments? Patterns emerge. Following those patterns is how you find your interests.

**The editing process (selection):** Professional photographers often return from a full day of shooting with 200-500 images and select 5-10. Ruthless selection clarifies what was actually working. If you keep everything, you can't see the difference between your best and worst work.

## Studying Other Photographers

Looking at photography — a lot of it, seriously — is training. Not for imitation, but for expanding your understanding of what's possible.

**How to study a photograph:**
- What is this image about?
- Where does my eye go first? Why?
- What's the light source? What quality?
- What is the relationship between the subject and the background?
- What's in the frame that doesn't need to be? What's excluded?
- Why was this moment and not another?
- What would I have done differently?

Looking this closely at many photographs builds your visual vocabulary — a library of solutions, approaches, and possibilities you can draw on when you're in the field.

**Photographers to study** (starting points, not a canon):
- Henri Cartier-Bresson — decisive moment, street photography
- Dorothea Lange — documentary, human dignity
- Ansel Adams — landscape, tonal control
- Vivian Maier — street photography, self-taught
- Saul Leiter — color, abstraction, the street as painting
- Martin Parr — color, satire, British life
- Diane Arbus — portraiture, outsiders, psychological intensity
- Gordon Parks — documentary, civil rights, humanity
- Josef Koudelka — displacement, exile, drama
- Sebastião Salgado — social documentary, epic scale

Study photographers who interest you, not just the ones on every "must-know" list. Find people making images that make you feel something.

## Projects and Constraints

Single, isolated images are useful for learning technique. Projects — a series of images connected by subject, place, idea, or approach — develop your eye much faster.

**Why projects work:**
- Returning to the same subject forces you to find new approaches
- Constraints (one location, one lens, one theme) eliminate infinite choice and force creative problem-solving
- Sequences of images reveal relationships and patterns invisible in individual images
- Having a project gives you a reason to photograph when you otherwise wouldn't

**Simple project ideas:**
- Photograph your neighborhood daily for a month
- Make portraits of strangers (if you can get comfortable doing this)
- Document one person or place over time
- Shoot only in one light condition (golden hour, overcast, night)
- Work with a single focal length for three months
- Photograph everything within walking distance of your home

The constraint is not a limitation — it's a frame that makes the work possible.

---

## The One-Lens Exercise

Commit to a single focal length for an extended period — ideally 3-6 months. One lens, nothing else.

The effect: you stop making focal length decisions and start seeing only in terms of what that lens shows. You learn exactly what 35mm (or 50mm, or 28mm) looks like — the angle of view, the spatial relationships, the way backgrounds fall off. You learn to work within those constraints, moving your feet instead of zooming.

Most photographers who do this come out with stronger images than before, because they're no longer shopping for focal lengths and instead fully inhabiting one perspective.

## The Gap Between Vision and Ability

Ira Glass, the radio producer, described a problem that applies directly to photography:

"When you first start, your taste is better than your ability. You know what good work looks and sounds like. You can see that your own work doesn't match that standard. This is normal. The only way to close the gap is volume — making a lot of work, more than feels comfortable."

The gap is real. You will see images in your mind's eye that your hands and camera can't yet produce. This is not failure. This is having taste, which is the prerequisite for good work. Keep shooting. The technical ability catches up with the vision if you keep at it.

## Developing a Consistent Practice

A photographic eye develops through consistent practice, not occasional bursts.

**Carry a camera always.** The best camera is the one you have with you. Your phone is a camera. The images you don't take because you didn't have a camera are lost. Carry something.

**Photograph what's around you.** The impulse to wait for exotic travel or unusual subjects before photographing seriously is a trap. The world immediately around you is full of images. Photographers who can work anywhere — in their kitchen, their neighborhood, the grocery store parking lot — develop faster than photographers who wait for the right trip.

**Sketchbook equivalent:** Some photographers keep a visual journal — not necessarily photography but visual notes, things that caught their eye, compositional sketches. The habit of looking and recording, in whatever form, builds the seeing muscle.

**Review your work regularly.** Monthly review: pull together the best images from the past month. What's working? What's missing? What do you want to try? Regular review connects your shooting to intentional development.

## On Originality

Beginning photographers often worry about originality — making images that look like everyone else's, using the same compositions and subjects they've seen on social media.

The concern is legitimate, but the solution isn't to consciously avoid what you've seen. Originality comes from specificity. The more specifically you look at what's in front of you — not "a tree" but this exact tree at this exact moment in this exact light — the less generic your images become.

The photographer who is genuinely curious about the world, who looks carefully at specific things and specific moments, produces specific images. Specific images are original because no one else was in exactly that place with exactly that curiosity at exactly that moment.

**Your specific life is your subject.** Your neighborhood, your relationships, your recurring experiences, your interests — these are uniquely yours. Photographs made from genuine curiosity about your own world are more interesting than technically excellent photographs made of subjects chosen because they photograph well.

---

## Patience and Persistence

Some days, nothing works. You go out with the camera and come home with nothing worth keeping. This is part of it.

The photographers who develop strong work show up consistently. They photograph when it's inconvenient, when they're tired, when the light isn't special, when nothing seems to be happening. They show up enough times that they're there when something does happen.

Photography rewards persistence more than talent. A person of modest natural talent who photographs attentively every day for a year will make better images than a talented person who photographed twice and stopped. This is true of almost every creative discipline, but it's particularly true of photography, where so much depends on being present.

Show up. Keep shooting. Pay attention to what works. Study the work of people who inspire you. Find what you're actually interested in and photograph that. Everything else follows from this.
