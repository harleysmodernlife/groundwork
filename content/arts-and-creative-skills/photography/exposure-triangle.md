## The Exposure Triangle: Aperture, Shutter, ISO

Every photographic exposure is controlled by three variables: aperture, shutter speed, and ISO. They're called the exposure triangle because they're interdependent — changing any one affects the others, and all three together determine how bright or dark your image comes out. Understanding how they interact is the difference between guessing and knowing.

---

## What Exposure Means

An image is properly exposed when the subject is rendered at the brightness you intended — not so dark that detail is lost in the shadows, not so bright that highlights are blown out to white. The histogram tells you objectively whether you've achieved this.

The camera's metering system tries to calculate the right exposure automatically. It's often right, but it makes assumptions — primarily that the scene averages to a medium gray. A field of snow will be underexposed. A black cat on a black background will be overexposed. Learning the triangle lets you override the camera's assumptions intentionally.

**Exposure Value (EV):** A single number representing a specific combination of aperture, shutter speed, and ISO that produces a given brightness. Any combination that gives the same EV produces the same exposure — even though the images will look different in other ways (depth of field, motion blur, noise).

---

## Aperture

The aperture is an iris inside the lens that opens or closes to control how much light passes through. It's measured in f-stops.

### F-Stop Numbers

F-stops are counterintuitive: **small f-number = large opening = more light.**

- f/1.4 — wide open, maximum light
- f/2.8 — wide
- f/4 — moderate
- f/5.6 — moderate
- f/8 — moderate-narrow
- f/11 — narrow
- f/16 — very narrow, minimum light on most lenses

Each full stop either doubles or halves the light. Going from f/2.8 to f/4 lets in half as much light. Going from f/8 to f/5.6 doubles the light.

The f-number is a ratio: focal length divided by aperture diameter. An f/2.8 aperture on a 50mm lens has a physical opening of about 18mm. On a 200mm lens, f/2.8 requires a 71mm opening — which is why long telephoto lenses are physically large and expensive.

### Aperture and Depth of Field

Aperture is the primary control for **depth of field** — how much of the image is in sharp focus from front to back.

**Wide aperture (f/1.8, f/2.8):** Shallow depth of field. The focused subject is sharp; background blurs into smooth bokeh. Common for portraits, food photography, any subject where you want the background separated from the foreground.

**Narrow aperture (f/11, f/16):** Deep depth of field. Everything from foreground to background is sharp. Common for landscapes, architecture, group shots.

**The sweet spot:** Most lenses are sharpest 2-3 stops down from wide open. An f/1.4 lens is often sharpest at f/4-f/5.6. For maximum sharpness without a depth-of-field reason to go wider or narrower, f/5.6-f/8 is usually the answer.

**Diffraction:** At very narrow apertures (f/16, f/22), diffraction softens the image even though depth of field is maximized. For critical sharpness, avoid the smallest apertures unless you specifically need the depth of field.

## Shutter Speed

The shutter controls how long the sensor is exposed to light. The duration is the shutter speed.

### Shutter Speed Values

Shutter speeds are expressed as fractions of a second (or whole seconds for long exposures):

- 1/8000 sec — freezes a hummingbird's wingbeat
- 1/1000 sec — freezes most fast action
- 1/500 sec — freezes a running person
- 1/250 sec — freezes moderate motion
- 1/125 sec — general handheld use
- 1/60 sec — minimum for handheld with a 50mm lens (no IS)
- 1/30 sec — handheld, requires steady technique or IS
- 1/15 sec — handheld becomes difficult; motion blur likely
- 1 sec — subject must be completely still; use a tripod
- 30 sec — long exposure; light trails, star photography

Each full stop doubles or halves the exposure duration.

### Shutter Speed and Motion

**Fast shutter speed** freezes motion — water drops in mid-air, athletes, birds in flight. Use when you want to capture a moment frozen in time.

**Slow shutter speed** allows motion blur — silky waterfalls, light trails from cars at night, intentional blur to show movement. Use when you want to show motion rather than stop it.

**Camera shake:** Even without subject motion, a slow shutter speed records the vibration of your hands holding the camera. The traditional rule: minimum handheld shutter speed is 1/focal length. For a 50mm lens, don't go below 1/50 sec. For a 200mm lens, don't go below 1/200 sec. Image stabilization (IS/VR/OSS) adds 3-5 stops of leeway — a 200mm lens with stabilization might be usable at 1/30 sec.

**Panning:** With a moving subject, track it with the camera during a slow exposure. The subject stays sharp against a motion-blurred background. Takes practice.

## ISO

ISO measures the sensor's sensitivity to light. Higher ISO lets you shoot in darker conditions.

### ISO Values

- ISO 100 — base ISO, lowest noise, needs the most light
- ISO 200 — 1 stop more sensitive than 100
- ISO 400 — good daylight ISO
- ISO 800 — indoor, cloudy conditions
- ISO 1600 — low light
- ISO 3200 — dim light, some noise visible
- ISO 6400 — dark conditions, noise increasing
- ISO 12800+ — very dark, noise significant

Each full stop doubles the sensor's sensitivity.

### ISO and Noise

Higher ISO amplifies the sensor signal — which also amplifies noise (visible grain or speckle in the image). **The cost of high ISO is noise.** Modern cameras handle high ISO much better than cameras of 10 years ago. A current full-frame camera at ISO 6400 produces less noise than an older camera at ISO 1600.

**Base ISO** is the camera's native sensitivity — the ISO at which the sensor performs at its best. This is usually ISO 100 or ISO 200 depending on the camera. Always shoot at the lowest ISO the conditions allow.

**Auto ISO:** Most cameras offer Auto ISO, which adjusts ISO automatically within limits you set. This is useful for run-and-gun shooting where you want to control aperture and shutter speed yourself while letting the camera adjust sensitivity for changing light conditions. Set a maximum ISO (e.g., 6400) and a minimum shutter speed.

## The Triangle in Practice

The three controls work together. For a given lighting situation, you have a target exposure — a specific total amount of light needed. You can achieve that exposure through many combinations of aperture, shutter speed, and ISO. The choice between combinations depends on the creative effect you want.

**Example:** You're photographing a moving subject in a dim room. Your meter says you need more light.

- Open the aperture (f/4 → f/2): doubles the light, adds bokeh, reduces depth of field
- Slow the shutter (1/250 → 1/125): doubles the light, risks motion blur
- Raise the ISO (400 → 800): doubles sensitivity, adds some noise

Or use all three in combination: open a little, slow a little, raise ISO a little — trading depth of field and motion sharpness for slightly less noise than maxing out ISO alone.

## Exposure Modes

Your camera's mode dial controls which variables are automatic and which are manual.

**Manual (M):** You set aperture, shutter speed, and ISO. Full control. The meter tells you if you're over or underexposed. Best when lighting is consistent and you want precise, repeatable results.

**Aperture Priority (Av/A):** You set the aperture and ISO; the camera sets the shutter speed. Use when depth of field is your priority. The most useful semi-automatic mode for most photographers.

**Shutter Priority (Tv/S):** You set the shutter speed and ISO; the camera sets the aperture. Use when motion control is your priority — sports, action, waterfalls.

**Program (P):** Camera sets both aperture and shutter speed. You can still shift the combination (program shift). Good starting point when you're learning.

**Auto:** Camera controls everything. Fine for snapshots, limits creative control.

---

## Exposure Compensation

In Av, Tv, or P modes, the camera's meter aims for a "correct" exposure based on its middle-gray assumption. When the scene is predominantly bright (snow, beach) or dark, the meter is wrong.

**Exposure compensation** shifts the camera's metered exposure up or down in stops:

- **+1 EV:** Brighter. Use for scenes with lots of white/bright tones.
- **-1 EV:** Darker. Use for scenes with lots of dark tones.

The dial or button is usually marked with a +/- symbol. Use it constantly — second-guessing the meter is not a failure, it's how you control the image.

---

## Stop Equivalents

| Adjustment | Effect |
|---|---|
| Open aperture by 1 stop (e.g., f/4 → f/2.8) | Double the light |
| Close aperture by 1 stop (e.g., f/2.8 → f/4) | Halve the light |
| Double the shutter duration (e.g., 1/250 → 1/125) | Double the light |
| Halve the shutter duration (e.g., 1/125 → 1/250) | Halve the light |
| Double the ISO (e.g., ISO 400 → 800) | Double the sensitivity |
| Halve the ISO (e.g., ISO 800 → 400) | Halve the sensitivity |

Stops are stops — one stop of aperture equals one stop of shutter speed equals one stop of ISO in terms of exposure effect. This equivalence is what makes the triangle work.

## Putting It Together

The practical sequence when you encounter a new shooting situation:

1. **What's the priority?** Freeze motion? Control depth of field? Minimize noise?
2. **Set the priority control first.** If depth of field matters most, set aperture. If motion matters most, set shutter speed.
3. **Set ISO** to the lowest value that still lets you get the shutter speed and aperture you need.
4. **Check the exposure.** Read the meter or look at the histogram. Adjust as needed.
5. **Check the image.** Does it look the way you intended? Adjust.

Repeat until it becomes automatic — which takes a few hundred shots, not a few thousand.
