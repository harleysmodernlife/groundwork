## Composition: Arranging What You See

Composition is the organization of visual elements in a work of art. It's not just what you put in a picture — it's where you put it, how you size it, and what relationship it has to everything else. A photograph with great content but poor composition is less effective than a simple image with strong composition. Learning to compose well is one of the highest-leverage skills in visual art.

---

## What Composition Does

Good composition:
- Guides the viewer's eye through the image in a deliberate sequence
- Creates a clear focal point (the most important element)
- Establishes relationships between elements (what's dominant, what's subordinate)
- Produces a sense of balance, tension, or movement appropriate to the subject
- Uses the frame efficiently — includes what matters, excludes what doesn't

Bad composition leaves the eye wandering, competing focal points pulling against each other, and important elements getting lost.

---

## The Picture Plane and the Frame

The frame is the boundary of your image — the edges of the canvas, paper, or photograph. Everything inside the frame is the picture plane.

**The frame is a tool, not a constraint.** Where you place the frame determines what the image is. Moving a frame by six inches in any direction creates a different composition, a different mood, a different meaning.

**Cropping:** In photography and post-processing, cropping is the primary compositional tool. You decide after the fact exactly where the frame sits. In painting and drawing, you make this decision continuously as you work.

**Aspect ratio:** The proportion of the frame's width to height. A square frame (1:1) creates symmetry and stillness. A wide frame (16:9) creates cinematic expansiveness. A tall frame (portrait orientation) emphasizes verticality. The shape of the frame itself contributes to the composition.

---

## The Rule of Thirds

The most widely taught compositional guideline. Divide the frame into a 3×3 grid with two horizontal and two vertical lines. Place important elements along these lines or at their intersections (the "power points").

**Why it works:** Centered compositions feel static and formal. Off-center placement creates tension and dynamism. The rule of thirds is a quick formula for getting elements off-center in a way that feels balanced.

**When to break it:** The rule of thirds is a guide, not a law. Centered compositions work well for subjects that require symmetry or formality (a face looking directly at the camera, a reflection in still water, architectural subjects). Break the rule when centering serves the image.

**The four power points** (intersections of the grid lines) are where the eye naturally rests. Placing the main subject at one of these points gives it emphasis while keeping the composition off-center.

---

## Leading Lines

Lines in the composition (actual lines, or edges and implied lines) that lead the eye toward the focal point. A road leading toward mountains, a fence row converging in the distance, a river curving through a landscape — these all draw the eye inward.

**The most powerful leading lines:** Lines that start at a corner or edge of the frame and move inward toward the focal point. The eye enters the frame at the edge and follows the line to the subject.

**Conflicting lines:** Lines that lead out of the frame or away from the subject pull the eye away. Be aware of any strong lines in your composition and consider whether they're leading toward or away from where you want attention.

---

## Focal Point and Visual Hierarchy

Every strong composition has a clear focal point — the single most important element. The eye should arrive at the focal point first, then explore the rest of the image.

**Creating a focal point:**
- **Contrast:** The highest contrast area in an image is where the eye goes first. A bright light against dark surroundings draws immediate attention.
- **Isolation:** A single element separated from others draws attention by its aloneness.
- **Detail:** The area with the most detail attracts the eye, especially in a field of simpler elements.
- **Size:** The largest element in a composition tends to dominate.
- **Color:** A saturated color against desaturated surroundings, or a warm accent among cool tones, creates a focal point.

**Visual hierarchy:** After the focal point, the eye moves to secondary elements, then tertiary elements. A well-composed image creates a path — the viewer arrives at the main subject, explores supporting elements, and the eye naturally cycles back.

---

## Balance

Balance refers to the visual weight distribution across the frame. Visual weight is how much attention a given element demands — a large, dark, complex object has more visual weight than a small, light, simple one.

**Symmetrical balance:** Elements are mirrored on both sides of an axis. Formal, stable, calm. Often used for portraits, architectural photography, and images meant to convey authority or solemnity.

**Asymmetrical balance:** Different elements on each side of the frame, but the visual weights balance out. A large, simple shape balanced by a small, complex one. More dynamic and interesting than symmetry, but harder to achieve.

**Radial balance:** Elements arranged around a central point. Used in design and some paintings.

**Imbalance:** Sometimes deliberate — a composition that's weighted heavily to one side creates tension. This can be effective when tension is the point, but unintentional imbalance feels awkward.

---

## The Golden Ratio and the Fibonacci Spiral

The golden ratio (approximately 1:1.618) appears throughout nature and has been used by artists and architects for millennia. The ratio of the whole to the larger part equals the ratio of the larger part to the smaller.

The Fibonacci spiral is a curve that follows the golden ratio. Many artists compose images so that the main subject sits at the center of the spiral.

In practice, the golden ratio and rule of thirds produce similar results. The mathematical precision of the golden ratio is often used retroactively to explain why compositions feel right, more than as a real-time tool. The rule of thirds is faster and almost as effective.

## Compositional Formats

Different compositional patterns suit different subjects:

**Horizontal:** Wide, calm, restful. Good for landscapes, horizons, scenes of tranquility.

**Vertical:** Tall, dynamic, upward movement. Portraits, towers, trees, anything with height.

**Diagonal:** Movement, tension, energy. A dominant diagonal line creates dynamism.

**Triangle:** Stable, classic. Three main elements forming a triangle. The cornerstone of Renaissance portrait composition.

**Circular / Oval:** The eye moves around continuously. Harmonious and energetic at the same time.

**S-curve:** A flowing S-shape within the composition creates movement and grace. Landscape paths, rivers, the spine in a figure pose.

---

## Framing and Foreground

**Natural framing:** Using elements in the scene to frame the subject — tree branches overhead, a doorway, a tunnel. Framing within the frame adds depth and draws attention to the subject.

**Foreground elements:** Placing something in the near foreground (even partially in the frame, slightly blurred) creates depth and context. A foreground flower blurred in front of a sharp landscape grounds the viewer and adds layering.

---

## What to Include and Exclude

A composition's strength often comes from what's left out. Simplicity amplifies impact. Before committing to a composition, ask:
- Does every element in the frame contribute something?
- Is there anything that competes with or distracts from the focal point?
- Would moving closer or cropping tighter improve the image?

The temptation is to include everything so you don't miss anything. The discipline is to include only what's essential.
