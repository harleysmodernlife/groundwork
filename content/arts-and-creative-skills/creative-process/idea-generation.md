## Generating Ideas: Techniques That Work

Ideas don't come from nowhere. They come from input, combination, constraint, and process. "I can't think of anything" is almost always "I haven't yet applied a systematic approach to generating possibilities." These techniques work because they operate on how ideas actually form — through unexpected combination, forced association, and deliberate exploration.

---

## Before the Techniques: Input

No generation technique produces ideas without raw material. The raw material is everything you've read, seen, experienced, learned, and paid attention to. Before worrying about generating ideas, invest in input:

**Read widely and outside your field.** The most useful connections are often between domains that don't normally talk to each other. A photographer who reads about architecture will see light and structure differently. A musician who reads about biology might notice rhythm in systems. Cross-domain input is idea fuel.

**Pay attention to what interests you.** Not what you think should interest you, or what seems sophisticated, but what actually pulls your attention. Your genuine interests are pointing at something — they're the raw material of ideas that will be distinctively yours.

**Keep a capture system.** Ideas are fragile. They appear and disappear. Carry a notebook, use a phone app, use whatever works — but have somewhere to put observations, phrases, images, questions, and half-formed ideas immediately when they occur. The capture habit turns a passive mind into an active idea collector.

Without input, idea generation techniques are trying to squeeze water from stone. Feed the input consistently, and ideas become easier to generate.

## Brainstorming Done Right

**Brainstorming** is widely misunderstood. Most "brainstorming sessions" produce mediocre ideas because they violate the core rule:

**During generation, judgment is suspended. Completely.**

The two phases must be separated:
1. **Diverge:** Generate as many ideas as possible. Quantity over quality. No evaluation, no "that's stupid," no filtering. Write everything down.
2. **Converge:** After you've run out of ideas, evaluate. Select the promising ones. Discard the rest.

Mixing these phases is where brainstorming fails. When you evaluate while generating, you stop exploring before you find the unexpected ideas. The good ideas are often not the first ideas — they come after the obvious options have been exhausted.

**Solo vs. group:** Research consistently finds that solo brainstorming produces more and better ideas than group brainstorming. The social dynamics of group brainstorming — deference to status, fear of judgment, anchoring to early ideas — suppress divergent thinking. If you need group input, have everyone brainstorm individually first, then share and combine.

**Quantity targets:** Commit to a number. "20 ideas in 15 minutes." The quantity target forces you past the obvious options and into territory that genuinely surprises you. The 15th idea, generated after you've exhausted the easy ones, is often more interesting than the first five.

## SCAMPER

A structured checklist for generating variations on an existing idea, product, or creative work. Apply each prompt to your starting point:

**S — Substitute:** What can be substituted? Different material, different character, different context, different medium, different process.

**C — Combine:** What happens if you combine this with something else? Two unrelated ideas, two techniques, two subjects, two styles.

**A — Adapt:** What else is this like? What could be adapted from another field, genre, or context? How has this problem been solved elsewhere?

**M — Modify / Magnify / Minimize:** What if you changed one aspect radically — made it bigger, smaller, faster, slower, louder, quieter, longer, shorter?

**P — Put to other uses:** What other uses could this serve? What if it were used in a completely different context?

**E — Eliminate:** What if you removed something essential? What's the stripped-down core? What's left if you take away the most obvious element?

**R — Reverse / Rearrange:** What if you reversed the process? Told the story backwards? Worked from the opposite assumption?

SCAMPER is particularly useful when you have a starting point but feel stuck on variations. It forces systematic exploration of a design space you might leave partly unexplored.

## Random Input / Forced Connections

Open a book, a dictionary, or a magazine to a random page. Pick a random word, image, or phrase. Now force a connection between the random element and your creative problem.

This sounds arbitrary, and it is — that's the point. Your brain is very good at finding connections even when none are obvious. The random element breaks you out of the associative ruts you'd fall into without it.

**Technique:**
1. Write your problem or creative challenge at the top of a page.
2. Generate a random word (flip through a book; use a random word generator; look around the room and name the third object you see).
3. Write down every association you have with the random word.
4. Force each association to connect with your problem.
5. Some connections will be useless. Occasionally, one will be genuinely unexpected and useful.

The connections that feel most forced are sometimes the most generative — they're the farthest from where you started, which means they're in territory you wouldn't have found through rational exploration.

## Constraints as Idea Generators

Constraints don't limit creative thinking — they focus it. An open-ended prompt ("make something interesting") produces less creative work than a constrained one ("make something interesting using only three colors and no more than five elements").

**Types of constraints to impose:**

**Material constraints:** Only use this tool, instrument, material, or medium. A photographer limited to black and white makes different compositional choices than one with full color options.

**Time constraints:** Make something in 30 minutes. Don't work on any piece for more than an hour. The urgency of time limits forces commitment to the first viable idea rather than endless refinement of nothing.

**Format constraints:** Write exactly 100 words. Make a piece that fits in a 4×4 inch frame. Three chords only. The limitation channels energy into execution.

**Thematic constraints:** Only work with objects from your own home. Only photograph within a one-block radius. Write only about a single day. Narrow themes force depth over breadth.

**Anti-constraints (remove what you usually rely on):** What's your default? Composition based on the rule of thirds? Remove it. Melody as the primary element? Write something purely rhythmic. Realistic representation? Abstraction only. Removing your comfortable defaults forces new solutions.

## Mind Mapping

A non-linear brainstorming structure that works with how ideas actually associate rather than against it.

**Process:**
1. Write your central concept in the middle of a blank page.
2. Draw lines out from the center to related concepts.
3. From each of those, draw further branches to their associations.
4. Keep going until the page is full.

Mind maps externalize the associative network in your mind, making visible the connections between ideas that would otherwise remain implicit. They're useful for exploring a topic before writing or designing — you end up with a visual inventory of everything you associate with the subject, which makes selection and structure easier.

**Key rule:** Don't filter while mapping. Write every association. Evaluate later.

## The Six Thinking Hats (Edward de Bono)

A structured framework for exploring a problem from multiple perspectives systematically. Each "hat" represents a different mode of thinking:

- **White hat:** Facts and information. What do we know? What data is available?
- **Red hat:** Emotions and intuition. What does this feel like? What's your gut reaction?
- **Black hat:** Critical judgment. What could go wrong? What are the risks and weaknesses?
- **Yellow hat:** Optimistic thinking. What's the best-case scenario? What's the value?
- **Green hat:** Creative thinking. What are alternative possibilities? What hasn't been considered?
- **Blue hat:** Process and meta-thinking. How are we approaching this? What should we do next?

The value is in the systematic rotation through modes that you might otherwise skip. Most people default to white and black hat thinking — facts and problems — and underuse yellow and green. Deliberately putting on the green hat forces idea generation even when critical thinking wants to take over.

## Sleep and Incubation

Many problems are best solved by working on them intensely, then stopping and doing something else. The subconscious continues processing while you sleep or engage in different activities, and solutions emerge that weren't accessible during focused effort.

This is not mysticism — it's memory consolidation and associative processing happening outside conscious awareness. The practical implication: load up on the problem thoroughly before bed or before a break. Brief yourself fully, work on it, then let it rest. Record what comes to you on waking or during the diffuse activity afterward.

The mistake is expecting solutions to arrive during focused work sessions only. Some of the most useful ideas surface while walking, showering, washing dishes — during activities that occupy the body but free the mind.

## Observation and Theft (Legal Kind)

All creative work builds on what came before it. Studying the work of others is not just acceptable — it's essential.

**Deliberate imitation:** Pick a work you admire and try to replicate it. Not to pass the result off as your own, but to understand how it was made. What choices were made and why? What techniques produce which effects? Imitation as learning is how every art form has been taught for most of human history.

**Taking inspiration from outside your field:** A writer studying how film directors control pacing. A graphic designer studying how architecture creates hierarchy. A musician studying how a comedian structures tension and release. The techniques of one field, applied to another, create fresh approaches.

**Combinatorial thinking:** Instead of asking "what should I make?", ask "what would happen if X met Y?" What if the narrative structure of a detective novel applied to a photography project? What if a musical approach to counterpoint shaped how you arrange elements in a visual composition?

## The "Yes, And" Principle

From improvisational theater: when your scene partner offers something, accept it ("yes") and add to it ("and"). Never block an idea ("no, that doesn't work") during generative play.

Applied to solo creativity: when an idea arises, don't immediately assess whether it's good. Say "yes, and" — accept it and see where it leads. Many ideas that seem bad on first inspection lead somewhere useful when followed.

The discipline of "yes, and" during idea generation is the same discipline as suspending judgment in brainstorming. Ideas need room to develop before they reveal whether they're useful. Blocking them early, out of habit or fear of looking foolish, kills the process before it can produce anything surprising.

## When Techniques Don't Work

Techniques generate starting points — they don't guarantee good ideas. Sometimes you apply every technique you know and still feel like nothing is working. This is normal.

**Signs you're blocked by input poverty:** You keep coming back to the same few ideas. Everything feels derivative or uninteresting. The problem is likely that you need more raw material — more reading, more looking, more experiences to draw connections from.

**Signs you're blocked by perfectionism:** You generate ideas but dismiss all of them immediately. The problem is applying convergent thinking too early. Force yourself to fill a page before evaluating anything.

**Signs you're blocked by the wrong framing:** You've been asking the wrong question. Try reframing the problem completely. Instead of "how do I make this better?", ask "what would this look like if I started over?" Instead of "what should the next scene be?", ask "what does the character most want to avoid?" Different questions open different solution spaces.

**When nothing helps:** Stop. Do something else. Come back in a day or a week. Some problems need time, not more technique.
