## What Creativity Is (and Isn't)

Creativity is widely misunderstood. Most people believe it's a special ability that some people are born with and others aren't — a gift that explains why certain people make things and others don't. This belief is wrong, and it's harmful, because it gives people a reason to stop before they start.

Here's what creativity actually is and where it actually comes from.

---

## What Creativity Is Not

**Creativity is not a talent.** You were not born creative or not creative. Creative output is the result of practice, input, attention, and willingness to make things that might fail. These are learnable behaviors, not innate traits.

**Creativity is not inspiration waiting to strike.** The romantic idea of the artist waiting for a muse is mostly a retrospective narrative. Creative people who make things consistently do not wait for inspiration — they show up and work, and sometimes inspiration arrives while they're already at it.

**Creativity is not originality from nothing.** Nothing comes from nothing. Every creative work is made from the inputs that preceded it — influences absorbed, experiences processed, problems encountered. The idea that truly original work emerges from nowhere is both historically inaccurate and paralyzing, because nothing you make will come from nowhere.

**Creativity is not only for artists.** Solving a problem in a new way, finding an approach that nobody tried before, seeing a connection that others missed — these are creative acts. Scientists, engineers, teachers, cooks, mechanics, and parents all engage in creative thinking. The arts don't own creativity.

---

## What Creativity Actually Is

**Creativity is making new connections between existing things.**

Not from nothing — from everything you've already taken in: ideas, images, experiences, techniques, knowledge, emotions. The creative act is finding an unexpected relationship between things that already exist and rendering that relationship in a form that communicates something.

James Webb Young, in his 1939 book *A Technique for Producing Ideas*, described creativity as "the combination of existing elements in new and useful ways." That's it. The creative person is someone who notices unexpected combinations — and has the skill and persistence to execute them.

**Creativity is a process, not an event.** There is no single moment of creation. A painting starts with thousands of prior paintings in the artist's memory, a vague sense of what they want to make, a series of small decisions, revisions, failures, accidents that become features, and continued work over time. The "finished work" is the endpoint of a process, not a single burst.

**Creativity is a skill built through practice.** The more you make, the more you understand the material you're working with, the more connections you can draw on, and the more you develop your own sense of what works. Making 100 paintings makes you better at making paintings than reading about painting, thinking about painting, or planning to paint. The skill is in the doing.

## The Creative Personality Myth

Psychology has studied creativity for decades. What actually differentiates more creative people from less creative people isn't a special brain or a special trait — it's a cluster of learnable behaviors:

**Curiosity:** Actively pursuing new information, experiences, and perspectives. Asking why things work the way they do. Noticing things that others walk past.

**Tolerance for ambiguity:** Comfort with not knowing the answer yet, with a work in progress that hasn't resolved into something clear. The ability to hold an unfinished problem in mind without needing to close it prematurely.

**Willingness to be wrong:** Making things that don't work is necessary to making things that do. Creative people make more failed work, not less — they just also make more successful work because they're making more overall.

**Persistence:** Working through the difficult middle where the vision is clear but the execution isn't there yet. Not stopping when the first version is bad.

**Wide-ranging inputs:** Reading outside your field, looking at work outside your discipline, pursuing interests that seem unrelated. The unexpected connections that define creative work often come from distance — from connecting knowledge in domain A with a problem in domain B.

None of these are talents. All of them are habits.

## The Skill-Taste Gap

Ira Glass's observation — applicable far beyond radio production:

"Nobody tells people who are beginners, and I really wish someone had told this to me: All of us who do creative work, we get into it because we have good taste. But there is this gap. For the first couple years you make stuff, it's just not that good. It's trying to be good, it has potential, but it's not. But your taste, the thing that got you into the game, is still killer. And your taste is why your work disappoints you."

Your taste develops faster than your technical ability. You can hear what good music sounds like before you can play it. You can see what a good photograph looks like before you can make one. You know what a good essay reads like before you can write one. This gap between taste and ability is not a sign that you're not creative — it's the normal experience of every developing creative person.

The only way to close the gap is to make a large volume of work over time. The gap closes. The taste that got you started begins to align with the ability you've built through practice.

## Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation

Research on creativity consistently finds that **intrinsic motivation** — doing something because you find it interesting, challenging, or meaningful — produces more and higher-quality creative work than **extrinsic motivation** — doing something for external reward (money, approval, grades, social media metrics).

This doesn't mean you can't get paid for creative work or that sharing your work publicly is wrong. It means that making work primarily to get likes, to impress people, or to be praised tends to produce safer, more derivative work. Making work because you're genuinely interested in solving the problem produces more adventurous, distinctive work.

The most useful question isn't "will people like this?" but "does this interest me?" The work you make from genuine interest is more likely to be interesting to others than work calculated to please.

## Convergent and Divergent Thinking

Psychologists distinguish two modes of thinking relevant to creativity:

**Divergent thinking:** Generating many possible ideas, associations, and approaches. Opening out, exploring, not yet judging. What happens in brainstorming. The mode of abundance.

**Convergent thinking:** Evaluating options, selecting the best approach, making decisions, refining. What happens in editing and revision. The mode of judgment.

Both are necessary. Pure divergent thinking without convergent thinking produces a lot of ideas and no finished work. Pure convergent thinking without divergent thinking produces conventional solutions — the obvious answer, not a creative one.

The problem most people have is applying convergent thinking too early — judging ideas while generating them, stopping exploration before it can surprise you. The discipline of divergent thinking is suppressing judgment temporarily. Let the ideas come. Evaluate later.

## Constraints and Creativity

A paradox: creativity often flourishes under constraints rather than in complete freedom. When everything is possible, making a decision requires justifying it against infinite alternatives. When constraints narrow the space, decisions become easier and the creative energy goes into working within the limits.

Examples:
- A sonnet has 14 lines, a specific rhyme scheme, and iambic pentameter. These constraints produce rather than prevent great poetry.
- A photographer limited to one lens for three months develops a stronger, more consistent body of work than one who constantly switches.
- A composer writing for a specific ensemble (string quartet, brass quintet) makes different and often more interesting choices than one writing for an unlimited orchestra.

Self-imposed constraints are a useful creative tool: one color, one location, one word per day, one instrument. The constraint forces solutions you wouldn't find in unlimited space.

## What This Means Practically

If creativity is what this lesson describes — a set of learnable behaviors, a process that improves with practice, dependent on inputs and connections and persistence — then several things follow:

**You are not disqualified.** Whatever you believe about your natural ability, you can make things. You can improve at making things. You can develop taste, practice technique, and close the skill-taste gap.

**Starting matters more than readiness.** There's no point at which you're ready to begin. You begin, and readiness follows.

**Volume matters.** Make more things. Most of them won't be good. Some of them will be better than you expected. The accumulation of practice builds skill.

**Inputs matter.** Consume broadly. Read outside your field. Look at work that intimidates you and work that delights you. Engage with the world actively, not passively.

**The work is the point.** Making things is the activity. Sharing it, being praised for it, succeeding commercially — these may or may not follow. But they're not what it's about. The point is the making.
