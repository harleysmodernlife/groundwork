## Perspective: One-Point and Two-Point

Perspective is the system for drawing three-dimensional space on a flat surface so that it looks correct to the human eye. Without perspective, buildings look like stacked boxes, roads don't recede into the distance, and objects don't diminish in size as they move away from you. With perspective, you can create a convincing illusion of depth.

Perspective is rule-based. You can learn it. And once you understand the principles, you can draw convincing spatial environments from imagination without needing to set up every drawing with mechanical draftsmanship.

---

## The Core Principles

**The horizon line** is the viewer's eye level — where the sky meets the earth if the terrain were perfectly flat. Everything above the horizon line is above the viewer's eye; everything below it is below eye level.

The horizon line moves with the viewer. Crouch down and it drops. Stand on a hill and it rises. When you draw, the first decision is where your horizon line is, because it determines where the viewer's eye is placed.

**Vanishing points** are specific points on the horizon line where parallel lines appear to converge. Train tracks don't actually meet in the distance — but they appear to, at a vanishing point on the horizon. This convergence is the core visual logic of perspective drawing.

**Parallel lines that share a direction all converge at the same vanishing point.** The left edge and right edge of a road converge at the same point on the horizon. The top and bottom of a building's front edge converge at the same vanishing point.

---

## One-Point Perspective

One-point perspective applies when you're looking directly at the front face of an object, with no sides visible from an angle. You're looking straight at it — the front face is flat, parallel to the picture plane.

**When to use it:** Long corridors, roads leading away from you, a room seen straight-on, train tracks stretching into the distance.

**Setup:**
1. Draw the horizon line across the page at your eye level
2. Mark one vanishing point on the horizon (typically centered or slightly off-center)
3. Draw the front faces of objects (rectangles, squares — they're drawn without any convergence because they're parallel to the picture plane)
4. Draw lines from the corners of those front faces converging toward the vanishing point — these receding lines define the depth of the objects

**Practical result:** A box in one-point perspective has a flat front face and converging lines on its sides and top that all meet at the single vanishing point.

**What one-point perspective does well:** Creates a strong sense of depth along a central axis. Roads, hallways, and tracks leading toward a distant point are classic one-point perspective.

**What it doesn't do well:** Any situation where you're viewing an object from an angle — where you can see two sides at once — breaks one-point perspective and requires two-point.

---

## Two-Point Perspective

Two-point perspective applies when you're viewing an object from an angle — you can see two sides of it simultaneously.

**When to use it:** Looking at the corner of a building, a box turned at an angle, cityscapes, most exterior architectural subjects.

**Setup:**
1. Draw the horizon line
2. Mark two vanishing points on the horizon — one to the left (VP-L) and one to the right (VP-R). They can be far apart or close; the distance determines how dramatic the perspective looks. Very close VPs create an extreme, fish-eye feel; far apart VPs are more natural.
3. Draw the nearest vertical edge of your object — a vertical line somewhere in the drawing
4. From the top of that edge, draw lines to both VP-L and VP-R — these define the top edges of the two visible faces
5. From the bottom of that edge, draw lines to both VPs — these define the bottom edges
6. Close the faces by drawing vertical lines where the sides of the object end

**The result:** A box with two visible faces, each face receding to a different vanishing point. The nearest edge is the vertical you drew first; all horizontal edges converge to one of the two vanishing points.

**Multiple objects in the same scene:** Objects aligned to the same grid (a row of buildings on a straight street) share the same two vanishing points. Objects at a different angle have their own pair of vanishing points at a different position on the horizon line.

---

## Three-Point Perspective

Adds a third vanishing point either above or below the horizon — used when looking up at a tall building (vanishing point above) or down at an object from above (vanishing point below). Vertical lines converge toward this third vanishing point rather than remaining perfectly vertical.

In most drawings, vertical lines are kept parallel (no third vanishing point) because the effect is subtle and the additional complexity slows the work. Use three-point perspective when the vertical convergence is dramatic enough to be noticeable — very tall buildings seen from below, aerial views.

---

## Common Mistakes

**Horizon line at eye level, not figure eye level.** The horizon should be at the viewer's eye level. If you're drawing a standing person, the horizon crosses at their eye level — usually head height. Objects above eye level are drawn above the horizon; objects below eye level below.

**Vanishing points too close together.** This creates extreme, distorted-looking perspective. For most subjects, place vanishing points at or outside the edges of the drawing — even off the paper.

**Making horizontal lines parallel when they should converge.** Beginners often draw the horizontal edges of buildings as slightly angled lines but don't follow them all the way to the vanishing point, resulting in inconsistent convergence. Use a ruler and actually test: do all horizontal lines on this face converge to the same point?

**Vertical lines leaning.** In one-point and two-point perspective, all vertical lines are truly vertical — perfectly perpendicular to the horizon. Even a slight lean looks wrong.

**Forgetting that the horizon is eye level.** The horizon passes through every object at the level of the viewer's eye. A table seen from above has the horizon above it. A table seen from below has the horizon below it. A table at eye level has the horizon running right through it.

## Using Perspective Freehand

Mechanical perspective — drawing with a ruler, marking vanishing points carefully — is precise and useful. But most drawing involves applying perspective principles without formal setup.

**The trained eye:** With practice, you can estimate where the horizon is and how strongly lines converge without measuring. You develop a feel for perspective accuracy.

**Error detection:** More important than drawing perspective correctly from imagination is noticing when perspective is wrong in your drawing. The skill of seeing perspective errors lets you correct them. Common signs of perspective error: a building that looks like it's leaning backward, objects that don't read as receding, rooms that feel like the walls are bending.

**Check with a ruler:** If something looks off, hold a straight edge along the horizontal edges in question. All edges of the same face should converge to the same vanishing point. If they don't, find and correct the inconsistency.

---

## Perspective in Everyday Drawing

Once you understand perspective, you start seeing it everywhere. The way a table recedes to one or two points. The way a road narrows in the distance. The way the undersides of buildings above your eye level slope upward toward vanishing points.

You also start drawing with it automatically. A sketch of a room begins with an eye-level decision and a sense of where the vanishing points are, even if you don't formally mark them. Objects placed in the room recede consistently.

This intuitive application of perspective principles is what distinguishes drawings that look spatially convincing from ones that feel flat or inconsistent.
