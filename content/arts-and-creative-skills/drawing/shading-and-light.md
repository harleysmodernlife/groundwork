## Shading: Making Things Look Three-Dimensional

A line drawing shows where the edges are. Shading shows where the light is — and light is what makes flat shapes look like three-dimensional objects. Without shading, a circle is just a circle. With shading that follows the way light falls on a sphere, that same circle becomes a ball you can almost reach out and pick up.

Shading is the application of value — lightness and darkness — in a way that describes form. This lesson covers the techniques for creating convincing shading in pencil, charcoal, or any medium where you control value.

---

## Understanding What You're Rendering

Before you shade, understand what you're looking at. Every object receiving light has these zones (covered in the Value lesson, reviewed here):

**Highlight:** Brightest spot. On shiny surfaces, it's a small, sharp reflection. On matte surfaces, it's the broad area most directly facing the light.

**Light:** The region receiving direct light. Bright, but not as bright as the highlight.

**Midtone/Halftone:** The transition area where the surface curves away from the light. Neither fully lit nor in shadow.

**Core shadow:** The darkest area on the object itself, where the surface curves furthest from the light. This is not at the edge of the object — it's slightly inside the shadow's edge.

**Reflected light:** Within the shadow, the edge of the form often receives bounced light from nearby surfaces. It's lighter than the core shadow but always darker than any area in direct light. Beginning shaders often make reflected light too bright, which collapses the shadow.

**Cast shadow:** The shadow the object throws onto other surfaces. Darkest directly under/adjacent to the object, fading and softening with distance.

## Shading Techniques

### Hatching

Parallel lines drawn close together. The closer the lines, the darker the value. The farther apart, the lighter.

**Directional hatching:** Lines follow the direction of the form's surface. If you're shading a sphere, the lines curve to follow the surface's direction. This technique, used by masters like Dürer and Michelangelo, makes the shading simultaneously describe value and form.

### Cross-Hatching

Two (or more) layers of hatching, with lines crossing each other at angles. More layers = darker value. Allows building up dark values through layering rather than pressing harder.

Cross-hatching gives a mechanical, controlled quality to the shading. Excellent for technical illustration, architectural rendering, and a precise, deliberate style.

### Blending

Applying graphite or charcoal and then blending it with a tortillon (paper stump), tissue, or fingertip to create smooth gradients without visible lines.

Blending produces soft, smooth shading appropriate for skin, smooth metal, glass, or any surface without texture. Over-blending creates a "chalky" or flat quality — avoid blending all the way to zero texture.

### Scumbling/Circular Marks

Random circular or curved marks that build up value in a textured way. Produces a rough texture appropriate for stone, fur, grass, or rough fabric.

### Stippling

Value built up through dots — dense clusters for dark areas, sparse for light. Very controlled, time-intensive. Common in pen-and-ink illustration, scientific illustration, and some printmaking.

### Wet Brush/Wash

Graphite or charcoal can be dissolved with a wet brush to create smooth washes. Similar to watercolor technique — can be layered and blended fluidly. Useful for large areas.

## How to Build Value Gradually

**Work light to dark.** Start with the lightest values and add progressively darker marks. It's easier to add than to erase, especially in pencil. In charcoal, you can use an eraser as a mark-making tool (lifting charcoal to create lights), which allows more flexibility in either direction.

**Don't press hard early.** Pressing hard too soon makes dark marks that are hard to control and impossible to erase cleanly. Build darkness gradually through multiple light layers.

**Squint at your work.** Squinting blurs detail and lets you see value relationships clearly. Squint at your subject, then squint at your drawing. Do the light and dark areas align? Are the relative values correct?

**Check your darkest dark.** Most beginning drawings are too light overall — the darkest shadows aren't dark enough. Your darkest shadow should be significantly darker than the midtones. Put your darkest marks in early as a reference, then key everything else to them.

## Edges in Shading

Not all edges in shading are equally defined:

**Hard edges:** Where one plane sharply meets another. The edge of a cast shadow, the corner of a box. Drawn with a clear, defined line between values.

**Soft edges:** Where a surface gradually curves away. The shadow on a sphere, the transition from cheek to forehead. The value changes gradually, with no visible boundary.

**Lost edges:** Where form disappears into shadow or merges with the background. Sometimes the right answer is to let the edge disappear entirely — the eye completes the form. Lost edges give a sense of the form extending beyond the drawing.

Varying edge quality throughout a drawing prevents a mechanical appearance and creates depth — harder edges come forward, softer edges recede.

## Materials

**Pencil:** The most controlled shading medium. Grades from 9H (very hard, light) through H, F, HB, B to 9B (very soft, very dark). For most shading work: use 2H-HB for light areas, HB-2B for midtones, 4B-6B for dark areas. A mechanical pencil (0.5mm, 2B lead) gives consistent fine lines; traditional pencils are faster for coverage.

**Charcoal:** Vine charcoal is soft and easy to erase. Compressed charcoal is darker and harder to erase. Charcoal is forgiving — you can wipe it with a rag and start over. Excellent for large-scale work and loose, expressive drawing.

**Graphite stick:** Like a large pencil without the wood, allows shading larger areas quickly.

**White charcoal or chalk:** Used on toned or black paper to add lights rather than darken shadows. The paper itself represents the midtone.

## A Shading Practice Exercise

Draw a sphere (trace a circular object if needed). Place a light source — a lamp to one side works well.

1. Lightly sketch the circle.
2. Add the cast shadow beneath and to the side (it's an ellipse on the ground).
3. Very lightly tone the entire sphere except the highlight area.
4. Identify the core shadow location (slightly inside from the edge, away from the light) and darken that zone.
5. Soften the transition from light to midtone by blending or using lighter marks.
6. Add a slight lightening at the shadow edge (reflected light) — but make sure it stays darker than any lit area.
7. Deepen the cast shadow, darkest at the base of the sphere, fading outward.
8. Check: Is the darkest area the core shadow? Is there a clear gradation from highlight to core shadow? Is the reflected light subtle, not overwhelming?

Do this exercise with a sphere, then a cylinder, then a cube. The principles are the same; the form of the light/shadow zones differs.
