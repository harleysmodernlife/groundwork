## Color in Design: Palette and Psychology

Color in graphic design operates differently from color in fine art. A painter can explore color relationships intuitively and revise as the painting develops. A graphic designer typically works with a defined palette — a deliberate selection of colors used consistently across a design system, brand, or campaign. The palette must work functionally (readable, accessible, printable), emotionally (communicating the right feeling), and practically (usable across many applications from a business card to a billboard).

---

## Building a Color Palette

### Starting Points

**Brand brief or design intent:** What is this design for? What should it feel like? What does the audience expect? A children's education company and a law firm have radically different color needs.

**Competitive research:** What colors do competitors use? Sometimes you follow a category convention (blue for banking, green for health). Sometimes you differentiate deliberately.

**Client input:** Existing brand materials, color preferences, colors to avoid.

### Color Selection Process

1. **Choose a primary color.** This is the dominant color — the one most strongly associated with the brand or design.

2. **Choose secondary colors.** Complement or contrast with the primary. Typically 1-2 secondary colors.

3. **Choose accent colors.** Used sparingly for emphasis, calls to action, or highlights.

4. **Include neutrals.** Every design needs neutrals — white, black, grays, and off-whites. Neutrals carry body text, backgrounds, and large areas.

5. **Test the palette.** Apply it to real design elements before committing. Are there accessibility issues? Does the combination work?

## Color Relationships in Design

**Monochromatic palette:** One hue in various tints, shades, and saturations. Highly cohesive, sophisticated, and easy to get right. Common in premium and luxury branding.

**Complementary:** Two colors opposite on the color wheel. High contrast and energy. The classic brand example: orange and blue (Fanta, Etsy, Firefox, Amazon). Creates visual tension that attracts attention.

**Triadic:** Three colors at 120° apart. Rich and balanced but complex to use well. Usually one color dominates and the other two serve as accents.

**Analogous:** Colors adjacent on the color wheel. Harmonious and natural-feeling. Many nature-themed brands use analogous palettes — greens and blue-greens, oranges and yellows.

## Color Psychology in Design

Color carries associations that are partly universal and partly cultural:

**Red:** Urgency, energy, passion, appetite. Used by food brands (McDonald's, Coca-Cola), sales promotions, anywhere you want to stimulate action.

**Orange:** Friendliness, creativity, enthusiasm, affordability. Brands that want approachability without formality (Amazon, Harley-Davidson).

**Yellow:** Optimism, warmth, attention, caution. Difficult to use on white backgrounds (low contrast). Works well for accents.

**Blue:** Trust, stability, professionalism, calm. The most common color in corporate branding (IBM, Ford, Facebook, Samsung). Overused in tech and financial sectors.

**Green:** Health, nature, growth, sustainability. Environmental and organic brands, health food, outdoor products.

**Purple:** Royalty, luxury, mystery, spirituality. Premium positioning, beauty, wellness.

**Black:** Sophistication, luxury, authority, formality. Premium brands across many categories.

**White:** Purity, simplicity, cleanliness, space. Used for breathing room, premium minimalism.

These associations vary by culture — white is associated with mourning in some East Asian cultures, for example. They're tendencies, not laws.

## Color and Accessibility

**Contrast ratios:** The difference in luminance between foreground text and background color determines readability. WCAG specifies:
- **AA standard (minimum):** 4.5:1 contrast ratio for normal text; 3:1 for large text (18pt+)
- **AAA standard (enhanced):** 7:1 for normal text; 4.5:1 for large text

Tools like WebAIM's Contrast Checker and Adobe Color's accessibility feature check contrast ratios automatically.

**Color blindness:** About 8% of men have some form of color blindness. The most common type (red-green) makes red and green difficult to distinguish. Designs that communicate information only through color — green means good, red means bad, no other indicator — fail for these users. Use color + shape + pattern + text labels together.

**Practical checks:**
- Convert your design to grayscale — does the hierarchy still make sense? Can you still read all the text?
- Use a color blindness simulation tool (Coblis, Adobe Color's color-blind safe mode) to see how the design appears with different types of color blindness

## Color in Print vs. Screen

**RGB:** Red, green, blue — how screens produce color. Additive color: combining all channels at full value produces white. Colors on screen can be more vivid than print because screens produce light.

**CMYK:** Cyan, magenta, yellow, black — how most commercial printing produces color. More limited gamut than RGB — some vivid RGB colors can't be reproduced in print.

**Pantone (PMS):** Proprietary standardized ink colors for brand consistency. A Pantone 485 red looks the same from any printer using that formula. Essential for brands where color consistency across all printed materials matters.

**The workflow implication:** Colors specified in RGB for screen may not reproduce accurately in CMYK print. Vivid screen blues and greens often become less saturated in print. Design for print in CMYK from the start, or convert carefully and check proofs.

## Applying Color Practically

**Start with grayscale.** Design the layout in grayscale first — get the hierarchy and composition right before adding color. Color should enhance a working design, not rescue a broken one.

**Use color sparingly.** A design with one strong primary color, one complementary accent, and a clean neutral background is more powerful than a rainbow of equally competing hues. The most common mistake is using too many colors.

**Build the color system, not just the palette.** Specify how colors are used: "this dark blue is always the background on hero sections; this orange is only used for call-to-action buttons; this gray is used for secondary text." Consistent application is what makes a palette feel like a system.

**Test across applications.** Apply your palette to a business card, a website header, a social media post, and a print ad. Does the palette hold up in both large and small applications?
