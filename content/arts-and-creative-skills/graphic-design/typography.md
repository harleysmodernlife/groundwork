## Typography: Choosing and Using Fonts

Typography is the art and technique of arranging type to make written language legible, readable, and visually effective. It's one of the most powerful tools in graphic design — and one of the most commonly done poorly. Bad typography makes information harder to read, undermines credibility, and signals amateur work. Good typography is often invisible: the reader just reads.

---

## The Basics of Type

### Anatomy of Letters

Understanding basic letterform terminology helps you talk about and choose type:

**Baseline:** The invisible line on which most letters sit.

**Cap height:** The height of capital letters, measured from the baseline.

**X-height:** The height of lowercase letters (specifically the height of the letter "x"), not counting ascenders or descenders.

**Ascenders:** The parts of lowercase letters that rise above the x-height: b, d, f, h, k, l, t.

**Descenders:** The parts that extend below the baseline: g, j, p, q, y.

**Serif:** Small finishing strokes at the ends of letterform strokes. Typefaces either have serifs or don't.

**Counter:** The enclosed or partially enclosed space within a letter: the hole in an "o," the bowl of a "d."

**Kerning:** The adjustment of space between specific letter pairs. Well-kerned type looks evenly spaced; poorly kerned type has awkward gaps or collisions between certain letter combinations (the classic example: "AV" — without kerning, these two letters appear to have a large gap between them).

**Tracking (letter-spacing):** The uniform spacing adjustment across a range of letters or words.

**Leading (line spacing):** The vertical spacing between lines of text. Measured baseline to baseline.

## Typeface Classification

### Serif Typefaces

Have small finishing strokes (serifs) on the ends of letterforms. Traditionally considered more readable in long-form text because the serifs create a horizontal flow that guides the eye.

**Old Style / Humanist serifs:** Based on Renaissance letterforms. Diagonal stress (the thinnest part of curved letters is at a diagonal). Examples: Garamond, Bembo, Caslon. Warm, historical, literary feeling.

**Transitional serifs:** More vertical stress, higher contrast between thick and thin strokes. Examples: Times New Roman, Baskerville, Georgia. The workhorses of book and editorial typography.

**Modern / Didone serifs:** Very high contrast between thick and thin. Elegant, sophisticated, but can reduce readability at small sizes. Examples: Bodoni, Didot. Used in fashion and luxury contexts.

**Slab serifs:** Heavy, unbracketed serifs of the same weight as the strokes. Bold, confident, mechanical feeling. Examples: Rockwell, Clarendon, Courier. Used in headlines, posters, brand contexts that want solidity.

### Sans-Serif Typefaces

No finishing strokes. Cleaner, more modern feeling.

**Grotesque / Neo-grotesque:** The classic sans-serifs — Helvetica, Arial, Univers. Neutral, functional, widely legible. The backbone of modern design.

**Humanist sans-serifs:** More organic proportions, influenced by handwriting. Examples: Gill Sans, Myriad, Frutiger. Friendlier and warmer than grotesque.

**Geometric sans-serifs:** Based on geometric shapes — circles, straight lines. Futura, Gotham, Avenir. Modern, clean, technical feeling.

### Script and Display

Script fonts imitate handwriting or calligraphy. Display fonts are designed to be used large, in headlines and titles — they often sacrifice legibility at small sizes for visual impact at large sizes.

**Rule:** Display and script fonts are for headlines and accents, not body text.

## Choosing Typefaces

### Matching to Context

Typeface choice communicates before readers read a word. A law firm using a playful, casual script would undermine its credibility. A children's book in an austere geometric sans would feel cold and wrong.

**Serif or sans-serif?** Traditionally, serifs for long print text, sans-serif for screens. This distinction has largely collapsed — modern sans-serifs are highly legible at text sizes on screen. More useful distinction: serifs feel traditional, established, and literary; sans-serifs feel modern, clean, and minimal.

**What associations does this font carry?** Comic Sans has been so overused and misused that it carries specific (negative) connotations. Helvetica carries corporate, neutral associations. Garamond carries literary, intellectual ones. Use fonts whose associations match your intent.

### The Two-Font Rule

Most designs need no more than two typefaces: one for headlines and one for body text. Using more than two creates visual noise without adding meaning.

**Combination strategies:**
- Serif headline + sans-serif body (or vice versa): The contrast creates clear hierarchy
- Display headline + readable text face: Dramatic headlines, practical body
- Different weights of the same typeface: The simplest approach — one versatile family with enough weight and style variation

### Type Hierarchy

Good typography creates a clear reading hierarchy through size, weight, and style variation:

- **Headline:** Largest, usually boldest, sets the main message
- **Subhead:** Smaller than headline, larger than body, organizes sections
- **Body text:** The readable main content
- **Caption/supporting text:** Smallest, least prominent

Each level should be clearly distinguishable from the others. If the headline and subhead are nearly the same size, the hierarchy fails.

## Practical Typography Rules

**Body text: 9-12pt for print, 14-18px for web.** Below about 9pt (print) or 14px (screen), most text becomes difficult to read.

**Line length (measure): 45-75 characters per line.** Lines that are too short create too many line breaks and interrupt reading flow. Lines that are too long require the eye to travel far to find the next line, causing the reader to lose their place.

**Leading (line spacing): 120-145% of the type size.** Text set at 10pt should have leading of about 12-14.5pt. Too tight and lines blur together; too loose and the text feels disconnected and hard to read.

**Don't stretch or distort fonts.** Condensing or stretching type horizontally or vertically destroys the letterform proportions. Use a typeface that's actually designed as condensed or extended if that's what you need.

**Avoid all caps for body text.** Words in all capitals lose their shapes (the ascenders and descenders that make words visually distinct at a glance), slowing reading significantly. All caps works well for short headlines and labels.

**Align text consistently.** Left-aligned text (ragged right) is generally most readable in English. Justified text (both edges aligned) requires careful attention to avoid large, awkward word spacing gaps — particularly problematic in narrow columns.

**Use real quotation marks and apostrophes, not inch/foot marks.** "Smart quotes" curve; straight marks (") are for inches and feet. Most design software substitutes automatically, but check for mistakes.

## Type and Space

Typography is as much about space as it is about letterforms:

**White space:** Space around text gives it room to breathe and makes the layout feel organized. Cramped text feels cheap and overwhelming.

**Margins:** Generous margins are a mark of quality design. They create a frame that focuses attention and signal that the content inside is valuable.

**Proximity:** Text that belongs together should be visually grouped. A caption should be clearly closer to its image than to adjacent images. A heading should be closer to its section than to the section above it.

**The hierarchy of space:** In a good layout, the space relationships create hierarchy: large margins separate major sections, medium margins separate subsections, small margins connect related items.
