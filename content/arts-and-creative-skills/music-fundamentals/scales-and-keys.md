## Scales and Keys

A scale is a specific sequence of pitches in ascending or descending order. A key is the tonal center that everything in a piece gravitates toward. Scales and keys are closely related — the key defines which notes belong together, and the scale is the ordered collection of those notes.

Understanding scales and keys explains why certain chords sound good together, why melodies feel resolved or unresolved, and how musicians communicate about harmonic relationships.

---

## Why Scales Exist

Out of the 12 available pitches in Western music, most pieces of music use only 7. The selection of those 7 (or fewer) pitches creates a tonal palette — a collection of notes that sound good together and create a sense of home and away, tension and resolution.

When music stays within a key, it has internal coherence. When it moves outside the key, it creates a special effect — surprise, color, tension.

---

## The Major Scale

The major scale is the most fundamental scale in Western music. "Do-re-mi-fa-sol-la-ti-do" is a major scale. It has a bright, stable, "happy" sound.

The major scale follows a specific pattern of whole steps (W) and half steps (H):

**W – W – H – W – W – W – H**

Starting from C, apply this pattern:
- C to D: whole step
- D to E: whole step
- E to F: half step
- F to G: whole step
- G to A: whole step
- A to B: whole step
- B to C: half step

Result: C – D – E – F – G – A – B – C. This is the C major scale — the seven white keys on a piano starting from C.

The same W-W-H-W-W-W-H pattern starting from any pitch gives you the major scale in that key:
- G major: G – A – B – C – D – E – F# – G (one sharp required to maintain the pattern)
- D major: D – E – F# – G – A – B – C# – D (two sharps)
- F major: F – G – A – B♭ – C – D – E – F (one flat)

## The Minor Scale

The minor scale sounds darker, more serious, or melancholic compared to the major scale.

The **natural minor scale** follows this pattern:

**W – H – W – W – H – W – W**

Starting from A (the relative minor of C major):
A – B – C – D – E – F – G – A

A natural minor uses the same notes as C major — just starting from A. This is why A minor is called the **relative minor** of C major.

Every major key has a relative minor starting on the sixth degree of the major scale.

### The Three Minor Scales

There are three variants of the minor scale. They share the same first five notes but differ in degrees 6 and 7.

**Natural minor:** W-H-W-W-H-W-W. The "pure" form. Example: A-B-C-D-E-F-G-A.

**Harmonic minor:** Raises the seventh degree by a half step. Creates an augmented second (1.5 steps) between degrees 6 and 7, giving the scale an exotic sound. The raised seventh makes the V chord (the fifth chord) major instead of minor, which strengthens the pull to the tonic. Example: A-B-C-D-E-F-G#-A.

**Melodic minor:** Raises both the sixth and seventh degrees when ascending; reverts to natural minor when descending. The ascending form avoids the augmented second of harmonic minor for smoother melodic movement. Example: ascending A-B-C-D-E-F#-G#-A; descending A-G-F-E-D-C-B-A.

In practice, musicians mix and match scale degrees from all three forms depending on the melodic and harmonic context.

## Key Signatures

Rather than writing sharps and flats on every note where they appear, music notation places them at the beginning of each line in a **key signature**. The key signature tells you which notes are sharp or flat throughout the piece.

**Key signatures with sharps (major keys):**
| Sharps | Key |
|---|---|
| 0 | C major |
| 1 (F#) | G major |
| 2 (F#, C#) | D major |
| 3 (F#, C#, G#) | A major |
| 4 (F#, C#, G#, D#) | E major |
| 5 (F#, C#, G#, D#, A#) | B major |
| 6 (F#, C#, G#, D#, A#, E#) | F# major |
| 7 (all seven sharps) | C# major |

**Key signatures with flats (major keys):**
| Flats | Key |
|---|---|
| 1 (B♭) | F major |
| 2 (B♭, E♭) | B♭ major |
| 3 (B♭, E♭, A♭) | E♭ major |
| 4 (B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭) | A♭ major |
| 5 (B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭) | D♭ major |
| 6 (B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, C♭) | G♭ major |
| 7 (all seven flats) | C♭ major |

**Memory aid for sharps:** The order of sharps is F-C-G-D-A-E-B. "Fat Cats Go Down Alleys Eating Birds." The key is always a half step above the last sharp.

**Memory aid for flats:** The order of flats is B-E-A-D-G-C-F (reverse of the sharps). "Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father." The key is the second-to-last flat (e.g., three flats = B♭, E♭, A♭ → key of E♭ major).

**Enharmonic keys:** Some keys sound the same but are spelled differently: F# major and G♭ major contain the same pitches. On a keyboard, you'd play the same keys. Composers choose spelling based on which is easier to read.

## The Circle of Fifths

The **circle of fifths** arranges all 12 major keys in a circle where each adjacent key is a perfect fifth higher (or a fourth lower). Moving clockwise adds one sharp; moving counterclockwise adds one flat.

```
           C (0)
      F(1♭)     G(1#)
   B♭(2♭)         D(2#)
  E♭(3♭)           A(3#)
   A♭(4♭)         E(4#)
      D♭(5♭)     B(5#)
           G♭/F#(6)
```

The circle of fifths is not just a memorization device — it reflects deep relationships between keys:
- Adjacent keys share all but one note
- Closely related keys (sharing most notes) produce harmonically smooth transitions
- Distant keys (opposite sides of the circle) create maximum contrast

**Relative minors** sit inside the circle: each major key's relative minor is 3 hours counterclockwise (or equivalently, the 6th degree of that major scale).

## The Pentatonic Scale

The **pentatonic scale** (five pitches) is simpler than the seven-note major or minor scale and appears in nearly every musical culture on earth.

**Major pentatonic:** The major scale with degrees 4 and 7 removed.
C major pentatonic: C – D – E – G – A (no F, no B)

**Minor pentatonic:** The natural minor scale with degrees 2 and 6 removed.
A minor pentatonic: A – C – D – E – G (no B, no F)

The minor pentatonic is one of the most-used scales in blues, rock, and pop — it works over many different chord types without clashing, making it a "safe" scale for improvisation.

## Modal Scales

Before the modern major/minor system, Western music was organized in **modes** — scales derived from different starting points of the major scale. Modes are still widely used in jazz, folk, and contemporary music.

Each mode starts on a different degree of the major scale and uses the same notes but with a different pitch as the tonal center:

| Mode | Starting Degree | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Ionian | 1 (same as major scale) | Bright, stable |
| Dorian | 2 | Minor with raised 6th; bluesy, sophisticated |
| Phrygian | 3 | Minor with flat 2nd; dark, Spanish/Flamenco character |
| Lydian | 4 | Major with raised 4th; dreamy, floating |
| Mixolydian | 5 | Major with flat 7th; blues-rock feel |
| Aeolian | 6 (same as natural minor) | Dark, serious |
| Locrian | 7 | Very dark, unstable; rarely used melodically |

**Example:** The C major scale (C-D-E-F-G-A-B) in Dorian mode starting on D: D-E-F-G-A-B-C-D. Same notes as C major, but D is the new tonal center. It has a minor feel (D to F is a minor third) but with a raised 6th (B instead of B♭), giving it a characteristic slightly brighter quality than pure minor.

Dorian and Mixolydian are the most common modes in popular music and jazz. Dorian is the backbone of many minor-key pop and folk songs. Mixolydian is the mode of countless rock riffs — it's a major scale that sounds a little rougher, a little more bluesy.

## Transposition

**Transposition** means moving a piece of music up or down by a consistent interval — playing in a different key while maintaining all the same internal relationships.

Why transpose?
- A vocalist can't reach the high notes in the written key
- A transposing instrument (clarinet, trumpet) sounds a different pitch than written
- A guitarist wants to use open-chord shapes in a different key (using a capo to raise pitch)
- A composer wants to contrast sections by moving to a brighter or darker key

To transpose: move every note by the same interval. A melody in C major moved up a perfect fifth is now in G major — every note moved up 7 half steps.

## Tonal Center and Resolution

The most important note in any key is the **tonic** — the first degree, the "home base." Music in a key gravitates toward the tonic. Phrases that end on the tonic feel complete and resolved. Phrases that end on other notes — especially the seventh degree or the fifth — feel unresolved, like they need to continue.

This pull toward the tonic is the basis of all tonal harmony. The elaborate harmonic progressions of Western classical music and the simple chord changes of pop songs are both creating, prolonging, and resolving tension relative to the tonic. Once you hear this pull, you'll hear it everywhere.
