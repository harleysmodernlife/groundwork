## Ear Training: Recognizing What You Hear

Ear training is the practice of developing your ear to understand music by listening — recognizing intervals, identifying chords, transcribing melodies, and hearing harmonic movement without looking at notation. It's the connection between the abstract theory and the actual sound. Theory without ear training produces musicians who can analyze scores but can't tell you what chord is playing. Ear training without theory produces musicians who can hear things but struggle to name or notate them. Together, they make a complete musician.

---

## Why Ear Training Matters

**For instrumentalists:** Reading notation tells you what to play; your ear tells you whether it sounds right. Players with good ears catch wrong notes, adjust intonation, and improvise confidently. Players without trained ears rely entirely on notation — they can only play what's written.

**For vocalists:** Singers cannot make adjustments without their ear. Pitch accuracy, intonation, blending with harmonies — all require hearing what you're doing and correcting in real time.

**For composers and arrangers:** Hearing the internal sound of notated music (audiation — hearing music in your mind without playing it) allows composition without constant reference to an instrument. Composers with strong audiation write fluidly; those without struggle to imagine sound on paper.

**For improvisers:** Jazz, blues, and other improvisational traditions are entirely ear-based. The theory gives the grammar; the ear makes the language fluent.

---

## The Two Branches of Ear Training

### 1. Listening Recognition (Passive)

Identifying what you hear: interval recognition, chord quality recognition, scale recognition, harmonic progression recognition. Listening to music with analytical attention.

### 2. Audiation and Production (Active)

Hearing music in your mind and producing it: singing intervals, sight-singing melody directly from notation, transcribing music by ear, writing down what you hear. The active version is harder and more useful.

Both branches develop in parallel. Passive recognition without production produces a listener who can analyze but not create. Production without recognition produces a musician who plays by feel without being able to explain what they're hearing.

## Interval Recognition

The interval — the distance between two pitches — is the fundamental unit of melodic and harmonic ear training.

### Learning Reference Songs

The fastest way to learn interval recognition is to associate each interval with a song where that interval appears prominently, ideally at the very beginning.

**Common interval reference songs:**

| Interval | Ascending Reference | Descending Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Minor 2nd (1 half step) | "Jaws" theme | "Joy to the World" (2nd note) |
| Major 2nd (2 half steps) | "Happy Birthday" (first two notes) | "Mary Had a Little Lamb" |
| Minor 3rd (3 half steps) | "Smoke on the Water" (riff) | "Hey Jude" (opening fall) |
| Major 3rd (4 half steps) | "When the Saints Go Marching In" | "Swing Low, Sweet Chariot" |
| Perfect 4th (5 half steps) | "Here Comes the Bride" | "Eine Kleine Nachtmusik" |
| Tritone (6 half steps) | "The Simpsons" theme | "Maria" (West Side Story) |
| Perfect 5th (7 half steps) | "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star" | "Feelings" |
| Minor 6th (8 half steps) | "The Entertainer" | "Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen" |
| Major 6th (9 half steps) | "My Bonnie Lies Over the Ocean" | "Nobody Knows..." |
| Minor 7th (10 half steps) | "Somewhere" (West Side Story) | "Watermelon Man" |
| Major 7th (11 half steps) | "Take On Me" (A-ha) | (very rare melodically) |
| Octave (12 half steps) | "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" | "Willow Weep for Me" |

Use your own reference songs — the important thing is that they're songs you know cold, so the interval sound is automatic.

### Practice Method

**Random interval drill:** Have a partner (or use a software tool) play two notes and name the interval. Check the answer. Repeat.

**Melodic vs. harmonic intervals:** Melodic intervals are played one after another; harmonic intervals are played simultaneously. Practice both — they feel different even at the same interval size. Harmonic intervals have a quality (consonant, dissonant, neutral) that melodic intervals only partially reveal.

**Singing intervals:** From any note, sing the note that is a perfect fifth above. A major third below. This production practice reinforces recognition.

## Chord Quality Recognition

Recognizing whether a chord is major, minor, diminished, or augmented, and eventually identifying seventh chord types.

### The Fundamental Distinction: Major vs. Minor

This is the first distinction to nail. Major sounds bright, open, stable. Minor sounds darker, more serious, inward.

**The test:** When you hear a chord, does it sound like a "happy" chord or a "sad" chord? This is a simplification, but it's a reliable first filter. Major = bright. Minor = dark.

**Sing the third:** The distinction between major and minor is the third degree. If you can sing the third of a chord you're hearing, you can tell whether it's major or minor — the major third sounds open and bright; the minor third sounds more closed.

### Diminished and Augmented

**Diminished:** Tense, unstable — like the chord is squeezing inward. Symmetrical quality (both intervals are minor thirds). Think "horror movie" chord.

**Augmented:** Floating, ambiguous, neither resolved to major nor minor. Both intervals are major thirds. Sounds like something could go multiple ways.

### Seventh Chords

- **Major seventh:** Rich, dreamy, jazz ballad quality. The major seventh creates a slightly open, unresolved but pleasant feeling above the stable major triad.
- **Dominant seventh:** Tense — there's a pull somewhere. The tritone between the third and seventh creates the tension.
- **Minor seventh:** Dark but smooth. Common in jazz, R&B, soul.
- **Half-diminished:** Tense, somber, not as acute as fully diminished.
- **Fully diminished seventh:** Maximum tension, symmetrical, ambiguous tonally (could resolve several ways).

### Practice Method

Listen to isolated chords (software, a piano, or a partner). Identify the quality. Start with major vs. minor until that distinction is automatic. Then add dominant seventh. Then other seventh chords. Build complexity gradually.

## Scale Recognition

Different scales have distinctive characters that become recognizable with practice.

**Major scale:** Bright, settled, familiar. The default in most Western music.

**Natural minor:** Darker than major. Compare A major to A minor — same rhythmic structure, very different emotional quality.

**Dorian:** Minor with a raised sixth. Slightly brighter than natural minor — not as dark. Blues scales often imply Dorian. Think of "So What" (Miles Davis) or "Eleanor Rigby."

**Phrygian:** Minor with a lowered second. Distinctive Spanish/flamenco sound due to the half-step from root to second degree.

**Mixolydian:** Major with a lowered seventh. Slightly rough, blues-influenced. Rock and folk music often use this scale.

**Pentatonic:** Sparse, open, works over many contexts. The absence of the 4th and 7th degrees gives it a floating quality.

**Blues scale:** Pentatonic with an added tritone (the "blue note"). The tritone gives it the characteristic blues tension.

**Whole tone scale:** All whole steps, completely symmetrical. Floating, dreamlike, disorienting — no half steps means no leading tone, no sense of pull or gravity.

**Diminished scale:** Alternating whole and half steps. Dense, tense, used in jazz over diminished chords.

## Harmonic Progression Recognition

Recognizing how chords move together — the direction and tension of a chord sequence.

### I – IV – V – I

This is the most fundamental progression to internalize. Play or listen to it until you can hear:
- The stability of I
- The gentle lift to IV
- The tension of V
- The resolution back to I

Once you can feel these functions, you're beginning to hear harmony, not just individual chords.

### ii – V – I

The jazz cadence. The ii chord creates a softer approach to the dominant; the V creates tension; I resolves. This progression has a specific flavor — slightly more sophisticated, more directional than I-IV-V. Once you hear it in a few jazz standards, you'll hear it everywhere.

### The Circle of Fifths in Action

Music often moves through harmonic progressions that follow the circle of fifths: each chord's root is a fourth (or fifth) from the previous. This is the strongest root motion in tonal music. Sequences like vi – ii – V – I follow the circle downward in fifths: A – D – G – C. Hearing root motion by fourths/fifths is a key aural skill.

### Deceptive Cadence (V → vi)

When you expect resolution to I but instead hear vi, there's a characteristic surprise — the music doesn't land where expected. Train your ear to hear this: the familiar approach of the V chord, then the unexpected but not dissonant vi.

## Transcription: Writing Down What You Hear

Transcription is the highest ear training skill — listening to a recording and notating exactly what's played. It develops every aural skill simultaneously: pitch, rhythm, harmony, voicing.

### How to Transcribe

1. **Listen to the whole passage first.** Don't start writing immediately. Get a feel for what's happening.
2. **Find the key.** Sing along and find what note feels like home.
3. **Find the meter.** Tap the beat and count.
4. **Transcribe the melody first.** Note by note if necessary, slowing the recording (most DAWs and apps let you slow audio without changing pitch).
5. **Add rhythm.** Once you have the pitches, notate the durations.
6. **Add harmony.** What chord is supporting each melodic phrase?
7. **Check against the recording.** Play what you've written and compare.

Start with simple melodies — folk songs, nursery rhymes, children's songs. These are short, use small intervals, and have predictable rhythms. Transcribing "Mary Had a Little Lamb" by ear is not trivial when you first start. That's fine.

## Sight-Singing

The opposite of transcription: reading notation and singing what you see. Sight-singing combines reading notation with audiation — imagining the sound of a note before you make it.

**The moveable-do system (solfege):**
- Do (1), Re (2), Mi (3), Fa (4), Sol (5), La (6), Ti (7), Do (8)
- In moveable-do, "Do" is always the tonic, regardless of key. Every note is named relative to its scale degree.

**The fixed-do system:**
- Do is always C, regardless of key. Less useful for ear training; more common in European classical conservatories.

**Sight-singing approach:**
1. Identify the key. Establish the tonic in your mind (sing or play it).
2. Identify the time signature. Establish the beat (count in).
3. Look ahead — read at least a measure ahead of where you're singing.
4. For difficult passages: identify the intervals rather than trying to feel each note from the tonic.
5. Sing at a comfortable tempo. Accuracy first, speed later.

## Practice Tools and Routines

**Daily minimum practice:** 10-15 minutes of focused ear training is more effective than an occasional long session. Daily repetition builds neural pathways more reliably than cramming.

**Apps and software:**
- **TonalEnergy:** Chromatic tuner with overtone analysis — useful for intonation work
- **Functional Ear Trainer:** Interval and chord recognition drilling in context (root position, inversions, in progressions)
- **Tenuto (musictheory.net):** Various drills including interval, chord, and scale recognition
- **EarMaster:** Comprehensive ear training software
- **iReal Pro:** Play-along with jazz and pop chord progressions — great for hearing harmony in context

**The best ear training is active listening to real music.** Pick a song you like. Try to:
- Identify the key
- Find the I, IV, V chords
- Hear where the bass note is
- Identify the intervals in the melody

This is slower than drills but builds musical understanding rather than just test-taking ability.

## Relative Pitch vs. Absolute Pitch

**Relative pitch:** Hearing intervals and relationships — how notes relate to each other and to the tonal center. Learnable with practice. What ear training develops.

**Absolute pitch (perfect pitch):** Knowing the name of a note (C, F#, B♭) upon hearing it, without any reference. Appears to be partially innate and much harder to develop in adulthood. Useful but not necessary — most professional musicians have strong relative pitch, not absolute pitch.

Do not worry about perfect pitch. Relative pitch is the skill that matters, and it's fully learnable.

---

## The Long Game

Ear training develops over years, not weeks. The skills build on each other:
1. Major vs. minor distinction
2. Basic interval recognition
3. Common chord qualities
4. Diatonic chord functions (I, IV, V)
5. ii-V-I and jazz harmony
6. Transcription of simple melodies
7. Sight-singing simple passages
8. More complex harmony, modulation, chromatic chords
9. Transcription of complex music

Don't skip steps. Each level is built on the previous one. Do the boring work — interval drills, chord identification — even when it feels rote. The moment the skill becomes automatic is when you stop feeling like you're working at it, and music starts revealing itself to you.
