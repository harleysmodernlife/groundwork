## Chords and Basic Harmony

A chord is three or more notes played simultaneously. Harmony is the study of how chords work together — which combinations create stability, which create tension, and how sequences of chords create the sense of movement and arrival that underlies most of Western music.

---

## Building Chords: Triads

The simplest chord is the **triad** — three notes built by stacking thirds. Starting from any note (the root), add a note a third above it, then another note a third above that.

### The Four Basic Triads

**Major triad:** Root + major third + perfect fifth.
- Intervals from root: 4 half steps, then 3 half steps
- C major: C – E – G
- Sound: bright, stable, "happy"

**Minor triad:** Root + minor third + perfect fifth.
- Intervals from root: 3 half steps, then 4 half steps
- C minor: C – E♭ – G
- Sound: darker, more serious, "sad"

**Diminished triad:** Root + minor third + diminished fifth.
- Intervals from root: 3 half steps, then 3 half steps
- C diminished: C – E♭ – G♭
- Sound: tense, unstable — always wants to resolve

**Augmented triad:** Root + major third + augmented fifth.
- Intervals from root: 4 half steps, then 4 half steps
- C augmented: C – E – G#
- Sound: unstable, mysterious, dreamlike

---

## Chord Notation

Chords are named after their root note and type:

- **C** = C major triad
- **Cm** (or Cmin) = C minor triad
- **Cdim** (or C°) = C diminished triad
- **Caug** (or C+) = C augmented triad

In guitar tab and pop/jazz notation, you'll see these applied to any root note: Am, F#m, B♭, Gdim, etc.

---

## Diatonic Chords: Chords in a Key

When you build triads on each degree of a major scale using only the notes in that scale, you get the **diatonic chords** of that key — the chords that "belong" to the key.

**Diatonic triads in C major (using only white keys):**

| Degree | Chord | Type |
|---|---|---|
| I (C) | C major | Major |
| ii (D) | D minor | Minor |
| iii (E) | E minor | Minor |
| IV (F) | F major | Major |
| V (G) | G major | Major |
| vi (A) | A minor | Minor |
| vii° (B) | B diminished | Diminished |

**Uppercase Roman numerals** = major chords. **Lowercase** = minor chords.

The pattern major-minor-minor-major-major-minor-diminished is the same in every major key. Learn this pattern and you know the diatonic chords in all 12 major keys.

## The Most Important Chords: I, IV, and V

The three most harmonically important chords in any key are:
- **I (tonic):** Home. Everything resolves to here.
- **IV (subdominant):** Away from home but stable. Creates a gentle lift.
- **V (dominant):** Maximum tension. Creates the strongest pull back to the tonic.

The progression I – IV – V – I is the foundation of an enormous amount of Western music: blues, folk, country, rock, classical cadences, pop. Thousands of songs reduce to some arrangement of these three chords.

**The dominant seventh chord (V7):** Adding a seventh to the V chord (a minor seventh above the root) intensifies the pull to the tonic. In C major, G7 (G – B – D – F) creates strong tension that nearly always resolves to C. The tension comes from the tritone between B and F, which resolves by half-step motion when the chord moves to C.

---

## The vi Chord (Relative Minor)

The **vi chord** (A minor in C major) is called the relative minor. It shares two notes with the I chord and creates a gentler, darker alternative to the tonic.

Many pop songs use the progression I – V – vi – IV (or its inversions). This sequence is so common it's become a cliché:

**I – V – vi – IV in C:** C – G – Am – F

Examples: dozens of pop hits follow this exact sequence. Try playing it and you'll recognize it immediately.

---

## Chord Progressions

A **chord progression** is a sequence of chords. The same chords can appear in different orders to create entirely different emotional effects.

### Common Progressions

**I – IV – V – I (Blues/Folk/Country):**
C – F – G – C
The foundation. Works in any key.

**I – vi – IV – V (Doo-wop, many pop songs):**
C – Am – F – G
"Heart and Soul," countless 1950s songs.

**I – V – vi – IV (Modern pop):**
C – G – Am – F
Extremely common in contemporary pop.

**ii – V – I (Jazz):**
Dm – G – C (in C major)
The most important progression in jazz. The ii and V together create a compound dominant function — strong pull to the I. In jazz, these chords are usually seventh chords: Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7.

**I – IV – I – V – I (12-bar blues):**
The 12-bar blues is a specific 12-measure structure:
- Bars 1-4: I chord
- Bars 5-6: IV chord
- Bars 7-8: I chord
- Bar 9: V chord
- Bar 10: IV chord
- Bars 11-12: I chord (with optional V chord turnaround)

Every blues musician knows this structure. It's the framework for improvisation, not a specific melody.

## Seventh Chords

Adding another third above a triad produces a **seventh chord** — four notes. Seventh chords are richer and more complex than triads.

### Common Seventh Chord Types

**Major seventh (maj7):** Major triad + major seventh above root.
Cmaj7: C – E – G – B. Rich, lush sound. Common in jazz, R&B, neo-soul.

**Dominant seventh (7):** Major triad + minor seventh above root.
C7: C – E – G – B♭. The tension-creating chord. The V7 chord. Used wherever strong pull to the tonic is needed.

**Minor seventh (m7):** Minor triad + minor seventh above root.
Cm7: C – E♭ – G – B♭. Dark, smooth. Extremely common in jazz, R&B, hip-hop.

**Half-diminished (m7♭5):** Diminished triad + minor seventh.
Cm7♭5 (or Cø): C – E♭ – G♭ – B♭. The ii chord in a minor key. Tense but less extreme than fully diminished.

**Fully diminished seventh (dim7):** Diminished triad + diminished seventh.
Cdim7 (or C°7): C – E♭ – G♭ – B♭♭ (A). Extremely tense. Symmetrical — divides the octave into four equal parts.

## Inversions

A chord doesn't have to have its root as the lowest note. When a note other than the root is in the bass, the chord is **inverted**.

**Root position:** Root in the bass. C major: C – E – G (C on bottom)

**First inversion:** Third in the bass. C major first inversion: E – G – C (E on bottom)

**Second inversion:** Fifth in the bass. C major second inversion: G – C – E (G on bottom)

Inversions are written with a slash notation in pop/guitar contexts:
- C/E = C major with E in the bass (first inversion)
- C/G = C major with G in the bass (second inversion)

Inversions create smoother voice leading — when chords change, individual notes can move by small intervals rather than jumping around. The bass line becomes melodic rather than jumping between roots.

---

## Voice Leading

**Voice leading** is how individual notes (voices) move from chord to chord. Smooth voice leading minimizes the distance each voice travels, creating a sense of flow rather than jumpiness.

Basic voice leading principles:
- **Common tones:** If two adjacent chords share a note, hold that note in the same voice.
- **Step motion:** When voices must move, move by the smallest interval possible (half step or whole step).
- **Contrary motion:** When the bass moves up, the upper voices can move down, and vice versa. Creates independence.

These principles explain why certain chord voicings work better than others, and why some transitions between chords sound smooth while others sound clunky.

## Cadences

A **cadence** is a chord progression that signals a phrase ending — the musical equivalent of a sentence-ending punctuation.

**Authentic cadence (V → I):** The strongest resolution. Dominant to tonic. Like a period.

**Perfect authentic cadence:** V → I with both chords in root position and the tonic note in the highest voice. Maximum resolution.

**Half cadence:** Any chord → V. Ends on the dominant, leaving tension unresolved. Like a comma — the phrase continues after.

**Plagal cadence (IV → I):** Softer resolution than V → I. The "Amen" cadence in church music.

**Deceptive cadence (V → vi):** V chord resolves to vi instead of the expected I. Surprise. Creates a prolonged, unresolved feeling.

---

## Borrowed Chords and Modulation

**Borrowed chords:** Chords taken from the parallel minor key while still in a major key (or vice versa). In C major, the ♭VII chord (B♭ major) is borrowed from C minor. Common in rock and pop. Creates color and surprise without fully changing keys.

**Secondary dominants:** A dominant seventh chord built on the fifth of a chord other than the tonic. In C major, the D7 chord (not diatonic) is the V7 of G — it temporarily treats G as a local tonic. Makes chord progressions feel more directed and harmonically rich.

**Modulation:** Fully changing keys. A piece can move from C major to G major and then back, with harmonic transitions that make the key change feel smooth or sudden depending on the effect desired.

## Applied Harmony: How to Hear It

The theory is most useful when it helps you understand what you're hearing. Active listening exercises:

**Identify I, IV, V:** Listen to a simple pop or folk song. Tap when the chord changes. Can you hear when the music feels "home" (I), "lifted" (IV), or "tense" (V)?

**Count the ii-V-I in jazz:** In any jazz standard, listen for the characteristic tension-and-release of the ii-V-I sequence. Once you hear it, you'll hear it in every song.

**Hear the deceptive cadence:** When a chord progression seems to be heading toward resolution and then doesn't quite get there, feeling slightly suspended — that's usually a deceptive cadence. Common in classical and film music to extend phrases.

**Recognize the 12-bar blues:** Listen to any blues track. Count 12 bars (measures), note where the IV chord appears (bars 5-6) and where the V chord appears (bar 9). Once you know the structure, you can play along with any blues.
