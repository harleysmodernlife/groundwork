## Time Signatures and Note Values

Time signatures and note values are the system music uses to organize time. They tell you not just what notes to play but when and for how long. This lesson goes deeper than the overview in the notation lesson — into the arithmetic of rhythm, how to count complex patterns, how to feel different meters in your body, and how composers use time signatures for expressive effect.

---

## The Logic of Note Values

Note values form a hierarchy of exact ratios:

```
Whole note (4 beats)
├── Half note (2 beats)     × 2 = Whole
│   ├── Quarter note (1 beat)     × 2 = Half
│   │   ├── Eighth note (½ beat)      × 2 = Quarter
│   │   │   ├── Sixteenth note (¼ beat)  × 2 = Eighth
│   │   │   │   └── Thirty-second note (⅛ beat)
```

Each level divides the one above it exactly in half. This binary subdivision is the foundation of simple meter.

**Critical insight:** Note values are relative, not absolute. A quarter note is not always "one beat" — it's one beat only in simple meter with a quarter note beat unit (4/4, 3/4, 2/4). In cut time (2/2), the half note gets one beat and the quarter note gets half a beat. In compound meter (6/8), the eighth note gets one sixth of a measure and the dotted quarter gets the beat.

## Simple vs. Compound Meter in Detail

### Simple Meter

The beat divides into two equal halves. One beat = two equal subdivisions.

**Simple time signatures:**
- **2/4:** Two quarter-note beats per measure. Binary, march-like feel. 1-2, 1-2.
- **3/4:** Three quarter-note beats per measure. Waltz feel. 1-2-3, 1-2-3.
- **4/4:** Four quarter-note beats per measure. The default. 1-2-3-4, 1-2-3-4.
- **2/2:** Two half-note beats per measure (cut time). Faster, more driven than 4/4.

In 4/4, the quarter note gets one beat. Subdividing: one beat divides into two eighth notes (1-and, 2-and). Further: four sixteenth notes per beat (1-e-and-a, 2-e-and-a).

**Counting 4/4:**
- Quarter notes: 1 – 2 – 3 – 4
- Eighth notes: 1-and – 2-and – 3-and – 4-and
- Sixteenth notes: 1-e-and-a – 2-e-and-a – 3-e-and-a – 4-e-and-a

### Compound Meter

The beat divides into three equal parts. One beat = three equal subdivisions.

In compound meter, the beat unit is always a dotted note (because dotted notes have three subdivisions).

**Compound time signatures:**
- **6/8:** Six eighth-note beats per measure, felt as two dotted-quarter beats. 1-and-a-2-and-a, or 1-2-3-4-5-6 (counting all eight notes).
- **9/8:** Nine eighth notes, felt as three dotted-quarter beats.
- **12/8:** Twelve eighth notes, felt as four dotted-quarter beats.

**The confusion about 6/8 vs. 3/4:** Both have six eighth-note equivalents per measure. The difference is how they're felt:
- 3/4 has THREE beats, each dividing into TWO eighth notes: 1-and, 2-and, 3-and (one-TWO-three, one-TWO-three)
- 6/8 has TWO beats, each dividing into THREE eighth notes: 1-and-a, 2-and-a (ONE-two-three, TWO-two-three)

The test: tap the beat. In 3/4, you tap three times per measure. In 6/8, you tap twice. Both have six eighth notes per measure, but the grouping is different.

**Counting 6/8:**
- Dotted quarter = one beat: 1 – 2
- Eighth notes counted as subdivisions: 1-and-a – 2-and-a
- All eighth notes: 1-2-3-4-5-6

## The Arithmetic of Rhythm

Every measure must be exactly filled. In 4/4, the note values in each measure must sum to exactly four quarter-note beats.

**Checking a measure:**
- Quarter note = 1
- Half note = 2
- Whole note = 4
- Eighth note = 0.5
- Sixteenth note = 0.25
- Dotted quarter = 1.5
- Dotted half = 3

A measure of 4/4 might contain: dotted quarter (1.5) + eighth (0.5) + half (2) = 4. Correct.

**Ties across bar lines:** A note can begin in one measure and continue into the next via a tie. The first measure must still be exactly full up to the tied note.

## Asymmetric (Irregular) Time Signatures

Meters that don't divide evenly into equal groups of two or three beats.

### 5/4

Five quarter-note beats per measure. Usually felt as 3+2 or 2+3:
- **3+2:** Strong-weak-weak-strong-weak (ONE-two-three-ONE-two)
- **2+3:** Strong-weak-strong-weak-weak (ONE-two-ONE-two-three)

Dave Brubeck's "Take Five" uses 3+2 grouping throughout. The pattern is instantly recognizable once you've heard it.

### 7/8

Seven eighth notes per measure. Common groupings:
- **2+2+3:** ONE-two-ONE-two-ONE-two-three
- **3+2+2:** ONE-two-three-ONE-two-ONE-two
- **2+3+2:** ONE-two-ONE-two-three-ONE-two

The accented first eighth note of each group is what you feel as the beat. 7/8 creates a persistent, slightly limping momentum — it's asymmetric enough to feel unusual but has a strong groove once internalized.

### 5/8

Five eighth notes per measure. Felt as 3+2 or 2+3 — like 5/4 but faster (eighth note = beat unit).

### Mixed Meters

Some music alternates between different time signatures measure by measure:
- Alternating 3/4 and 4/4
- A pattern like 3/8 – 3/8 – 2/8 repeating
- Stravinsky's Rite of Spring famously changes meter nearly every measure in some sections

Mixed meters create rhythmic unpredictability and excitement. They're common in 20th-century classical music, progressive rock, and some jazz.

## The Triplet

In simple meter, the beat normally divides into two equal parts. A **triplet** forces three equal notes into the space of two — temporarily borrowing the compound subdivision.

**Quarter note triplet:** Three quarter notes in the space of two quarter notes (one half note's worth).
**Eighth note triplet:** Three eighth notes in the space of two eighth notes (one quarter note's worth).
**Sixteenth note triplet:** Three sixteenth notes in the space of two sixteenth notes.

Triplets are marked with a "3" above or below the group, usually with a bracket.

**The triplet feel:** In blues and jazz, the eighth note triplet feel is constant — the music "swings." Instead of straight eighth notes (even 50-50 division), the beat is divided in the long-short ratio of a triplet pair (roughly 67-33). This is what swing is: a specific long-short ratio in the eighth note subdivision.

The written notation of swing music often uses straight eighth notes with a performance notation that swing is intended. Some scores write it with triplets explicitly.

---

## Tuplets

The general term for forcing a non-standard number of notes into a metric space. A triplet is a 3:2 tuplet. Others:

- **Duplet:** Two notes in the space of three (common in compound meter — fills one beat with two even notes instead of three)
- **Quintuplet:** Five notes in the space of four
- **Sextuplet:** Six notes in the space of four (essentially double-time triplets)
- **Septuplet:** Seven notes in the space of four

Tuplets become complex quickly. The rule is always: the tuplet number tells you how many notes, and the context tells you what space they fill (usually the next lower power-of-two note value).

---

## Hemiola

**Hemiola** is the temporary imposition of a 3-against-2 rhythmic relationship. In 3/4, if you accent every other beat across two measures (rather than every three), you create the feel of 2/4 — the listener briefly loses track of where beat 1 is.

Hemiola appears in Baroque dance music, Brahms, Latin music (the clave patterns), and many folk traditions. It's the rhythmic technique of implying a different meter than the written one through accent patterns.

## Conducting Patterns

Conductors use specific arm patterns to indicate different meters. These patterns reflect the internal organization of the beat.

**2/4 or 2/2:** Down-Up (beat 1 down, beat 2 up)

**3/4:** Down-Right-Up (beat 1 down, beat 2 to the right, beat 3 up)

**4/4:** Down-Left-Right-Up (beat 1 down, beat 2 left, beat 3 right, beat 4 up)

**6/8 (slow):** Down-right-right-up-left-left (six individual motions for six eighth notes)

**6/8 (fast):** Down-Up (only two beats felt; same as 2)

The downbeat always goes down. Knowing conducting patterns helps musicians feel the downbeat even when playing — you internalize the gesture.

## Feeling Time in Your Body

Reading time signatures is necessary but not sufficient. The rhythm has to live in your body, not just your brain.

**Physical internalization methods:**

**Tapping and counting simultaneously:** Tap the beat with your foot while counting the subdivisions out loud. This separates the beat from the subdivision — you must feel them independently.

**Body percussion:** In 4/4, pat your knees on beats 1 and 3, clap on beats 2 and 4. This builds the backbeat feel of rock and pop into muscle memory.

**Walking:** A walking pace at a comfortable BPM is a 2 or 4 feel. Counting in 3 while walking creates a deliberate displacement — every third step is a "1," so your left and right feet alternate which carries the downbeat. This is physically uncomfortable at first — which is exactly what learning a new meter feels like.

**Conducting while reading:** When learning a new time signature, conduct the pattern with one hand while playing or tapping with the other. Forces physical engagement with the meter.

## Why Time Signatures Matter Expressively

Composers choose time signatures for their expressive effect, not just organizational convenience.

**3/4 → waltz:** The triple meter creates a circular, rotating sensation. Waltzes, mazurkas, and many folk dances use triple meter because it suggests bodily rotation.

**4/4 → stability:** The symmetry of four beats creates balance and predictability. Most Western popular music defaults to 4/4 because it's easy to march, dance, and settle into.

**6/8 → lilt:** The two-beat feel with triplet subdivision gives folk songs, lullabies, and sea shanties their characteristic rocking quality. "Row Your Boat," Irish jigs.

**5/4 → unease:** Five beats doesn't resolve symmetrically. It creates perpetual forward momentum without a sense of settling. Used for tension in film scores and progressive rock.

**Changing meters → disorientation or complexity:** When meters change within a piece, the listener can't settle into a predictable pulse. Used by Stravinsky, Bartók, prog rock, and some jazz to create rhythmic tension.

Time and rhythm are not neutral containers — they are the first and most fundamental expressive element of music.
