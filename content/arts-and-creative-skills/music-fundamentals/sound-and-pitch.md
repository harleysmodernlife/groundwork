## Sound, Pitch, and the Musical Alphabet

Music is organized sound. To understand music — to read it, play it, compose it, or talk about it intelligently — you need to understand what sound actually is, how pitch works, and how the twelve tones of Western music relate to each other. These are the first principles everything else is built on.

---

## What Sound Is

Sound is a pressure wave moving through air (or any medium). Something vibrates — a string, a vocal cord, an air column in a tube — and that vibration pushes against neighboring air molecules, which push the next ones, and so on. Your eardrum detects the pressure variations and your brain interprets them as sound.

**Three fundamental properties:**

**Frequency:** How fast the object is vibrating, measured in cycles per second (Hertz, Hz). Fast vibration = high pitch. Slow vibration = low pitch. The note A above middle C vibrates at exactly 440 Hz — 440 times per second. The A one octave higher vibrates at 880 Hz, exactly twice as fast.

**Amplitude:** How big the pressure variations are. Large amplitude = loud sound. Small amplitude = quiet sound. In audio, amplitude corresponds to volume; in physics, to the energy of the wave.

**Timbre (pronounced TAM-ber):** The characteristic quality that makes a piano sound different from a guitar playing the same note at the same volume. Both might vibrate at 440 Hz, but each produces a complex mixture of the fundamental frequency and higher-frequency overtones in different proportions. The overtone mixture is what we perceive as timbre — the "color" of a sound.

---

## Pitch and the Octave

**Pitch** is the subjective experience of frequency — we hear some sounds as higher and some as lower. Pitch corresponds directly to frequency, but it's measured differently: musicians use note names, not Hertz.

**The octave** is the most fundamental interval in music. When you double the frequency, you get a note that sounds like the "same note, higher." A string vibrating at 110 Hz and another at 220 Hz and another at 440 Hz all produce an A. They're the same note in different registers — one octave apart.

This relationship (doubling = one octave up, halving = one octave down) appears everywhere in music theory and physics. It's why an octave is called an octave: there are 8 note names between any note and its octave.

## The Musical Alphabet

Western music uses seven letter names for notes:

**A – B – C – D – E – F – G**

After G, the sequence repeats: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C... This cycle never ends; each repetition is one octave higher than the previous one.

On a piano keyboard, the white keys follow this sequence starting from any A. Middle C — the C near the center of the piano — is the reference point for locating notes on a staff (explained in the notation lesson).

### Sharps and Flats

Between most adjacent notes, there are additional pitches. Between A and B, there's a note called A-sharp (written A#) or B-flat (written B♭). These are the same pitch with two different names — the name used depends on context.

The 12 notes (including sharps/flats) in order:
A — A#/B♭ — B — C — C#/D♭ — D — D#/E♭ — E — F — F#/G♭ — G — G#/A♭ — (back to A)

Notice: there's no sharp/flat between B and C, or between E and F. These pairs are "naturally" a half step apart — there's no note in between.

**Sharp (#):** Raises a note by one half step (one piano key to the right).

**Flat (♭):** Lowers a note by one half step (one piano key to the left).

**Natural (♮):** Cancels a previous sharp or flat, returning the note to its natural pitch.

---

## Half Steps and Whole Steps

The smallest interval in Western music is the **half step** (also called a semitone). On a piano, it's the distance from any key to the immediately adjacent key — white or black. From C to C# is a half step. From E to F is a half step.

A **whole step** (whole tone) is two half steps: from C to D is a whole step (passing through C#/D♭). From A to B is a whole step.

Half steps and whole steps are the building blocks of all scales, intervals, and chords. Almost everything in music theory can be described in terms of how many half steps separate two notes.

---

## Intervals

An **interval** is the distance between two notes. Intervals have specific names based on how many half steps separate them:

| Half Steps | Interval Name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Unison | C to C |
| 1 | Minor second (half step) | C to C# |
| 2 | Major second (whole step) | C to D |
| 3 | Minor third | C to E♭ |
| 4 | Major third | C to E |
| 5 | Perfect fourth | C to F |
| 6 | Tritone | C to F# |
| 7 | Perfect fifth | C to G |
| 8 | Minor sixth | C to A♭ |
| 9 | Major sixth | C to A |
| 10 | Minor seventh | C to B♭ |
| 11 | Major seventh | C to B |
| 12 | Octave | C to C (next octave) |

**Consonant** intervals sound stable and resolved: unisons, thirds, fifths, sixths, octaves. **Dissonant** intervals create tension that feels like it needs to resolve: seconds, sevenths, the tritone.

These aren't value judgments — consonance and dissonance are both essential to music. Tension and resolution, dissonance and consonance, are what create forward motion in music.

---

## Enharmonic Equivalents

Two different names for the same pitch:
- F# = G♭
- C# = D♭
- D# = E♭
- G# = A♭
- A# = B♭

These are **enharmonic equivalents**. They sound identical but are spelled differently. Which name you use depends on context — the key signature and harmonic function of the note in a given piece. In the key of D major, you'd call it F#. In the key of D♭ major, you'd call it G♭. Same sound, different theoretical role.

---

## Register and Range

**Register** refers to how high or low a note is in pitch. Middle C is in the middle register. Notes above it are higher register; notes below are lower register.

**Range** is the span from the lowest to highest notes:
- Human hearing: roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz)
- Standard piano: 27.5 Hz (A0) to 4186 Hz (C8) — 88 keys, about 7+ octaves
- Human voice: typically 2-4 octaves depending on the singer
- Violin: about 3.5 octaves
- Trumpet: about 2.5 octaves

Different instruments and voices occupy different parts of the pitch range, which is why orchestras can play many notes simultaneously without everything colliding in the same frequency space.

---

## Concert Pitch and A440

By convention, the A above middle C is tuned to 440 Hz in most modern performance. This is called **A440** or **concert pitch**. All other notes are tuned in relationship to this reference.

Before this standardization, different orchestras, cities, and eras used different tuning references. A440 was standardized internationally in 1939. Some orchestras today use slightly different references (A441 or A442 for a slightly brighter sound), but 440 remains the global default.

When an orchestra "tunes up" before a concert, the oboe plays A440 and all other instruments adjust to match. The oboe is used because its pitch is stable and cuts through the ensemble.

---

## Why This Matters

Understanding pitch and the musical alphabet is the prerequisite for everything that follows: scales (patterns of half and whole steps), intervals (the building blocks of chords), keys (which notes belong together), and notation (reading and writing music). These concepts are simple but they underpin everything. If you understand what a half step is and can name the 12 notes in sequence, you have the foundation.
