## Reading Standard Music Notation

Standard music notation is a system for writing down music with precision — not just which notes to play, but how long to hold each one, how loud, how fast, and with what character. Learning to read music doesn't require years of study before it becomes useful. The fundamentals can be learned in a few hours, and fluency builds with practice over months.

---

## The Staff

Music is written on a **staff** (plural: staves) — five parallel horizontal lines. Notes are placed on the lines and in the spaces between them. The higher on the staff, the higher the pitch.

Lines and spaces from bottom to top:
- Line 1 (bottom)
- Space 1
- Line 2
- Space 2
- Line 3 (middle)
- Space 3
- Line 4
- Space 4
- Line 5 (top)

Notes sit on lines (the line passes through the center of the notehead) or in spaces (the notehead fills the space between two lines).

---

## Clefs

A **clef** is the symbol at the left end of the staff that defines which notes correspond to which lines and spaces.

### Treble Clef (G Clef)

The treble clef looks like an ornate capital G. It curls around line 2, indicating that line 2 = G4 (the G above middle C).

Notes on the treble clef from bottom to top:

**Lines (bottom to top):** E – G – B – D – F
Memory aid: "Every Good Boy Does Fine"

**Spaces (bottom to top):** F – A – C – E
Memory aid: They spell "FACE"

So: below the staff is D-E, then F (first space), G (first line), A (second space), B (second line), C (third space), D (third line), E (fourth space), F (fourth line), G (top space), A (top line area)...

Middle C is one ledger line below the treble clef staff.

Used for: right hand piano, violin, flute, oboe, trumpet, voice (soprano, mezzo, alto), guitar (sounds an octave lower than written — octave treble clef).

### Bass Clef (F Clef)

The bass clef looks like a backwards C with two dots. The dots straddle line 4, indicating that line 4 = F3 (the F below middle C).

**Lines (bottom to top):** G – B – D – F – A
Memory aid: "Good Boys Do Fine Always"

**Spaces (bottom to top):** A – C – E – G
Memory aid: "All Cows Eat Grass"

Middle C is one ledger line above the bass clef staff.

Used for: left hand piano, bass guitar, cello, trombone, tuba, bass voice.

### Grand Staff

For piano and keyboard instruments, the treble and bass clef staves are connected by a brace on the left side, creating the **grand staff**. The right hand reads the treble clef; the left hand reads the bass clef. Middle C sits in the gap between the two staves — one ledger line below the treble clef and one ledger line above the bass clef.

---

## Ledger Lines

When notes go higher than the top line or lower than the bottom line of the staff, **ledger lines** are added — short horizontal lines above or below the staff, each representing an additional line of the staff extended.

Reading ledger lines: continue the same alternating line-space-line-space pattern beyond the staff. Middle C is the first ledger line below the treble clef. High C (two octaves above middle C) is one ledger line above the treble clef.

Avoid reading notes far from the staff ledger by ledger — instead, look for the closest notes you know and count from there.

## Note Values (Notation)

**Whole note:** Open oval, no stem. Lasts 4 beats in 4/4 time.

**Half note:** Open oval with a stem. Lasts 2 beats.

**Quarter note:** Filled oval with a stem. Lasts 1 beat.

**Eighth note:** Filled oval with a stem and one flag. Lasts 1/2 beat. When multiple eighth notes appear in a row, flags are replaced by a beam connecting the stems.

**Sixteenth note:** Filled oval with a stem and two flags (or double beam). Lasts 1/4 beat.

**Dotted notes:** A dot after a notehead adds half the note's value. Dotted quarter = 1.5 beats. Dotted half = 3 beats.

**Ties:** A curved line connecting two noteheads of the same pitch combines their durations. The second note is not re-attacked; the first note is held for the combined duration.

---

## Rest Symbols

Every note value has a corresponding rest:

- **Whole rest:** A filled rectangle hanging below line 4. Four beats of silence.
- **Half rest:** A filled rectangle sitting on top of line 3. Two beats of silence.
- **Quarter rest:** A Z-like symbol. One beat of silence.
- **Eighth rest:** A "7" with a dot. Half beat of silence.
- **Sixteenth rest:** Like an eighth rest with an extra flag. Quarter beat of silence.

---

## Time Signatures

At the beginning of a piece (after the clef and key signature), two stacked numbers appear: the **time signature**.

The top number = beats per measure.
The bottom number = which note value = one beat (4 = quarter note, 8 = eighth note, 2 = half note).

**4/4:** Four quarter-note beats per measure. The most common.
**3/4:** Three quarter-note beats per measure.
**6/8:** Six eighth-note beats per measure (felt as two dotted-quarter beats).
**2/2:** Two half-note beats per measure (cut time, indicated with a C with a line through it).

**Common time:** A "C" symbol, equivalent to 4/4.
**Cut time:** A "C" with a vertical line, equivalent to 2/2.

---

## Key Signatures

After the clef, key signatures appear: one or more sharps or flats placed on specific lines/spaces of the staff. These apply to all notes on those lines/spaces throughout the piece unless cancelled by a natural sign (♮) or a new key signature.

A key signature of two sharps (F# and C#) means: every F and every C in the piece is sharp, regardless of octave, unless a natural sign appears.

See the Scales and Keys lesson for the complete key signature reference.

---

## Accidentals

An **accidental** is a sharp, flat, or natural sign placed directly before a note — not in the key signature — that temporarily alters that specific note.

**Sharp (#):** Raises the note one half step.
**Flat (♭):** Lowers the note one half step.
**Natural (♮):** Cancels a key signature sharp or flat for the duration of the measure.

Accidentals apply to all subsequent notes on the same line or space within the same measure, unless cancelled. At the next bar line, the accidental expires and the key signature resumes.

**Double sharp (×):** Raises a note two half steps (a whole step). Used when the key signature already sharpens the note but the notation needs it raised again.
**Double flat (♭♭):** Lowers a note two half steps.

## Dynamics

Dynamic markings indicate volume:

| Symbol | Italian | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ppp | pianississimo | As quiet as possible |
| pp | pianissimo | Very quiet |
| p | piano | Quiet |
| mp | mezzo piano | Moderately quiet |
| mf | mezzo forte | Moderately loud |
| f | forte | Loud |
| ff | fortissimo | Very loud |
| fff | fortississimo | As loud as possible |

**Hairpins:** Gradual dynamic changes.
- **Crescendo (<):** Gradually louder
- **Decrescendo/Diminuendo (>):** Gradually softer

**sfz (sforzando):** A sudden, sharp accent on a single note.
**fp:** Forte-piano — loud, then immediately soft.

---

## Tempo Markings

Tempo is indicated above the staff, often with an Italian term or a metronome marking:

**♩ = 120** means: 120 quarter-note beats per minute.

Common Italian tempo terms:
- **Grave:** Very slow, solemn
- **Largo:** Slow and broad
- **Adagio:** Slow
- **Andante:** Walking pace
- **Moderato:** Moderate
- **Allegretto:** Moderately fast
- **Allegro:** Fast
- **Vivace:** Lively and fast
- **Presto:** Very fast
- **Prestissimo:** As fast as possible

**Ritardando (rit.):** Gradually slowing down.
**Accelerando (accel.):** Gradually speeding up.
**A tempo:** Return to the original tempo after a deviation.

---

## Articulation Marks

These indicate how individual notes should be played:

**Staccato (•):** A dot above or below the notehead. Play the note short and detached — shorter than its full written value.

**Legato / Slur:** A curved line connecting a group of notes. Play them smoothly connected, without silence between them. Different from a tie — a slur connects different pitches; a tie connects the same pitch.

**Accent (>):** Emphasize this note — play it louder than surrounding notes.

**Tenuto (—):** Hold the note for its full value; slightly emphasize it. Play it connected and sustained.

**Fermata (𝄐):** Hold the note longer than its written value — the performer or conductor decides how long.

**Trill (tr~~~):** Rapidly alternate between the written note and the note above it.

---

## Repeat Signs and Structure

**Repeat sign (:|):** Two dots inside a double bar line. When you reach this symbol, return to the previous :|: symbol (or to the beginning if there's only one) and play that section again.

**First and second endings:** When a repeated section has a different ending:
- Play through the first time, take the "1st ending" bracket
- Repeat, then skip the first ending and play the "2nd ending" instead

**D.C. (Da Capo):** "From the head" — return to the beginning.

**D.S. (Dal Segno):** "From the sign" — return to the 𝄋 symbol.

**Fine:** The end (when it appears mid-page after a D.C. or D.S.).

**Coda (𝄌):** Jump to the coda section (end section) when instructed.

## Reading a New Piece

A systematic approach for first reading a new piece:

1. **Check the key signature.** Know what's sharp or flat before you play anything.
2. **Check the time signature.** Know the beat and meter.
3. **Check the tempo marking.** Understand the intended speed and character.
4. **Scan the structure.** Repeats, D.C./D.S., first/second endings — understand the form before playing.
5. **Identify the range.** Where are the highest and lowest notes? Any particularly wide jumps?
6. **Clap or count the rhythm** of difficult passages before adding pitch.
7. **Play slowly.** Slow enough that you can read ahead. If you have to stop to decode a note, you're playing too fast.

The goal of early reading is accuracy, not speed. Speed comes from familiarity. Play slowly and correctly rather than fast and wrong — wrong repetitions teach the wrong pattern.
