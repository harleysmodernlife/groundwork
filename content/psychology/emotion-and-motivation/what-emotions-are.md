Emotions are among the most vivid aspects of human experience — and among the most scientifically complex. Are they biological reactions, cultural constructions, or something in between? Do they precede thought or follow from it? What even is an emotion, and how do we know what we're feeling?

---

## 1. What Is an Emotion?

An emotion is not a single thing — it involves multiple components:

- **Physiological arousal**: changes in heart rate, breathing, muscle tension, skin conductance, hormone release
- **Behavioral expression**: facial expressions, body posture, voice tone, action tendencies (approaching, fleeing, freezing)
- **Cognitive appraisal**: the interpretation of a situation as threatening, wonderful, unfair, etc.
- **Subjective experience**: the felt quality of the emotion — what it's like to be afraid or joyful

Moods are different from emotions: moods are lower-intensity, longer-lasting, and often don't have a specific object ("I'm in a good mood" vs. "I'm happy because I got the job").

---

## 2. Basic Emotions

Paul Ekman, studying facial expressions across cultures, proposed a set of **basic emotions** that are biologically based and universally expressed:

- **Happiness** (joy)
- **Sadness**
- **Fear**
- **Anger**
- **Disgust**
- **Surprise**

(Later researchers added **contempt** and debated others.)

Evidence for universality: people in isolated cultures with no exposure to Western media recognize these emotional expressions in photographs at above-chance rates. And people produce the same expressions even when they can't observe others (congenitally blind people display the same facial expressions in equivalent emotional situations).

**Critics** argue the universality is overstated and that context matters more than Ekman acknowledges — the same expression can mean different things in different cultures.

## 3. Theories of Emotion

### James-Lange Theory

William James (and independently Carl Lange, 1880s): emotions are the *perception* of physiological changes. You don't tremble because you're afraid; you're afraid because you notice you're trembling. We run from a bear, notice our racing heart and legs churning, and this bodily experience *is* the fear.

**Evidence supporting**: manipulations of physical states can induce emotional states (inducing faster heart rates through exercise increases anxiety-like reports; botox injections that prevent frowning reduce the intensity of sadness).

**Limitation**: similar physiological arousal can be associated with very different emotions (fear and excitement have similar physiological profiles).

### Cannon-Bard Theory

Walter Cannon argued that physiological responses are too slow and too undifferentiated to generate specific emotions. Instead, sensory input simultaneously triggers physiological arousal **and** emotional experience, both mediated by the thalamus. The body and the emotion co-occur but neither causes the other.

### Schachter-Singer Two-Factor Theory

Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer (1962): emotion is determined by **two factors**: (1) physiological arousal, and (2) cognitive labeling of that arousal. We experience arousal and look to context to figure out *why* we're aroused — then label the emotion accordingly.

**Classic experiment**: participants given epinephrine (which causes physiological arousal) were either correctly informed of side effects, misinformed (no mention), or misled (about different side effects). They were then exposed to either an angry confederate or a euphoric confederate. The misled/uninformed participants, unable to attribute their arousal to the injection, adopted the emotion of the confederate (euphoric or angry). Those who were correctly informed felt neither — they attributed their arousal to the drug.

**Implication**: arousal is non-specific; the same arousal gets labeled as fear on a shaky bridge vs. excitement at a party.

**Misattribution of arousal**: in one study, men walking across a scary high suspension bridge were more likely to call an attractive female researcher afterward than men who crossed a stable low bridge — interpreting their arousal from the bridge as attraction.

### Appraisal Theory

Richard Lazarus argued that **cognitive appraisal** — the evaluation of what a situation means for one's well-being — is the primary determinant of emotion. The same event (loss of a job) produces different emotions depending on how it's appraised (threat vs. opportunity vs. injustice). Appraisal can be conscious or automatic.

### Constructionist Theory (Barrett)

Lisa Feldman Barrett argues that emotions are not discrete, pre-wired states triggered by events. Instead, the brain continually makes **predictions** (constructs) about the causes of internal sensory signals — and these constructions, shaped by past experience and cultural concepts, produce emotional experience. Emotions are actively made, not passively triggered.

## 4. The Role of the Amygdala

The **amygdala** is central to emotional processing, especially fear. It:
- Detects threatening stimuli — rapidly and automatically, often before conscious awareness
- Triggers fear responses (heart rate, freeze or flee, arousal)
- Consolidates emotional memories — emotionally arousing events are remembered better because the amygdala activates the hippocampus during encoding

Joseph LeDoux identified two pathways for fear:
- **Low road**: sensory thalamus → amygdala (fast, automatic, pre-conscious)
- **High road**: sensory thalamus → cortex → amygdala (slower, conscious evaluation)

This explains why phobias can trigger automatic fear responses before any conscious thought.

---

## 5. Emotional Expression

**Facial Action Coding System (FACS)**: Ekman developed this system for objectively coding all possible facial muscle movements. It allows systematic study of emotional expression.

**Facial feedback hypothesis**: facial expressions not only express emotions but can also influence them. Smiling (even artificially, as in the "pencil in mouth" studies — now partially replicated with controversy) can slightly increase positive affect; frowning can deepen negative mood.

**Cultural display rules**: cultures differ in when and how emotions are expressed. Japanese participants watching disturbing films showed similar facial expressions to Americans when alone but suppressed negative expressions in the presence of an authority figure — demonstrating that emotional expression, if not emotional experience, is regulated by social norms.

---

## 6. Managing Emotions

**Emotion regulation** is the process of influencing which emotions occur, when they occur, and how they're expressed. Strategies include:

- **Cognitive reappraisal**: reinterpreting a situation to change its emotional impact ("the job rejection means I'm available for something better"). Generally adaptive — reduces negative emotion without physiological costs.
- **Expressive suppression**: hiding emotional expression. Reduces outward expression but not internal arousal; can have social costs and cognitive costs (suppressing requires effort).
- **Rumination**: repeatedly dwelling on negative emotions. Amplifies negative affect; associated with depression.
- **Mindfulness**: non-judgmental awareness of current experience, including emotions. Associated with better emotional regulation and well-being.

---

## Summary

Emotions involve physiological arousal, expressive behavior, cognitive appraisal, and subjective experience. Basic emotions (happiness, sadness, fear, anger, disgust, surprise) appear universal across cultures. Major theories of emotion — James-Lange (emotion = perception of physiological changes), Cannon-Bard (co-occurrence), two-factor (arousal + cognitive label), appraisal (meaning-making), and constructionist (prediction and cultural concepts) — each capture different aspects of emotional processes. The amygdala is central to fear and emotional memory. Cognitive reappraisal is among the most effective and adaptive emotion regulation strategies.
