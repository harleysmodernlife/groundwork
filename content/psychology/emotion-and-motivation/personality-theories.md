Personality is the consistent pattern of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors that distinguish one person from another and remain relatively stable across time and situations. Understanding personality means grappling with fundamental questions: What dimensions describe human personality? Where does personality come from? How stable is it? And can it change?

---

## 1. The Psychoanalytic Approach

Freud's theory of personality was among the first systematic accounts of why people differ from each other.

**Freud's structural model:**
- **Id**: the primitive, unconscious reservoir of drives — seeking immediate gratification of sexual and aggressive impulses (operates on the pleasure principle)
- **Ego**: the rational self — balances the id's demands against reality (operates on the reality principle)
- **Superego**: the internalized moral standards of society — conscience and ego ideal; produces guilt when violated

**Defense mechanisms**: the ego protects itself from anxiety through unconscious strategies:
- **Repression**: pushing threatening thoughts out of consciousness
- **Rationalization**: creating acceptable explanations for unacceptable motivations
- **Projection**: attributing one's own unacceptable thoughts to others
- **Reaction formation**: behaving opposite to how one feels
- **Displacement**: redirecting impulses toward safer targets
- **Sublimation**: channeling unacceptable impulses into socially productive activities

**Neo-Freudians**: Adler (inferiority complex, striving for superiority), Jung (collective unconscious, archetypes), Erikson (psychosocial stages across the lifespan), and Horney (basic anxiety, moving toward/against/away from people) extended and modified Freud's ideas.

## 2. Trait Approaches

Rather than positing unconscious mechanisms, trait theories describe personality in terms of stable dispositional dimensions.

### Allport's Trait Theory

Gordon Allport identified traits as genuine dispositions that cause consistent behavior. He distinguished:
- **Cardinal traits**: a single dominant trait that shapes virtually all of a person's behavior (e.g., Albert Schweitzer's humanitarianism)
- **Central traits**: 5–10 core characteristics describing a person (honest, warm, ambitious...)
- **Secondary traits**: situational preferences that show up inconsistently

### The Big Five (OCEAN)

The dominant modern framework, derived from factor analysis of personality ratings:

- **Openness to Experience**: curiosity, imagination, appreciation for art, intellectual openness vs. conventional, narrow interests
- **Conscientiousness**: organization, reliability, self-discipline, goal-directedness vs. careless, impulsive
- **Extraversion**: sociability, talkativeness, assertiveness, positive emotionality vs. reserved, solitary
- **Agreeableness**: cooperative, trusting, empathic vs. suspicious, critical, competitive
- **Neuroticism** (vs. emotional stability): emotional volatility, anxiety, moodiness vs. calm, emotionally stable

The Big Five emerge consistently across cultures and methods. Traits have substantial heritability (40–60%) and show meaningful stability across the lifespan, though personality continues to change gradually — people tend to become more conscientious and agreeable with age.

### Eysenck's Three-Factor Model

Hans Eysenck proposed three "super-traits": **Extraversion** (E), **Neuroticism** (N), and **Psychoticism** (P). He linked these to biological systems — introversion/extraversion to cortical arousal levels; neuroticism to limbic system reactivity.

## 3. Social-Cognitive Approaches

**Walter Mischel** challenged trait theories in 1968, arguing that behavior is less consistent across situations than trait theory implies. He emphasized **situational factors** — specific contexts strongly determine behavior.

**Albert Bandura's social-cognitive theory**: personality results from the interaction of:
- **Person variables**: goals, expectancies, competencies, self-regulatory systems
- **Behavior**: what we actually do
- **Environment**: social situations and their consequences

**Reciprocal determinism**: person, behavior, and environment all mutually influence each other in an ongoing cycle.

**Self-efficacy** (Bandura): belief in one's ability to perform specific behaviors. People with high self-efficacy for a task set higher goals, persist longer, and perform better. Self-efficacy is domain-specific, not a general self-esteem.

**Mischel's "if-then" behavioral signatures**: what looks like inconsistency across situations may actually be consistent *if-then* patterns — if someone cuts in front of me, *then* I get angry; if someone compliments me, *then* I feel open and generous. These signatures characterize personality just as reliably as cross-situational traits.

## 4. Humanistic Theories

**Carl Rogers' person-centered theory**: personality is organized around the **self-concept** — one's view of who one is. Healthy personality develops when the self-concept aligns with actual experience and when people receive **unconditional positive regard** — acceptance regardless of behavior.

**Conditions of worth**: when parents attach acceptance to certain behaviors ("I'll love you if you're successful"), people learn to deny experiences that conflict with these conditions — creating incongruence between experience and self-concept, and psychological distress.

Rogers believed people have an inherent actualizing tendency — a drive toward growth, fulfillment, and authenticity.

**Maslow's self-actualizing person**: Maslow described self-actualizing individuals (based on biographical study of Lincoln, Einstein, and others) as having: clear perception of reality, acceptance of themselves and others, spontaneity and creativity, autonomy, deep relationships, peak experiences, and a sense of mission.

## 5. Biological Approaches

Personality has substantial heritability. Twin studies show identical twins raised apart have more similar personalities than fraternal twins raised together — suggesting genes are more important than shared environment for personality.

Key biological correlates:
- **Extraversion**: linked to lower baseline cortical arousal; extroverts seek stimulation to reach optimal arousal; introverts are already close to their optimum and seek less stimulation
- **Neuroticism**: linked to reactivity of the limbic system (amygdala, hippocampus)
- **Serotonin and dopamine systems**: linked to emotional stability and sensation-seeking

**Temperament**: biologically based emotional and behavioral dispositions present from infancy (easy, difficult, slow-to-warm-up babies). Temperament interacts with parenting and environment to shape adult personality.

---

## Summary

Personality is the stable pattern of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors that distinguish individuals. Psychoanalytic theory emphasizes unconscious drives and defense mechanisms. Trait approaches — particularly the Big Five (OCEAN) — describe personality along dimensions that are consistent across cultures and have moderate heritability. Social-cognitive approaches highlight how person, behavior, and environment mutually shape each other; Bandura's self-efficacy is especially well-supported. Humanistic theories focus on the self-concept and actualizing tendency. Biological approaches link traits to genetics, neural systems, and temperament.
