Why do we do what we do? Motivation is the force that initiates, directs, and sustains behavior toward a goal. Psychologists have proposed numerous theories explaining motivation — from basic biological drives to the subtlest human needs for growth and meaning. Understanding motivation is key to understanding why people work, learn, create, and persevere or give up.

---

## 1. Instinct Theory

Early theories proposed that behaviors are driven by **instincts** — innate, fixed patterns of behavior triggered by specific stimuli. William James listed 37 human instincts; William McDougall listed 18.

**Problem**: this approach was circular and explanatorily empty. Calling a behavior an "instinct" simply renamed it without explaining it. And humans are far more flexible than instinct theory allows.

**Legacy**: the concept survived in evolutionary psychology — specific behavioral tendencies (preferences for sweet foods, fear of heights, certain mate preferences) can be understood as evolved adaptations.

---

## 2. Drive Reduction Theory (Hull)

Clark Hull (1940s) proposed that **biological needs** create **drives** — unpleasant states of tension that motivate behavior to satisfy the need. Satisfying the need reduces the drive.

- Hunger (need) → hunger drive → eating → drive reduced → homeostasis restored

**Homeostasis**: the body's tendency to maintain a stable internal state. The hypothalamus monitors internal states (blood glucose, body temperature, fluid levels) and triggers motivational states when they deviate from optimal range.

**Limitation**: humans seek stimulation and novelty even when drives are satisfied — people eat when not hungry, seek new experiences, and climb mountains. Drive reduction theory can't explain intrinsically motivated behavior for its own sake.

---

## 3. Optimal Arousal Theory

Rather than minimizing arousal (drive reduction), we seek an **optimal level of arousal** — not too low (boredom) and not too high (anxiety).

**Yerkes-Dodson Law**: performance is best at moderate arousal levels. For simple tasks, higher arousal is better; for complex or creative tasks, lower arousal is better.

- Highly aroused people do well at simple, routine tasks
- Moderate arousal is optimal for complex problems
- Very low arousal (boredom/fatigue) impairs performance

This explains why people seek stimulation when understimulated (sensation-seeking behavior) and calm when overstimulated.

## 4. Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

Abraham Maslow (1943) proposed that human needs are hierarchically organized — lower needs must be substantially met before higher needs become motivating.

**The hierarchy (bottom to top):**

1. **Physiological needs**: survival basics — food, water, sleep, warmth, air, shelter
2. **Safety needs**: security, stability, freedom from fear; predictable environment
3. **Belonging and love needs**: social connection, intimate relationships, group membership
4. **Esteem needs**: achievement, competence, recognition, self-respect
5. **Self-actualization**: realizing one's full potential; creative expression; "becoming everything one is capable of becoming"

**Later addition — Self-transcendence**: Maslow added a sixth level beyond self-actualization: connection to something beyond the self (spirituality, helping others, peak experiences).

**Criticisms:**
- The hierarchy is too rigid — people sometimes prioritize higher needs despite unmet lower ones (artists who starve for their work)
- The distinction between levels is culturally variable
- The concept of self-actualization is difficult to define and measure empirically

**Legacy**: Maslow's framework remains enormously influential in humanistic psychology, management theory, and education — despite limited empirical validation of the specific hierarchy.

## 5. Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation

**Intrinsic motivation**: doing something because it is inherently interesting, enjoyable, or satisfying. Reading a book you love; playing an instrument for pleasure; painting for its own sake.

**Extrinsic motivation**: doing something to obtain an external reward or avoid a punishment. Working for a paycheck; studying to avoid failing.

### The Overjustification Effect

Adding extrinsic rewards for an intrinsically motivated activity can *undermine* intrinsic motivation.

**Classic study** (Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, 1973): children who liked drawing were either given an "expected reward" (told they'd get a certificate), an "unexpected reward" (given a certificate afterward), or no reward. Two weeks later, the expected-reward group showed significantly less interest in drawing than the other groups.

**Explanation**: when people receive external rewards for something they do freely, they may reattribute their motivation — "I must be doing this for the reward, not because I enjoy it." The reward undermines the sense of autonomous, self-determined activity.

**Implications for practice:**
- Avoid expected, tangible rewards for activities that are already intrinsically motivating
- Praise for competence ("you're really good at this") maintains intrinsic motivation better than rewards; it provides information rather than control
- Offering choices and acknowledging feelings preserves autonomy and intrinsic motivation

## 6. Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan)

**Self-determination theory (SDT)** proposes that humans have three universal psychological needs:

1. **Autonomy**: feeling that behavior is self-chosen and self-directed
2. **Competence**: feeling effective and capable
3. **Relatedness**: feeling connected to and cared for by others

When these needs are met, intrinsic motivation flourishes and well-being increases. Environments that undermine these needs (controlling rewards, excessive criticism, social isolation) reduce intrinsic motivation and well-being.

SDT also explains how **extrinsic** motivation can be more or less self-determined:
- **External regulation**: doing something to get a reward or avoid punishment (least autonomous)
- **Introjection**: doing something to avoid guilt or to maintain self-esteem ("should")
- **Identification**: doing something because you personally value it, even if not inherently enjoyable
- **Integration**: the activity is fully aligned with your identity and values (most autonomous)

The goal of effective education, parenting, and management is to help people move toward more autonomous forms of motivation.

## 7. Achievement Motivation and Goal-Setting

**Achievement motivation** (n-Ach, McClelland): the desire to excel, solve difficult problems, and outperform others. People high in achievement motivation tend to prefer moderately difficult challenges, take personal responsibility, and seek feedback.

**Goal-setting theory** (Locke & Latham): specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than vague ("do your best") or easy goals — provided the person is committed to the goal and receives feedback.

**Approach vs. avoidance goals**:
- **Approach goals**: pursuing positive outcomes ("I want to run a 5K")
- **Avoidance goals**: avoiding negative outcomes ("I don't want to embarrass myself")

Approach goals are generally associated with better well-being and more sustained motivation. Avoidance goals are associated with anxiety.

**Growth vs. fixed mindset** (Carol Dweck):
- **Fixed mindset**: intelligence and ability are fixed traits; failure means you lack ability → avoid challenges
- **Growth mindset**: abilities can be developed through effort and learning; failure is information → embrace challenges

Cultivating a growth mindset is associated with greater persistence, resilience, and achievement — and it can be taught.

---

## Summary

Motivation ranges from basic homeostatic drives (drive reduction theory) to optimal arousal seeking, to complex needs for competence, autonomy, and self-actualization. Maslow's hierarchy organizes needs from survival to self-actualization, though the hierarchy is less rigid than the original model suggests. Intrinsic motivation — doing things for their inherent interest — is undermined by controlling external rewards. Self-determination theory identifies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as universal psychological needs. Goal-setting, approach orientation, and growth mindset all support sustained motivation and achievement.
