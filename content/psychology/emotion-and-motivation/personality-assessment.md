Measuring personality is fundamentally harder than measuring height or temperature. Personality is an invisible construct — it must be inferred from what people do, say, or report about themselves. Different assessment approaches make different assumptions about what personality is and how best to measure it.

---

## 1. Self-Report Measures (Personality Inventories)

The most widely used approach: ask people to describe themselves using structured questionnaires.

### Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI-3)

Originally developed in 1943 (now in its third revision), the MMPI is the most widely researched clinical personality inventory. It uses hundreds of true-false items grouped into clinical scales (depression, schizophrenia, hysteria, etc.) and validity scales.

**Validity scales** are a distinctive feature: they detect response biases:
- **Lie scale (L)**: endorsing items that are favorable but rarely true ("I never get angry")
- **Infrequency scale (F)**: endorsing unusual items — suggests random responding or exaggeration
- **Defensiveness scale (K)**: subtle measure of defensiveness and denial

The MMPI is primarily a clinical tool — used in diagnosis, forensic evaluations, and employment screening in sensitive positions.

### NEO Personality Inventory (NEO-PI-3)

Measures the Big Five personality dimensions and their six facets each (30 facets total). Based on solid factor-analytic research; widely used in research and organizational settings.

### Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)

Assigns people to 16 types based on four dichotomies (E/I, S/N, T/F, J/P). Extremely popular in corporate settings and popular culture.

**Scientific assessment**: the MBTI has poor test-retest reliability (many people get different types when retested weeks later), its bipolar categories don't reflect the underlying continuous distributions found in research, and it doesn't predict important outcomes as well as the Big Five. Psychologists generally consider it psychometrically inferior to Big Five measures despite its popularity.

## 2. Projective Tests

Projective tests present ambiguous stimuli and ask people to respond freely, with the assumption that people will "project" unconscious thoughts, feelings, and conflicts onto the ambiguous material.

### Rorschach Inkblot Test

Ten symmetrical inkblots; the person describes what they see. Responses are coded for content (animal, human, object), location (whole blot or detail), and determinants (form, color, movement).

**Scientific status**: highly controversial. The Rorschach has faced sustained criticism for questionable reliability and validity. Some scoring systems (the Comprehensive System) have been criticized for making many normal people look pathological. The Exner Comprehensive System and more recent revisions attempt to improve reliability, but the test remains disputed among psychologists.

### Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

Ambiguous pictures of people in various situations; the person tells a story about each picture. Scores are derived from achievement, affiliation, and power themes in the stories.

**Scientific status**: better theoretical grounding than the Rorschach for measuring specific motivational needs (McClelland's achievement motivation research used TAT methods) but still lower reliability than self-report measures.

---

## 3. Behavioral Assessment

Rather than asking about behavior, **behavioral assessment** observes behavior directly.

- **Behavioral observation**: trained observers record frequency, duration, and context of specific behaviors in natural or structured settings. High reliability if observation criteria are clear; time-intensive.
- **Experience sampling (ecological momentary assessment)**: participants are prompted multiple times a day via phone to report their current thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. Avoids retrospective distortion; captures within-person variation.
- **Behavioral tests and situational tests**: standardized situations designed to elicit relevant behaviors. Military and government agencies sometimes use "assessment centers" where candidates work through realistic scenarios while evaluators observe.

---

## 4. Informant Reports

Rather than self-report, someone who knows the person well rates their personality.

**Advantages**: less subject to defensiveness and self-presentation concerns; may capture behaviors the person is unaware of or doesn't report accurately.

**Research finding**: informant reports often agree substantially with self-reports AND with each other — and in some cases predict important outcomes (job performance, relationship satisfaction) better than self-reports. The consensus across multiple informants is especially valuable.

---

## 5. What Makes a Good Assessment?

**Reliability**: consistency. Types:
- **Test-retest reliability**: same results over time (important for stable traits)
- **Internal consistency**: items that supposedly measure the same construct should correlate with each other
- **Inter-rater reliability**: different raters should agree (crucial for projective tests and observational measures)

**Validity**: does the measure actually measure what it claims?
- **Content validity**: does it comprehensively cover the domain?
- **Criterion validity**: does it predict relevant outcomes? Does a "conscientiousness" scale predict academic performance and job success?
- **Construct validity**: does the pattern of correlations with other measures make theoretical sense?

**Standardization**: norms from representative samples allow interpretation of individual scores relative to the population.

---

## 6. Assessment Ethics and Bias

Personality assessments can be misused — particularly in high-stakes contexts (hiring, custody decisions, criminal justice).

- **Diagnostic labeling**: psychological labels have consequences; they can influence how others treat a person and how the person sees themselves
- **Cultural bias**: many assessments were developed on Western samples; norms may not apply across cultures
- **Employment screening**: use of personality tests in hiring is legal in most contexts but must be job-related and non-discriminatory; lawsuits have challenged assessments that produce adverse impact on protected groups without demonstrable validity
- **Privacy**: psychologists have obligations to protect assessment data; results should be shared only with appropriate consent

---

## Summary

Personality assessment approaches include self-report inventories (MMPI, NEO-PI, MBTI), projective tests (Rorschach, TAT), behavioral observation, and informant ratings. Self-report measures are most reliable and valid for most purposes; projective tests are scientifically controversial. The MBTI is widely used but lacks strong psychometric support; the Big Five measures are better validated. Good assessment requires reliability (consistency), validity (measuring what's claimed), and appropriate norms. Assessments carry ethical responsibilities regarding labeling, cultural bias, and privacy.
