Mood disorders are among the most prevalent and disabling conditions in the world. They involve profound disruptions of emotional state — not simply sadness or happiness, but dysregulation of affect that pervades cognition, motivation, behavior, and the body. Understanding mood disorders requires grasping both their biological underpinnings and the cognitive patterns that maintain them.

---

## 1. Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)

**Core features**: depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure (anhedonia) most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks, accompanied by at least 5 total symptoms.

**DSM-5 diagnostic criteria** (at least 5, including depressed mood or anhedonia):
- Depressed mood (sadness, emptiness, hopelessness)
- Markedly diminished interest or pleasure in activities
- Significant weight/appetite change
- Sleep disturbance (insomnia or hypersomnia)
- Psychomotor agitation or retardation
- Fatigue or loss of energy
- Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt
- Difficulty thinking, concentrating, or making decisions
- Recurrent thoughts of death or suicidal ideation

**Prevalence**: ~7% in a given year; ~20% lifetime. Most common mental disorder. More prevalent in women (2:1) — attributed to biological (hormonal), psychological (ruminative coping style), and social (greater rates of trauma, social role stress) factors.

**Biological factors**:
- **Monoamine hypothesis**: depression involves deficiency in serotonin, norepinephrine, and/or dopamine. This is an oversimplification — antidepressants that increase monoamines take weeks to work despite immediately raising neurotransmitter levels, suggesting more complex downstream mechanisms.
- **HPA axis dysregulation**: elevated cortisol in depression; stress-induced HPA activation can damage the hippocampus
- **Neuroplasticity**: depression involves reduced neurogenesis in the hippocampus; antidepressants promote neurogenesis — this may be part of their mechanism
- **Genetics**: heritability ~40%; no single gene; polygenic risk

**Cognitive model** (Beck): depression is maintained by the **cognitive triad** — negative views of the self, the world, and the future — and by **cognitive distortions** (all-or-nothing thinking, catastrophizing, overgeneralization, personalization).

**Subtypes**: psychotic depression (with hallucinations or delusions), seasonal affective disorder (SAD) (fall/winter pattern), postpartum depression, treatment-resistant depression.

---

## 2. Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia)

**Core features**: depressed mood more days than not for at least 2 years, with at least 2 additional symptoms. Less intense than MDD but more chronic. Often described as a "gray cloud" state.

**Comorbidity**: ~75% of people with dysthymia develop MDD at some point (**double depression** — dysthymia with superimposed MDD episode). Responds more slowly to treatment than MDD alone.

## 3. Bipolar Disorders

Bipolar disorders are characterized by episodes of **mania** or **hypomania** (elevated mood states) in addition to depressive episodes.

### Manic Episode

- Elevated or irritable mood, lasting at least 7 days or requiring hospitalization
- Plus at least 3 of: grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity, and risky behavior
- Causes marked impairment; may involve psychotic features
- During mania, people often feel wonderful and don't recognize they're ill — creating treatment challenges

### Hypomanic Episode

- Same features as mania but less severe; lasting at least 4 days; **does not** cause severe impairment or require hospitalization
- Hypomania may feel like peak functioning — creativity, energy, reduced sleep need

### Bipolar I Disorder

At least one manic episode (with or without depressive episodes). The manic episodes are typically severe — often requiring hospitalization and involving significant social/occupational impairment.

### Bipolar II Disorder

At least one hypomanic episode + at least one major depressive episode; no manic episodes. Often more time in depression than in elevated states. Frequently misdiagnosed as unipolar depression if the hypomanic episodes aren't recognized.

### Cyclothymia

Subsyndromal hypomanic and depressive symptoms alternating for at least 2 years — the "bipolar spectrum" disorder. Does not meet full criteria for bipolar I or II.

**Prevalence**: Bipolar I ~1%; Bipolar II ~1%; spectrum conditions higher (~3–5%).

**Biological factors**: high heritability (~80–90%); circadian rhythm dysregulation; dopamine and glutamate dysregulation; lithium (the mainstay treatment) has multiple neurobiological effects including neuroprotection.

**Treatment**: mood stabilizers (lithium, valproate, lamotrigine) are the foundation; atypical antipsychotics; psychotherapy (especially psychoeducation and sleep/routine regulation). Antidepressants are used cautiously (can trigger mania or rapid cycling in some patients).

## 4. Suicide

Mood disorders carry substantial risk of suicide. Understanding the epidemiology and risk factors is essential.

**Epidemiology**: ~45,000 suicides annually in the U.S. (~125/day); the 12th leading cause of death overall; 2nd among those 10–34. Suicide attempts occur about 25× more often than deaths. Women attempt more often; men die more often (use more lethal methods).

**Risk factors**:
- Previous suicide attempt (the strongest single predictor)
- Psychiatric diagnosis — especially major depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, alcohol use disorder
- Acute stressors (recent loss, humiliation, failure)
- Access to lethal means (especially firearms)
- Social isolation
- Hopelessness (a better predictor than depression severity per se)
- Impulsivity

**Protective factors**:
- Social support and belonging
- Religious or moral objections to suicide
- Responsibility for children
- Effective mental health treatment
- Means restriction (reducing access to firearms significantly reduces suicide rates)

**Warning signs**: expressing hopelessness or intent; giving away possessions; sudden calm after depression (may indicate a decision has been made); increased substance use; withdrawal.

**Responding to suicidal ideation**: take it seriously; ask directly ("Are you thinking about suicide?"); asking does not increase risk. Connect with professional help. Remove means if possible.

## 5. Treatments for Mood Disorders

### Psychotherapy

**Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)**: identifies and modifies negative automatic thoughts and behavioral patterns (behavioral activation — increasing engagement in rewarding activities). First-line treatment for moderate depression; comparable to antidepressants for mild to moderate depression; more durable (lower relapse rates) than medication alone.

**Behavioral Activation**: focuses on increasing engagement with rewarding activities to disrupt the withdrawal-depression cycle; effective even as a standalone treatment.

**Interpersonal Therapy (IPT)**: focuses on improving relationships and communication; particularly effective for depression triggered by interpersonal difficulties.

**DBT** (Dialectical Behavior Therapy): developed for borderline personality disorder; effective for emotional dysregulation; increasingly used for bipolar disorder and treatment-resistant depression.

### Pharmacological Treatment

**Antidepressants**:
- **SSRIs** (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors): fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, etc. First-line; effective for ~60% of patients; side effects include sexual dysfunction, GI upset, initial anxiety increase
- **SNRIs** (serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors): venlafaxine, duloxetine. Similar efficacy; may help comorbid pain
- **Bupropion**: different mechanism (dopamine/norepinephrine); fewer sexual side effects; contraindicated with eating disorders and seizure risk
- **TCAs, MAOIs**: older classes; effective but more side effects

For depression, antidepressants are most effective for moderate-to-severe depression; their efficacy for mild depression is less clear above placebo.

### Neuromodulation

**ECT** (electroconvulsive therapy): brief electrical stimulation of the brain under general anesthesia; the most effective antidepressant treatment available, with response rates of 60–80% for treatment-resistant depression. Modern ECT is safe; primary side effect is memory impairment (usually temporary). Highly underutilized due to stigma from historical overuse and misuse.

**TMS** (transcranial magnetic stimulation): FDA-approved for treatment-resistant depression; delivers magnetic pulses to the prefrontal cortex; fewer side effects than ECT; somewhat less effective.

**Ketamine and esketamine**: rapid-acting antidepressants (hours, not weeks); effective for treatment-resistant depression; esketamine (Spravato) is FDA-approved for nasal administration.

---

## Summary

Major depressive disorder involves persistent depressed mood or anhedonia with accompanying symptoms for at least two weeks. Bipolar disorders involve manic or hypomanic episodes, classified as Bipolar I (full mania), Bipolar II (hypomania + depression), or cyclothymia. The monoamine hypothesis is an oversimplification; current models emphasize HPA dysregulation, neuroplasticity, and circadian rhythm disruption. Beck's cognitive triad maintains depression through negative views of self, world, and future. Treatments include CBT (most durable), antidepressants (most prescribed), and ECT (most effective for severe/resistant cases). Suicidality requires immediate attention; hopelessness and previous attempts are the strongest predictors.
