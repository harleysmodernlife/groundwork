Diagnosis in psychology is not like diagnosing a broken bone — there's no blood test or X-ray that confirms depression or schizophrenia. Psychiatric diagnosis is a clinical judgment based on observed symptoms, reported experiences, and explicit criteria. Understanding how diagnosis works — its logic, its tools, and its limitations — is essential for anyone who wants to evaluate mental health claims critically.

---

## 1. The Purpose of Diagnosis

Diagnosis serves several functions:

- **Communication**: a shared vocabulary that allows clinicians, researchers, and patients to understand each other efficiently ("she has panic disorder" conveys a specific constellation of symptoms)
- **Treatment guidance**: different disorders respond to different treatments; accurate diagnosis points toward effective intervention
- **Research**: comparing groups of people with the same diagnosis allows research on causes, prognosis, and treatment
- **Insurance and reimbursement**: in the U.S. healthcare system, most insurance requires a DSM diagnosis for treatment coverage
- **Reducing self-blame**: for some people, a diagnosis explains their suffering in terms of a recognized condition rather than a personal failing

**Risks and downsides**:
- **Labeling effects**: diagnoses can become self-fulfilling (you're now treated as "a depressive") and carry stigma
- **Heterogeneity within categories**: two people with the same diagnosis may have few symptoms in common, since many DSM criteria require meeting only a subset of listed symptoms
- **False positives**: normal distress can be pathologized
- **Overlooking context**: diagnosing individual pathology without addressing social and environmental causes

---

## 2. How Diagnosis Works

### Clinical Interview

The primary diagnostic tool is the **clinical interview** — a structured or semi-structured conversation in which the clinician gathers information about:

- **Presenting complaint**: what brings the person in; their own account of their difficulty
- **History of present illness**: onset, course, severity, triggers
- **Past psychiatric history**: previous episodes, diagnoses, treatments
- **Medical history**: physical conditions that can cause or mimic psychiatric symptoms (hypothyroidism mimics depression; hyperthyroidism mimics anxiety)
- **Medications**: many medications cause psychiatric symptoms
- **Family history**: psychiatric disorders in biological relatives (informs genetic risk)
- **Social history**: relationships, occupation, stressors, substance use
- **Mental status examination (MSE)**: direct observation of appearance, behavior, speech, mood, affect, thought process, thought content, perceptions, cognition, insight, and judgment

### Structured Diagnostic Interviews

For research and clinical purposes, validated structured interviews provide standardized assessments:

- **SCID** (Structured Clinical Interview for DSM): the gold standard research interview; covers major DSM categories systematically
- **MINI** (Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview): shorter; used in research and clinical settings
- **PHQ-9** (Patient Health Questionnaire): 9-item self-report measure of depression severity; widely used in primary care

### Psychological Testing

Standardized tests supplement clinical interviews:

- **Personality measures**: MMPI-3, NEO-PI, etc.
- **Cognitive testing**: neuropsychological batteries assess memory, attention, language, and executive function
- **Severity scales**: Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, Beck Depression Inventory, Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale
- **IQ testing**: standardized intellectual assessment when intellectual disability is a question

## 3. Reliability and Validity of Diagnosis

**Reliability**: do different clinicians arrive at the same diagnosis for the same patient?

Early DSMs had poor inter-rater reliability. DSM-III's shift to explicit diagnostic criteria dramatically improved reliability by giving clinicians a checklist to follow. Field trials for DSM-5 found variable reliability across categories — mood disorders had moderate reliability; personality disorders were among the least reliable.

**Validity**: does the diagnosis identify a real, distinct underlying condition with a characteristic cause, prognosis, and treatment?

This is harder to establish. Diagnostic validity is evaluated by:
- **Descriptive validity**: do symptoms cluster together as claimed?
- **Predictive validity**: does the diagnosis predict prognosis or treatment response?
- **Biological validity**: does the diagnosis correspond to distinct biological findings?

Most psychiatric diagnoses have established descriptive validity but limited biological validity — there are no biological tests that definitively confirm a psychiatric diagnosis. This contrasts with many medical conditions, where biomarkers exist.

## 4. The Dimensional vs. Categorical Debate

The DSM treats disorders as categories — either you meet criteria or you don't. But research increasingly suggests that many psychiatric conditions lie on **dimensions** — continuous with normal variation rather than sharply distinct from it.

**Evidence**: anxiety, depression, and psychotic symptoms all exist in the general population at subclinical levels. Many people fluctuate across diagnostic thresholds over time. The boundaries between disorders are fuzzy; comorbidity is the rule rather than the exception.

**Alternative approaches**:
- **Research Domain Criteria (RDoC)** (NIMH): an alternative framework organized around behavioral and neurobiological dimensions (threat response, reward learning, working memory) rather than symptom categories
- **Spectrum models**: disorders are conceptualized as points on continuous spectra (autism spectrum disorder adopted this in DSM-5; a psychosis spectrum approach is being developed)
- **Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP)**: a dimensional model organizing disorders into a hierarchy based on empirical structure rather than clinical tradition

## 5. Common Diagnostic Errors

**Confirmation bias**: once a diagnostic hypothesis is formed, clinicians tend to seek confirming evidence and discount disconfirming evidence.

**Anchoring**: the first diagnosis given to a patient tends to stick, even when new information suggests a revision.

**Diagnostic overshadowing**: a pre-existing diagnosis leads clinicians to attribute new symptoms to the known condition rather than considering a new diagnosis.

**Cultural bias**: clinicians may pathologize culturally normative behavior (e.g., hearing the voice of a deceased relative, common in some cultures, is not psychosis).

**Overdiagnosis**: growing evidence that some conditions are overdiagnosed — particularly ADHD in children, bipolar disorder in adults, and PTSD.

---

## 6. Diagnosis and Stigma

Psychiatric diagnosis carries social stigma — negative stereotypes and discrimination that affect how others treat a person and how they see themselves. Stigma is a major barrier to help-seeking: many people delay treatment for years out of fear of the label.

**Structural stigma**: policies and systems that disadvantage people with psychiatric diagnoses — employment discrimination, custody decisions, insurance limitations.

**Self-stigma**: internalizing negative stereotypes, leading to shame, reduced self-efficacy, and withdrawal from treatment.

**Anti-stigma efforts**: "mental illness is a brain disease" campaigns have had mixed results — reducing blame but increasing social distance (people see the person as more unpredictable). Contact-based approaches (exposure to people with mental health challenges) are more effective.

---

## Summary

Psychiatric diagnosis is based on clinical interviews, structured assessments, and symptom criteria from the DSM or ICD. It serves communication, treatment guidance, and research functions but carries risks of labeling, stigma, and oversimplification. Reliability has improved with explicit criteria; validity remains limited by the absence of biological markers for most conditions. Evidence increasingly supports dimensional over categorical models of psychopathology. Alternative frameworks like RDoC and HiTOP aim to organize mental health science around empirically derived dimensions rather than clinical tradition. Stigma — social, structural, and self-imposed — remains a major barrier to treatment-seeking.
