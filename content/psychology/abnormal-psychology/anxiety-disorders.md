Anxiety is a universal human experience — a signal that something may be wrong, a preparation to respond to threat. But when anxiety is chronic, excessive, or triggered by stimuli that pose no genuine threat, it becomes a disorder that interferes with daily life. Anxiety disorders are the most common mental health conditions worldwide.

---

## 1. Normal Anxiety vs. Anxiety Disorders

**Normal anxiety** is an appropriate, adaptive response to genuine threats — public speaking, a job interview, a dangerous situation. It motivates preparation and increases alertness.

**Anxiety disorders** involve:
- Anxiety that is excessive relative to the actual threat
- Anxiety that is persistent and difficult to control
- Anxiety that causes significant distress or impairs functioning
- Often, avoidance of situations associated with anxiety — which maintains the anxiety by preventing the learning that the feared situation is actually safe

The **fear response** involves the amygdala (rapid threat detection) and the hypothalamus (triggering the HPA axis and fight-or-flight response). In anxiety disorders, this system is dysregulated — triggered too easily, too intensely, or not turned off appropriately.

---

## 2. Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

**Core feature**: persistent, excessive, uncontrollable worry about multiple areas of life (health, finances, relationships, work) — not tied to a specific situation.

**Diagnostic criteria**: worry occurring more days than not for at least 6 months, accompanied by at least 3 of: restlessness, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance.

**Prevalence**: ~5% lifetime. More common in women (2:1).

**Biological mechanisms**: evidence for elevated baseline arousal, reduced GABA inhibitory function, and hyperactivity in the anterior cingulate cortex (worry circuits).

**Treatment**: CBT (identifying and challenging catastrophic thinking; worry reduction strategies; relaxation training) and SSRIs/SNRIs are both effective; combination is often best.

---

## 3. Panic Disorder

**Core feature**: recurrent unexpected **panic attacks** — sudden surges of intense fear or discomfort peaking within minutes, with symptoms like racing heart, shortness of breath, chest pain, dizziness, derealization, numbness, and fear of dying or "going crazy." Plus persistent worry about future attacks and/or behavioral changes to avoid them.

**Panic attacks** can occur in multiple contexts but in panic disorder the initial attacks are "out of the blue" — not triggered by an obvious threat. Subsequent attacks may be triggered by bodily sensations that the person has learned to associate with panic.

**Cognitive model** (Clark): panic disorder is maintained by **catastrophic misinterpretation** of benign physical sensations. A slightly elevated heart rate is interpreted as a heart attack → fear → more adrenaline → faster heart rate → more fear. This vicious cycle produces a panic attack.

**Agoraphobia**: often develops alongside panic disorder — avoidance of situations where escape might be difficult or help unavailable if a panic attack occurs (public transportation, shopping malls, being alone). In severe cases, people become housebound.

**Treatment**: **interoceptive exposure** (deliberately inducing feared physical sensations to break the association with danger) + cognitive restructuring. SSRIs and SNRIs are also effective.

---

## 4. Specific Phobias

**Core feature**: marked fear and avoidance of a specific object or situation, with recognition that the fear is disproportionate.

**Common types**:
- Animal (dogs, spiders, snakes, rodents)
- Natural environment (heights, water, storms)
- Blood-injection-injury (unique: produces vasovagal syncope — fainting — rather than typical fight-or-flight response; the only type treated with applied muscle tension rather than relaxation)
- Situational (flying, enclosed spaces, driving)
- Other (vomiting, choking, costumed characters)

**Prevalence**: ~10–12% lifetime; typically develop in childhood.

**Treatment**: **systematic desensitization** or **exposure therapy** — the most effective treatment for any psychological disorder, with 80–90% of phobias significantly improved after a few sessions. A single 3-hour intensive exposure session can eliminate most simple phobias.

---

## 5. Social Anxiety Disorder (Social Phobia)

**Core feature**: intense fear of social situations involving possible scrutiny by others — fear of embarrassment, humiliation, or negative evaluation. Situations include public speaking, eating in public, meeting new people, or almost any interaction.

**Key cognitive distortion**: the person believes they will behave in a way that is embarrassing or humiliating, and that this will have catastrophic social consequences. They also tend to believe they come across worse than they actually do ("the post-event processing trap": replaying social interactions and imagining how terrible they appeared).

**Prevalence**: ~13% lifetime; equally common in men and women.

**Distinction from shyness**: social anxiety disorder is characterized by significant impairment and distress, not just preference for solitude.

**Treatment**: CBT with exposure to feared social situations (not just talking about it) is most effective. SSRIs also effective. Beta-blockers can reduce physiological symptoms for performance-specific situations.

---

## 6. Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Classified separately from anxiety disorders in DSM-5 but closely related:

**Core feature**: obsessions (recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges that cause distress) and/or compulsions (repetitive behaviors or mental acts performed to reduce obsession-related distress — temporarily effective but maintaining the disorder long-term).

**Common obsessions**: contamination, harm (to oneself or others), symmetry, taboo thoughts (blasphemous, sexual, violent — these are ego-dystonic and cause great distress; having the thought doesn't indicate desire to act on it)

**Common compulsions**: washing, checking, ordering, repeating, mental reviewing/reassurance

**The maintenance cycle**: obsession → anxiety → compulsion → temporary relief → more obsessions. Compulsions prevent natural anxiety reduction through exposure and reinforce the belief that the obsession is dangerous.

**Treatment**: **Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP)** — the most effective behavioral treatment. Deliberately triggering obsessions while preventing the compulsive response. SSRIs (at higher doses than for depression) are effective; combination is best for severe cases.

---

## 7. Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

Also classified separately in DSM-5 (trauma and stressor-related disorders):

**Core feature**: following exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence (directly or witnessing), development of intrusion symptoms, avoidance, negative alterations in cognitions and mood, and hyperarousal.

**Intrusion symptoms**: flashbacks (reliving the trauma as if it's happening), nightmares, distressing memories triggered by reminders

**Avoidance**: avoiding trauma-related thoughts, feelings, reminders, places, people

**Negative cognitions and mood**: persistent negative beliefs ("I am permanently damaged," "The world is completely dangerous"), distorted blame, persistent negative emotion, diminished interest

**Hyperarousal**: hypervigilance, exaggerated startle response, sleep disturbance, irritability

**Risk factors**: severity and proximity of trauma, prior trauma history, lack of social support, female sex, pre-existing anxiety, childhood adversity.

**Prevalence**: ~8% lifetime; much higher in combat veterans, sexual assault survivors, first responders.

**Treatment**: **Prolonged Exposure** (PE) and **Cognitive Processing Therapy** (CPT) are gold-standard trauma-focused treatments. EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing) also evidence-based. SSRIs (paroxetine, sertraline) are FDA-approved.

---

## Summary

Anxiety disorders share excessive, persistent fear and avoidance that impairs functioning. GAD involves chronic diffuse worry; panic disorder involves unexpected panic attacks with catastrophic misinterpretation of body sensations; specific phobias involve disproportionate fear of specific stimuli; social anxiety disorder involves fear of negative social evaluation. OCD is characterized by obsessions and compulsions that form a self-maintaining cycle. PTSD follows trauma and involves intrusions, avoidance, and hyperarousal. Cognitive-behavioral therapy — especially exposure-based approaches — is the most effective treatment across all anxiety-spectrum disorders.
