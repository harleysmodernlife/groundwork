Every culture draws a line between normal and abnormal behavior — but where that line falls, and who draws it, are far from simple questions. What counts as a psychological disorder, how we distinguish eccentricity from pathology, and who has the authority to make that judgment are foundational issues in abnormal psychology.

---

## 1. Defining Abnormality

No single criterion adequately defines abnormal behavior. Psychologists use multiple criteria together:

### Statistical Infrequency

Behavior that is rare in the population. IQ below 70, for example, is statistically unusual (about 2% of the population) — and is used as a criterion for intellectual disability.

**Limitation**: many statistically unusual things are not disordered (exceptional athletic ability, extreme generosity) and many common things are (depression affects ~15–20% of people in a given year — hardly "rare").

### Violation of Social Norms

Behavior that deviates from what is expected and accepted in a particular culture.

**Limitation**: norms vary dramatically across cultures and change over time. Homosexuality was classified as a mental disorder by the American Psychiatric Association until 1973. Drapetomania — the "disorder" of slaves wanting to escape — was proposed by a 19th-century physician. Social norm violations reveal how psychiatric diagnosis can encode and enforce cultural prejudices.

### Personal Distress

The person's behavior causes them significant suffering — anxiety, depression, shame, guilt.

**Limitation**: some disordered people don't experience distress (people in manic states often feel wonderful; people with antisocial personality disorder may feel no guilt). Conversely, some distress is a normal response to life circumstances (grief after loss is not a disorder).

### Functional Impairment

The behavior impairs the person's ability to function — in work, relationships, self-care, or other important areas.

**Limitation**: what counts as adequate functioning varies by context. Some people with serious disorders function very well; some people without disorders function poorly.

### The 4Ds Framework

Many textbooks organize these criteria as the "4Ds":
- **Deviance**: different from cultural norms
- **Distress**: causes suffering
- **Dysfunction**: impairs functioning
- **Danger**: risk of harm to self or others

Danger is a relatively rare criterion — most people with psychological disorders are not dangerous to others; the association of mental illness with violence is largely a media-driven misconception.

## 2. The Medical Model vs. Biopsychosocial Model

### The Medical Model

Psychological disorders are conceptualized as diseases with biological causes, specific symptoms, and treatments aimed at the underlying pathology. This model has driven the development of psychiatric medications and the DSM's categorical diagnostic approach.

**Strengths**: destigmatization (illness, not weakness), guides biological research, aligns with effective drug treatments.

**Criticisms**: oversimplifies multifactorial conditions; ignores social and psychological contributors; "medicalizes" normal human suffering.

### The Biopsychosocial Model

Disorders result from the interaction of biological (genetics, brain chemistry), psychological (thoughts, emotions, behavior), and social (relationships, culture, socioeconomic conditions) factors. This is the dominant model in contemporary clinical psychology.

## 3. The DSM and Classification

The **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)** — currently in its fifth edition, revised (DSM-5-TR) — is the primary classification system used in the United States. It lists ~300 disorders with specific diagnostic criteria (symptom checklists with duration and impairment requirements).

**History:**
- **DSM-I** (1952): heavily psychoanalytic; ~100 disorders
- **DSM-II** (1968): similar approach; included homosexuality
- **DSM-III** (1980): revolutionary shift to atheoretical, symptom-based diagnosis; 300+ disorders; introduced specific diagnostic criteria; homosexuality removed
- **DSM-5** (2013): continued refinement; added dimensions alongside categories; significant controversy (many worried about over-diagnosis)

**ICD-11**: the World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases — used globally; its mental health chapter is the alternative international system.

**Criticism of the DSM**:
- **Categorical vs. dimensional**: mental health problems may be continuous with normal functioning rather than distinct categories — but the DSM assigns binary diagnoses (you have it or you don't)
- **Comorbidity**: most people who receive one diagnosis also meet criteria for others — suggesting the categories don't capture distinct underlying conditions
- **Cultural validity**: criteria developed primarily in Western contexts may not apply universally
- **Medicalization of normal distress**: expanded criteria may pathologize ordinary human experiences (bereavement, shyness, difficult behavior in children)

## 4. Rosenhan's Study: "On Being Sane in Insane Places" (1973)

Psychologist David Rosenhan sent 8 mentally healthy pseudo-patients to psychiatric hospitals, claiming to hear voices saying "thud," "empty," and "hollow." All were admitted; most were diagnosed with schizophrenia. Once admitted, they behaved normally — yet hospital stays averaged 19 days, and note-taking was interpreted as paranoid behavior.

In a follow-up, Rosenhan told a hospital to expect pseudo-patients over a period — and the hospital flagged 41 of 193 subsequent patients as likely pseudo-patients. Rosenhan sent none.

**Implications**: diagnosis is influenced by context and labeling; once a label is applied, behavior is interpreted through that lens. The study raised profound questions about the reliability of psychiatric diagnosis — though its methodology has since been questioned.

## 5. Culture and Abnormality

**Culture-bound syndromes** are patterns of distressing behavior specific to particular cultural contexts:

- **Taijin kyofusho** (Japan): intense fear of offending others through one's appearance or smell (in contrast to Western social phobia, which centers on embarrassing oneself)
- **Susto** (Latin America): "fright sickness" — a sense of the soul leaving the body after a frightening event
- **Running amok** (Malaysia/Indonesia): a dissociative episode of random violence following social humiliation
- **Dhat syndrome** (South Asia): extreme anxiety about loss of semen, associated with sexual dysfunction

These syndromes reveal that what counts as disturbing, the meaning given to symptoms, and the categories available for understanding distress are all culturally shaped.

---

## Summary

Defining abnormality requires multiple criteria — statistical infrequency, norm violation, personal distress, and functional impairment — each capturing part of the picture but none sufficient alone. The 4Ds (deviance, distress, dysfunction, danger) provide a practical framework. The DSM provides the dominant categorical classification system in the U.S., though it faces criticisms about comorbidity, medicalization, and cultural validity. The biopsychosocial model is preferred over the pure medical model. Cultural context profoundly shapes what counts as abnormal and what symptoms are expressed.
