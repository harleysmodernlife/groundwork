Language is one of the most distinctly human abilities — a system for encoding and communicating an unlimited range of ideas using a finite set of words and rules. But language is more than a communication tool: it shapes how we think, what we can easily conceptualize, and how we understand our experience. The relationship between language and thought is one of psychology's most fascinating questions.

---

## 1. Properties of Language

Language has several defining properties that distinguish it from animal communication:

- **Arbitrary symbols**: words are arbitrary pairings of sounds/letters with meanings ("dog" and "perro" and "hund" all mean the same thing)
- **Structure (grammar)**: words combine according to rules that allow infinite new sentences to be produced
- **Generativity (productivity)**: a finite set of words and rules can produce an infinite number of sentences — you can say sentences never said before
- **Displacement**: language can refer to things not present in time or space (past, future, hypothetical events)
- **Cultural transmission**: language is learned from others, not genetically encoded in its specifics

---

## 2. Language Structure

Linguistics analyzes language at multiple levels:

- **Phonemes**: the smallest units of sound (English has ~44). "Cat" has three phonemes: /k/, /æ/, /t/
- **Morphemes**: the smallest units of meaning. "Cats" has two: "cat" + "s" (plural marker). Prefixes and suffixes are morphemes.
- **Syntax**: rules for combining words into grammatically correct sentences. "The dog bit the man" and "The man bit the dog" use the same words but mean different things.
- **Semantics**: the study of meaning — what words and sentences mean
- **Pragmatics**: how context shapes meaning. "Can you open the window?" is a question about ability but is interpreted as a request based on pragmatic understanding.

## 3. Language Acquisition

Children around the world follow a remarkably consistent sequence of language development:

- **0–6 months**: cooing, responding to voices, distinguishing language from non-language sounds
- **6–8 months**: **babbling** — producing consonant-vowel syllables (ba-ba-ba, da-da-da). Babbling includes sounds from all languages; by ~10 months, it narrows to sounds of the native language.
- **10–13 months**: **one-word stage** — single words ("mama," "more," "no"). These **holophrases** carry the meaning of whole sentences.
- **18–24 months**: **two-word stage** — "more milk," "daddy go," "no nap"
- **2–3 years**: **telegraphic speech** — short sentences with content words, omitting function words ("Where kitty go?")
- **3–5 years**: rapid vocabulary growth (~10 new words per day) and increasingly complex grammar

**Critical period hypothesis**: there is a sensitive period for language acquisition (roughly until puberty) during which language is acquired with native-like ease. After puberty, learning a second language becomes progressively more difficult, and never-learned first languages produce permanent deficits. Evidence: Genie, a girl severely isolated until age 13, never acquired normal grammar despite years of instruction.

---

## 4. Theories of Language Acquisition

**B.F. Skinner** (behaviorist): language is learned through reinforcement of imitation — parents reinforce correct utterances and ignore or correct incorrect ones.

**Noam Chomsky** (nativist): children acquire language too rapidly and with too little direct instruction for learning to explain it. They make systematic, not random, errors ("goed" for "went," "foots" for "feet") consistent with applying grammar rules, not imitating. Chomsky proposed a **Language Acquisition Device (LAD)** — an innate, species-specific mechanism for acquiring language, embodying universal grammar rules.

**Modern view** (interactionist): language acquisition involves both innate predispositions (babies are uniquely attuned to human speech, have phoneme discrimination abilities from birth) and environmental input (child-directed speech/"motherese" facilitates learning; neglect or hearing impairment severely impairs acquisition).

## 5. Language and Thought: The Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis

Does language shape thought? The **Sapir-Whorf hypothesis** (linguistic relativity) has two versions:

**Strong version (linguistic determinism)**: language determines thought — you cannot think about concepts your language doesn't have. This strong version is now rejected: people who lack a word for something can still perceive and think about it.

**Weak version (linguistic relativity)**: language influences thought — differences in language lead to differences in thinking, even if language doesn't fully constrain thought. This moderate version has empirical support.

**Color perception**: Russian has separate basic words for light blue (goluboy) and dark blue (siniy) where English has just "blue." Russian speakers are faster at distinguishing shades across this boundary than shades on the same side of it — and faster than English speakers at the same task.

**Spatial reasoning**: some languages (like Guugu Yimithirr, an Australian Aboriginal language) use absolute spatial terms (north, south, east, west) rather than relative terms (left, right, in front, behind). Speakers of these languages maintain an exceptional sense of absolute direction at all times — a cognitive ability that seems tied to the linguistic habit.

**Number**: the Pirahã language reportedly has no words for exact numbers (only concepts of "few" and "many"). Speakers show distinctive limitations in tasks requiring exact numerical matching.

**Conclusion**: language doesn't determine what we can think, but it influences what we think about, how efficiently we process certain distinctions, and what concepts we habitually attend to.

## 6. Language and the Brain

**Broca's area** (left inferior frontal gyrus): language *production*. Damage → **Broca's aphasia**: slow, effortful speech; grammar is impaired; comprehension is relatively intact. Patients know what they want to say but can't produce it fluently.

**Wernicke's area** (left posterior temporal lobe): language *comprehension*. Damage → **Wernicke's aphasia**: fluent, grammatically correct speech that lacks meaningful content ("word salad"); poor comprehension. Patients produce speech easily but it doesn't make sense.

**Arcuate fasciculus**: white matter tract connecting Broca's and Wernicke's areas. Damage → **conduction aphasia**: poor repetition despite intact production and comprehension.

**Split-brain patients**: when the corpus callosum is severed, the left hemisphere (verbal) and right hemisphere (nonverbal) process information independently. Information shown to the right visual field reaches the verbal left hemisphere; information shown to the left visual field reaches the mostly nonverbal right hemisphere. These patients cannot verbally name an object shown only to their left visual field — but they can pick it out with their left hand.

---

## Summary

Language is characterized by arbitrary symbols, grammatical structure, generativity, displacement, and cultural transmission. It is structured at levels of phonemes, morphemes, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics. Children acquire language in a universal sequence; Chomsky proposed an innate LAD; modern accounts emphasize both innate predispositions and environmental input. The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis in its weak form has support: language influences thought, perception, and cognitive habits without fully determining them. Language is lateralized to the left hemisphere, with Broca's area for production and Wernicke's area for comprehension.
