Thinking is not random. It follows patterns — some of which lead to correct conclusions, and some of which systematically lead us astray. The study of thinking and problem-solving examines how people reason, make decisions, and solve problems — and why those processes sometimes fail in predictable, repeatable ways.

---

## 1. Concepts and Categories

Thinking is built on **concepts** — mental representations of categories. Concepts allow us to generalize: once you know what a "dog" is, you can recognize new dogs without learning each one individually.

**Classical view**: concepts are defined by necessary and sufficient features (a chair must have a seat, legs, and back). Works for logical categories but fails for natural ones — most people can't specify what all birds have in common that nothing else does.

**Prototype view** (Rosch, 1970s): categories are organized around **prototypes** — the most typical or average member. We judge new instances by their similarity to the prototype. A robin is a better example of a bird than a penguin; a couch is a better chair than a stool. This explains why category membership is graded, not all-or-nothing.

## 2. Problem-Solving Strategies

### Algorithms

An **algorithm** is a guaranteed, step-by-step procedure for solving a problem. Given a password of N characters, checking every combination is an algorithm — it will find the solution. Algorithms work but can be slow or impractical.

### Heuristics

A **heuristic** is a mental shortcut or rule of thumb that usually leads to a good solution quickly but doesn't guarantee one. We use heuristics constantly because they're efficient. But they produce systematic errors.

**Means-ends analysis**: identify the gap between current state and goal; take action to reduce the gap.

**Working backward**: start from the goal and reason backward to the starting point.

**Analogy**: apply a solution from a similar problem you've already solved.

### Insight

Sometimes solutions arrive suddenly — the "Aha!" moment. **Insight** occurs when a problem that seemed stuck suddenly resolves, often after a period of incubation (not consciously working on it). Evidence suggests incubation allows spreading activation to find associations not accessible during focused effort.

---

## 3. Obstacles to Problem-Solving

### Mental Set

A **mental set** is a tendency to approach problems the same way that worked before, even when a different approach would work better. It produces functional fixedness and resistance to novel solutions.

**Functional fixedness**: inability to see an object used in an unconventional way. In Duncker's candle problem, participants needed to mount a candle on a wall using a box of tacks — most couldn't see the box as a potential shelf, only as a container.

### Confirmation Bias

We seek information that confirms our existing beliefs and ignore disconfirming information. In Wason's four-card selection task, most people check the wrong cards — choosing to confirm the rule rather than testing it.

## 4. Judgment and Decision-Making: Heuristics and Biases

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman (Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2002) documented systematic biases in human judgment — showing that people don't reason like statisticians, even when statistics would be helpful.

### Availability Heuristic

Estimating frequency or probability by how easily examples come to mind. We overestimate risks that are vivid and memorable (plane crashes, shark attacks) and underestimate risks that are mundane and common (car accidents, heart disease).

**Example**: after extensive media coverage of a plane crash, people overestimate the risk of flying while underestimating the risk of the drive to the airport.

### Representativeness Heuristic

Judging probability based on how representative a case is of a category — how much it looks like our prototype.

**Conjunction fallacy** (Kahneman & Tversky, 1983): participants were given a description of Linda (feminist, philosophy major, concerned with social justice) and asked: is it more likely that Linda is (a) a bank teller, or (b) a bank teller who is active in the feminist movement? Most chose (b) — but (a) is mathematically more probable; option (b) is a *subset* of (a). The "feminist bank teller" description is more representative of Linda but cannot be more probable.

### Anchoring and Adjustment

Estimates are disproportionately influenced by an initial reference point — the **anchor** — even when the anchor is arbitrary.

**Example**: spin a wheel that lands on 65, then estimate the percentage of African countries in the UN. Average estimate: 45. Wheel lands on 10: average estimate drops to 25. Identical question; wildly different answers depending on the random anchor.

### Framing Effect

The way a choice is **framed** (presented) changes decisions, even when the options are logically equivalent.

- "90% survival rate" vs. "10% mortality rate" — same information, but people rate the first option more favorably
- "Gain frame" vs. "loss frame": people are more willing to accept risk to avoid a loss than to secure an equivalent gain (loss aversion)

## 5. Dual-Process Theory (System 1 and System 2)

Kahneman's framework (popularized in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*) proposes two modes of thinking:

**System 1 (fast, intuitive)**: automatic, rapid, effortless; pattern-matching; heuristic-driven; emotional; usually runs in the background.

**System 2 (slow, analytical)**: deliberate, effortful, rule-governed, logical; engaged for difficult problems; requires working memory.

Most errors in judgment occur when System 1 gives a confident but wrong answer and System 2 fails to override it. Cognitive biases are largely System 1 outputs that System 2 didn't catch.

---

## 6. Creativity

**Creativity** requires both **convergent thinking** (finding the single correct answer) and **divergent thinking** (generating many possible answers, exploring in multiple directions). Standard IQ tests measure convergent thinking; creativity assessments measure divergent thinking.

Characteristics associated with creative thinking:
- Willingness to tolerate ambiguity
- Remote associative ability — connecting distantly related concepts
- **Incubation**: stepping away from a problem allows unconscious processing to find unexpected connections
- **Openness to experience** (personality trait): strong predictor of creative achievement

The **ten-year rule** (Ericsson): deep expertise in a domain typically requires ~10 years of deliberate practice before creative breakthroughs occur. Creativity is not spontaneous — it builds on a foundation of deep knowledge.

---

## Summary

Thinking uses concepts organized around prototypes. Problem-solving employs algorithms (guaranteed but slow) and heuristics (fast but fallible). Mental set and functional fixedness create obstacles to insight. Judgment relies heavily on heuristics — availability, representativeness, anchoring — that produce systematic biases. Framing effects show that logically equivalent choices produce different decisions depending on presentation. Dual-process theory distinguishes fast, intuitive System 1 from slow, deliberate System 2 thinking. Creativity draws on divergent thinking, remote association, and deep domain expertise.
