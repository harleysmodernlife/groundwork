Memory is not a recording. It is a reconstruction — an active process of encoding, storing, and retrieving information that is prone to distortion, loss, and revision. Understanding how memory actually works, rather than how we imagine it works, reveals both its remarkable capabilities and its systematic limitations.

---

## 1. The Three Processes of Memory

Memory involves three stages:

- **Encoding**: converting information into a form the brain can store. The quality of encoding determines how well information is retained. Deeper processing (thinking about meaning) produces better encoding than shallow processing (thinking about surface features).
- **Storage**: maintaining encoded information over time.
- **Retrieval**: accessing stored information when needed. Retrieval failure — forgetting that the information is there — is more common than storage failure.

---

## 2. Types of Memory

### Sensory Memory

Brief, pre-attentive storage of sensory information. **Iconic memory** (visual) lasts about 250ms; **echoic memory** (auditory) lasts about 3–4 seconds. More than we can attend to is briefly available before most of it decays.

### Working Memory (Short-Term Memory)

**Working memory** is the mental workspace for temporarily holding and manipulating information. It is the "workspace" of thought.

**George Miller (1956)**: the capacity of working memory is approximately **7 ± 2 chunks** (meaningful units). More recent estimates (Cowan, 2001) suggest the true limit may be closer to 4 chunks.

**Chunking**: meaningfully grouping items increases effective capacity. "H-F-B-I-C-I-A-J-F-K" is 9 letters, hard to remember as separate items — but "HFB-ICI-AJF-K" chunked as familiar abbreviations becomes far more manageable.

**Duration**: without rehearsal, information in working memory decays in about 15–30 seconds.

**Baddeley's model of working memory**: working memory is not a single store but a system with components:
- **Central executive**: attentional control; directs the other components
- **Phonological loop**: stores and rehearses verbal/auditory information (the inner voice)
- **Visuospatial sketchpad**: stores and manipulates visual and spatial information
- **Episodic buffer**: integrates information across sources and connects to long-term memory

### Long-Term Memory

Potentially unlimited in capacity and duration. Divided into:

**Explicit (declarative) memory** — consciously accessible:
- **Episodic memory**: personally experienced events ("I remember my first day of school")
- **Semantic memory**: general knowledge and facts ("Paris is the capital of France")

**Implicit (nondeclarative) memory** — not consciously accessible:
- **Procedural memory**: motor skills and habits (riding a bike, typing). Highly resistant to forgetting; doesn't require hippocampus.
- **Priming**: prior exposure to a stimulus influences response to a related stimulus, even without conscious awareness
- **Conditioned associations**: classically conditioned emotional responses (fear associations, taste aversions)

## 3. Encoding: Making Memories Stick

### Levels of Processing (Craik & Lockhart, 1972)

Information is encoded more durably when processed at deeper levels:
- **Shallow**: physical features (font, color) — poor retention
- **Intermediate**: phonetic features (does it rhyme with?) — moderate retention
- **Deep/semantic**: meaning (does it fit in this sentence? Does it describe you?) — excellent retention

**Self-reference effect**: encoding information in relation to yourself produces particularly good memory — "does this word describe me?" leads to better recall than "is this word pleasant?"

### Elaborative Encoding

Connecting new information to existing knowledge strengthens encoding. Asking "how does this relate to what I already know?" dramatically improves retention.

### The Testing Effect (Retrieval Practice)

The act of retrieving information — practicing recall — strengthens memory more than re-reading. Testing yourself (flashcards, practice questions) is consistently more effective for long-term retention than passively re-studying. The act of retrieval itself modifies and strengthens the memory trace.

### Spacing Effect

Distributed practice (studying at intervals) produces far better long-term retention than massed practice (cramming the same total study time into one session). The **forgetting curve** (Ebbinghaus) shows exponential forgetting shortly after learning; spaced repetition takes advantage of this by reviewing material just before it would be forgotten.

## 4. The Biology of Memory

**Long-term potentiation (LTP)**: when two neurons repeatedly fire together, the synapse between them strengthens — the postsynaptic neuron becomes more sensitive to the presynaptic one. LTP is the synaptic mechanism underlying learning and memory: "neurons that fire together wire together" (Hebb's postulate).

**Consolidation**: newly encoded memories are initially fragile and can be disrupted. Consolidation stabilizes them over time:
- **Synaptic consolidation**: occurs over hours; protein synthesis strengthens synaptic changes
- **System consolidation**: over weeks to years, memories are gradually transferred from hippocampus-dependent storage to distributed cortical storage. Sleep is critical for this process — **memory consolidation during sleep** explains why sleep deprivation impairs learning.

**The hippocampus** is critical for forming new explicit memories. Patient **H.M.** (Henry Molaison), who had bilateral hippocampal removal in 1953, was unable to form new long-term explicit memories — though his implicit memory (procedural learning, priming) was intact. He could learn new motor skills but had no memory of having practiced them.

## 5. Forgetting

### Decay Theory

Memories fade over time if not used. The **Ebbinghaus forgetting curve** shows that ~70% of newly learned material is forgotten within 24 hours without review.

### Interference Theory

Other memories interfere with recall:
- **Proactive interference**: old memories interfere with learning new information (years of driving on the right interferes when switching to the left)
- **Retroactive interference**: new learning interferes with old memories

### Retrieval Failure (Tip-of-the-Tongue Phenomenon)

Information is in storage but cannot be accessed. The **encoding specificity principle**: memory is best when retrieval cues match encoding context. **Context-dependent memory**: information recalled better in the same environment where it was learned. **State-dependent memory**: recall is better when emotional/physiological state at recall matches encoding state.

### Motivated Forgetting (Repression)

Freud proposed that threatening memories are pushed out of consciousness. The evidence for repression is weak, but evidence exists for **motivated forgetting** — people do sometimes suppress uncomfortable memories, and this involves prefrontal inhibition of hippocampal retrieval.

---

## Summary

Memory involves encoding, storage, and retrieval — all subject to failure. Sensory memory is brief; working memory is limited (~4 chunks); long-term memory is potentially unlimited but organized into explicit (episodic, semantic) and implicit (procedural, priming) systems. Deep encoding, elaboration, testing, and spaced practice improve memory durability. Long-term potentiation is the synaptic mechanism of learning; the hippocampus is essential for explicit memory formation and consolidation. Forgetting results from decay, interference, retrieval failure, and sometimes motivated suppression.
