We tend to think of perception as a window onto reality — an accurate recording of what's out there. But perception is not passive reception. It is active construction. The brain takes fragmentary, ambiguous sensory data and builds a model of the world — a model that is usually useful but sometimes dramatically wrong.

---

## 1. Perception Is Constructive

Perception is not like a camera passively recording what's in front of it. The brain receives limited and noisy sensory input and uses prior knowledge, expectations, and context to actively construct a perceptual experience.

**Evidence:**

- **Filling in the blind spot**: you have a blind spot where the optic nerve exits the retina, containing no photoreceptors. You don't see a hole in your visual field — the brain fills it in seamlessly with surrounding information.

- **Completion**: the Kanizsa triangle — three Pac-Man shapes arranged so that their open mouths suggest a triangle that isn't actually there. You vividly perceive the triangle's edges even though no edges are drawn.

- **Ambiguous figures**: the Necker cube and Rubin's vase (two faces or a vase?) can be perceived in two different ways; the sensory input is identical but the perceptual interpretation alternates. Your perception is not reading off reality — it's choosing an interpretation.

---

## 2. Top-Down Processing

**Top-down processing** means that higher-level knowledge, expectations, and context influence the processing of lower-level sensory input.

**Context effects:**

- The same gray square looks different when surrounded by a lighter vs. darker background (**simultaneous contrast**).
- A letter in a word that you've never encountered before is perceived correctly because the word context tells you what to expect — **context influences identification**.
- In a brief display of random letters, people are more likely to perceive a "B" in a sequence they expect to see letters and the number "13" in a sequence they expect to see numbers — the same ambiguous stimulus is perceived differently based on context.

**Priming**: recent exposure to one stimulus influences perception of a subsequent related stimulus. If you're shown "doctor" briefly, you're faster to read "nurse" — the concept has been activated.

**Expectations**: a doctor entering a hospital room is more likely to perceive a patient's symptoms as consistent with their working diagnosis — even when the evidence is ambiguous. This is adaptive (allows rapid pattern completion) and dangerous (causes perceptual errors).

---

## 3. Perceptual Constancies

Despite constantly changing sensory input, we perceive a stable world — objects maintain their identity even as distance, lighting, and angle change.

- **Size constancy**: a friend walking away from you doesn't appear to shrink — though the retinal image does — because the brain compensates based on distance cues
- **Shape constancy**: a door remains a rectangle as it opens and its retinal image becomes a trapezoid
- **Color constancy**: a white piece of paper looks white in dim yellow lamplight and in bright sunlight, despite reflecting very different wavelengths, because the brain calibrates color relative to the overall illumination

These are examples of the brain doing sophisticated inference to maintain a stable world model.

---

## 4. Depth and Distance Perception

The retina is 2D but we perceive a 3D world. The brain uses multiple cues:

**Binocular cues** (require both eyes):
- **Retinal disparity**: each eye sees a slightly different image; the brain computes depth from the degree of difference. Closer objects have more disparity.
- **Convergence**: the degree to which eyes turn inward to focus on close objects

**Monocular cues** (available with one eye; used in art and photography):
- **Relative size**: smaller objects are assumed to be farther away
- **Linear perspective**: parallel lines converge in the distance
- **Interposition**: an object that blocks another is closer
- **Texture gradient**: texture elements get denser with distance
- **Atmospheric perspective**: distant objects appear hazier and bluer
- **Height in visual field**: objects higher in the visual field appear farther away
- **Motion parallax**: close objects move more across the visual field during head movement than distant objects

## 5. Perceptual Illusions

Illusions are not failures — they reveal the heuristics and assumptions the brain normally uses successfully. When those assumptions are violated, we're tricked.

**Müller-Lyer illusion**: two equal lines with different arrowheads (inward vs. outward pointing) appear different in length. The inward-pointing arrowheads resemble the corners of rooms (where walls meet near you), and the brain interprets them as closer — and therefore physically larger at the same retinal size.

**Ponzo illusion**: two identical horizontal lines between converging lines appear different in length. The upper line appears longer because the converging lines signal linear perspective (railroad tracks), so the upper line appears to be farther away — and must therefore be physically larger.

**The moon illusion**: the moon appears larger on the horizon than overhead. This is not an optical illusion (atmospheric lensing). It is perceptual: terrain cues on the horizon provide distance information, and the brain interprets the horizon moon as farther away — and therefore larger — than the overhead moon.

---

## 6. Inattentional and Change Blindness

We don't perceive everything — we perceive what we attend to.

**Inattentional blindness**: failure to notice a fully visible but unexpected object because attention is directed elsewhere. In the famous "invisible gorilla" study (Simons and Chabris), participants asked to count basketball passes by a team in white failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene.

**Change blindness**: failure to notice changes between two views when the change coincides with a visual disruption (blink, cut, or distraction). Studies show people can miss dramatic changes to a scene or even a conversational partner's appearance when their attention is diverted.

These phenomena show that consciousness is not a complete recording of the environment — it is a sparse, attention-dependent sampling.

---

## 7. Perceptual Differences Between Individuals

Perception is influenced by:
- **Culture**: people from non-Western cultures (without rectangular architecture) show less susceptibility to the Müller-Lyer illusion
- **Language**: the Pirahã people of the Amazon (whose language lacks number words) perceive quantities differently. The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (language shapes thought) is supported in moderate form for color perception.
- **Experience and expertise**: a trained radiologist perceives a chest X-ray differently than a layperson; a chess master perceives a chess position differently than a novice
- **Emotional state**: anxious individuals perceive more ambiguous situations as threatening

---

## Summary

Perception is constructive — the brain builds a model of reality from incomplete sensory input, guided by expectations and prior knowledge. Top-down processing means context and expectations influence what we perceive. Perceptual constancies (size, shape, color) maintain a stable world model despite changing sensory input. Depth is perceived through binocular and monocular cues. Illusions reveal the perceptual heuristics the brain normally applies successfully. Inattentional and change blindness demonstrate that perception is attention-dependent — we do not perceive a complete, continuous record of our environment.
