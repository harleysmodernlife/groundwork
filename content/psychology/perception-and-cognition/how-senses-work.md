The world doesn't arrive in the brain pre-packaged. Raw physical stimuli — light waves, pressure variations, chemical molecules — must be converted into electrochemical signals the brain can process. This conversion, and the complex processing that follows, determines what we experience as reality. Sensation and perception are two distinct stages of this journey.

---

## 1. Sensation vs. Perception

- **Sensation**: the process by which sensory receptors detect physical stimuli and convert them into neural signals. This is largely bottom-up — driven by incoming data.
- **Perception**: the brain's interpretation of sensory signals — making sense of them, filling in gaps, and constructing a coherent experience. This is heavily top-down — shaped by expectations, memories, context, and prior knowledge.

The same sensory input can produce different perceptual experiences depending on context, attention, and mental state.

---

## 2. Transduction

**Transduction** is the conversion of a physical stimulus into a neural signal. Each sensory system has specialized **receptor cells** that perform this conversion:

- **Eyes**: photoreceptors (rods and cones) convert light into electrical signals
- **Ears**: hair cells in the cochlea convert sound vibrations into electrical signals
- **Skin**: various mechanoreceptors, thermoreceptors, and nociceptors (pain receptors) convert pressure, temperature, and damage into signals
- **Nose**: olfactory receptor neurons bind airborne chemical molecules
- **Tongue**: taste receptor cells bind dissolved chemicals

All sensory information is ultimately encoded as patterns of action potentials — the brain's universal language.

---

## 3. Absolute and Difference Thresholds

**Absolute threshold**: the minimum intensity of a stimulus that can be detected 50% of the time. For humans:
- Vision: a candle flame on a clear night at 30 miles
- Hearing: the tick of a watch 20 feet away in a quiet room
- Smell: one drop of perfume in a 6-room house
- Touch: a bee's wing falling from 1 cm onto your cheek

**Just noticeable difference (JND) / difference threshold**: the minimum difference between two stimuli that can be detected 50% of the time.

**Weber's Law**: the JND is a constant proportion of the original stimulus intensity. For weight, the JND is about 2% — you can detect a 2g difference between 100g and 102g, or a 20g difference between 1000g and 1020g, but not smaller differences. This proportion is the **Weber fraction**, which varies by sense.

---

## 4. Signal Detection Theory

Detection is not just about threshold — it also involves **decision criteria**. A radiologist looking for tumors may set a liberal criterion (say "yes" to any suspicious shadow) or conservative (only flag clearly suspicious cases). Their decisions involve:

- **Hit**: signal present and detected
- **Miss**: signal present but not detected
- **False alarm**: signal absent but "detected"
- **Correct rejection**: signal absent and correctly dismissed

Signal detection theory separates **sensitivity** (ability to detect a weak signal) from **response bias** (tendency to say yes or no regardless of signal strength). Motivation, experience, and costs/benefits influence where people set their criterion.

---

## 5. Sensory Adaptation

When exposed to a constant stimulus, sensory neurons reduce their firing rate over time — **sensory adaptation**. You stop noticing the hum of a refrigerator, the pressure of your clothing, or a persistent smell. Adaptation keeps the sensory system sensitive to *changes* in the environment — which is usually what matters for survival.

---

## 6. Vision

Light enters the eye through the **cornea** and **lens** (which focuses the image) onto the **retina** — the inner lining of the eye containing photoreceptors.

- **Rods**: ~120 million; at the periphery; sensitive to light/dark and motion; used in low-light conditions; not color-sensitive
- **Cones**: ~6 million; concentrated in the **fovea** (center of retina); sensitive to color (three types: short/blue, medium/green, long/red wavelengths); high acuity; require good lighting

Signals from rods and cones converge on **bipolar cells** → **ganglion cells** → the **optic nerve**. The two optic nerves meet at the **optic chiasm**, where fibers from each eye cross, sending information from each visual field to the opposite hemisphere.

**Visual cortex** (occipital lobe) processes basic features; higher visual areas process color, motion, objects, and faces.

**Color vision**: explained by **trichromacy theory** (three cone types; most color blindness results from missing or altered cones) and **opponent-process theory** (cones feed into opponent channels: red-green, blue-yellow, black-white). Both are correct at different levels of visual processing.

---

## 7. Hearing

**Sound** is pressure variations in the air (or other media) — measured in **frequency** (Hz; perceived as pitch) and **amplitude** (dB; perceived as loudness).

Sound travels through the outer ear → **ear canal** → **eardrum** (tympanic membrane) → three tiny bones (**ossicles**: malleus, incus, stapes) → **oval window** → fluid-filled **cochlea**.

In the cochlea, pressure waves move the **basilar membrane**, stimulating **hair cells**. Different frequencies maximally stimulate different locations on the basilar membrane (**tonotopic organization**):
- High frequencies → base of cochlea
- Low frequencies → apex

Hair cell signals travel via the **auditory nerve** to the **auditory cortex** in the temporal lobe.

**Hearing loss**: can be **conductive** (damaged middle ear structures) or **sensorineural** (damaged hair cells — irreversible; most common cause is loud noise exposure).

---

## 8. Other Senses

**Smell (olfaction)**: ~400 types of olfactory receptor neurons in the nasal cavity; each responds to different molecules. The only sense that doesn't relay through the thalamus first — olfactory signals go directly to the limbic system (amygdala, hippocampus), explaining why smells are so powerfully linked to emotion and memory.

**Taste (gustation)**: five basic tastes: sweet, salty, sour, bitter, **umami** (savory). Most of what we call "flavor" is actually olfaction — hence food tasting bland when you have a cold.

**Touch**: skin contains mechanoreceptors (pressure, vibration), thermoreceptors (temperature), and **nociceptors** (pain). Pain is not simply the activation of nociceptors — it is modulated by attention, expectation, and context (gate-control theory; endorphins).

**Proprioception**: sense of body position and movement, from receptors in muscles and joints.

**Vestibular sense**: sense of balance and head movement, from the semicircular canals and otolith organs in the inner ear.

---

## Summary

Sensation converts physical stimuli into neural signals through transduction; perception interprets those signals using top-down and bottom-up processes. Absolute and difference thresholds describe detection limits; sensory adaptation reduces response to constant stimuli. Vision relies on rods and cones in the retina; hearing on hair cells in the cochlea. Each sense has specialized receptors tuned to its particular physical stimulus modality. Smell uniquely connects directly to the limbic system, explaining its power to evoke memory and emotion.
