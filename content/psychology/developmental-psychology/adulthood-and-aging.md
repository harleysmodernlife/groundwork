Development doesn't stop at 18. Adulthood and aging bring their own challenges, transitions, and opportunities for growth. The decisions, relationships, and adaptations of adult life shape well-being just as powerfully as early childhood experiences — and the psychology of aging challenges many cultural assumptions about decline.

---

## 1. Erikson's Adult Stages

Erik Erikson proposed three psychosocial stages beyond adolescence:

### Intimacy vs. Isolation (Young Adulthood, ~20s–40s)

Having established an identity, the developmental challenge is forming **intimate relationships** — deep, committed bonds with a partner and close friends. Those who can share themselves authentically with others develop a capacity for intimacy; those who avoid this risk remain isolated and self-absorbed.

Romantic partnership is the prototype, but intimacy extends to close friendships and collaborative relationships. The capacity to truly commit to another person — risking rejection and loss — depends partly on the security established in earlier development.

### Generativity vs. Stagnation (Middle Adulthood, ~40s–60s)

The central task is **generativity**: contributing to the next generation and to society — through parenting, mentoring, teaching, creative work, or civic involvement. It is the sense of giving back and leaving something of value beyond oneself.

Stagnation is the failure of generativity — self-absorption, a sense that life is passing without purpose. Middle adults who achieve generativity report higher life satisfaction and better physical health.

### Integrity vs. Despair (Late Adulthood, ~65+)

Looking back on one's life, the question is whether it was meaningful and well-lived. Those who can accept their choices, mistakes, and the life they actually lived achieve **ego integrity** — a sense of completion and acceptance. Those filled with regret and bitterness, who see their lives as wasted, experience **despair**.

## 2. Cognitive Development in Adulthood

### Intelligence in Adulthood: Two Trajectories

**Fluid intelligence** — reasoning ability, working memory, processing speed, novel problem-solving — peaks in the mid-20s and declines gradually thereafter.

**Crystallized intelligence** — accumulated knowledge, vocabulary, expertise, and wisdom — continues to increase through middle adulthood and remains relatively stable or even grows into later adulthood.

This explains why older adults often outperform younger people on tasks requiring knowledge and expertise, even as they struggle with novel problems requiring pure processing speed.

### The Seattle Longitudinal Study (Schaie)

This landmark study followed adults from their 20s to 80s. Findings:
- Most cognitive abilities show little decline before age 60
- Significant individual differences — many people show minimal decline even into their 70s and 80s
- Different abilities decline at different rates; verbal abilities are most preserved
- Cognitive engagement throughout life (continued learning, complex work) predicts better preservation

### Factors that Protect Cognitive Aging

- **Education**: higher educational attainment predicts better cognitive reserve
- **Physical exercise**: the most consistently effective modifiable factor; aerobic exercise preserves hippocampal volume and slows decline
- **Cognitively stimulating activities**: sustained intellectual engagement ("use it or lose it")
- **Social engagement**: maintained social networks and meaningful relationships
- **Healthy lifestyle**: cardiovascular health, sleep, diet affect brain aging

## 3. Relationships in Adulthood

**Marriage and partnership**: long-term partnerships are among the strongest predictors of health and longevity — for both men and women, though the effect has historically been larger for men. The quality of the relationship matters enormously; unhappy marriages are associated with worse health outcomes than being unmarried.

**The Gottman Four Horsemen**: John Gottman's research on couples identified four communication patterns that predict divorce with high accuracy:
- **Criticism**: attacking the partner's character rather than the behavior
- **Contempt**: belittling, mocking, eye-rolling — the most toxic predictor
- **Defensiveness**: refusing accountability, counter-attacking
- **Stonewalling**: emotional withdrawal and non-responsiveness

Couples who handle conflict with humor, repair attempts, and respect maintain stable, satisfying relationships.

**Friendship**: adult friendships become smaller in number but deeper in quality over time. Social network size typically peaks in young adulthood and narrows in later adulthood — but satisfaction with social relationships often increases as people become more selective.

## 4. Physical Aging

### The Body

- **Sensory decline**: vision (presbyopia — difficulty focusing on close objects; common from 40s), hearing (presbycusis — high-frequency hearing loss, beginning in 40s–50s; accelerated by noise exposure)
- **Slowing**: reaction time slows, processing speed decreases, physical recovery takes longer
- **Health vulnerabilities**: risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, and arthritis increases; immune function declines
- **Menopause** (women, ~45–55): cessation of menstruation; decreased estrogen production; hot flashes, sleep disruption, mood changes; variable psychological impact

### The Brain

Normal aging involves:
- Gradual neuronal loss (less than once thought; more significant is reduced synaptic density)
- Slower processing speed
- More difficulty with divided attention and multitasking
- Reduced working memory capacity
- Relatively preserved long-term memory, vocabulary, and procedural memory

## 5. Dementia and Alzheimer's Disease

**Dementia** refers to a progressive decline in cognitive function severe enough to interfere with daily life — it is not a normal part of aging. The most common cause (~60–70%) is **Alzheimer's disease**.

**Alzheimer's disease** involves:
- Accumulation of **amyloid plaques** (protein deposits between neurons)
- **Neurofibrillary tangles** (tau protein disrupting transport within neurons)
- Neuronal death — particularly in the hippocampus, then spreading to other regions

Early signs: memory problems (especially for new information), difficulty with word-finding, getting lost in familiar places, personality changes. Progression leads to inability to perform activities of daily living.

Risk factors: age, family history (strong genetic component), APOE-e4 gene variant, cardiovascular risk factors, low educational attainment, and head trauma.

No disease-modifying treatment currently exists, though several drugs targeting amyloid have shown modest effects in clinical trials.

## 6. Well-Being and Happiness in Later Life

Counterintuitively, many studies show that **subjective well-being** — life satisfaction and positive affect — remains stable or even increases in older adulthood despite physical decline and social losses.

**Socioemotional selectivity theory** (Carstensen): as people perceive their time horizon shrinking, they prioritize emotionally meaningful goals and relationships over future-oriented goals. Older adults choose fewer but deeper social connections and experience less negative emotion than younger adults — partly because they selectively withdraw from relationships and situations that generate negative affect.

**The positivity effect**: older adults attend to and remember positive information better than negative information, unlike younger adults. This is not denial — it is an adaptive shift in emotional regulation priorities.

**Successful aging**: involves maintaining physical and cognitive function, engagement with life, and positive emotion. The research consistently shows that attitude, meaning, and social connection matter more than objective health status in predicting subjective well-being in old age.

---

## Summary

Erikson's adult stages — intimacy vs. isolation, generativity vs. stagnation, and integrity vs. despair — describe the central psychosocial challenges from young adulthood through old age. Fluid intelligence declines with age while crystallized intelligence is preserved; exercise and cognitive engagement slow decline. Relationship quality powerfully predicts health and longevity. Physical aging includes sensory decline and slowing; dementia is not normal aging. Counterintuitively, subjective well-being often improves with age as people prioritize emotionally meaningful goals (socioemotional selectivity theory) and show a positivity bias in attention and memory.
