From the first steps to the first job application, development across childhood and adolescence involves dramatic changes in the body, brain, relationships, and sense of self. Understanding these changes helps explain why children think the way they do at different ages — and why adolescence is such a turbulent and consequential period.

---

## 1. Physical Development in Childhood

**Early childhood (2–6 years)**: rapid growth slows from the infancy pace; the brain continues myelinating, improving coordination and motor control. Children develop fine motor skills (drawing, using scissors) and gross motor skills (running, jumping, climbing). Sleep needs are high (10–13 hours); brain development is still proceeding rapidly.

**Middle childhood (6–11 years)**: growth is steady, about 5–7 cm and 2–3 kg per year. Physical activity is important for health and cognitive development — children who are physically active show better attention, memory, and academic performance.

**Brain development**: the prefrontal cortex continues to develop throughout childhood, gradually improving impulse control, planning, and emotional regulation. Full maturation doesn't occur until the mid-20s.

## 2. Social and Emotional Development in Childhood

### Parenting Styles

Diana Baumrind identified four parenting styles defined by dimensions of **demandingness** (behavioral expectations) and **responsiveness** (warmth and sensitivity):

- **Authoritative**: high demandingness + high responsiveness. Sets clear expectations with warmth and explanation. Associated with the best outcomes: children who are confident, self-reliant, socially competent, and academically successful.
- **Authoritarian**: high demandingness + low responsiveness. Strict rules, little warmth, "because I said so." Associated with obedience but also lower self-esteem and social competence.
- **Permissive**: low demandingness + high responsiveness. Warm but few rules. Associated with impulsivity and lower achievement.
- **Uninvolved (neglectful)**: low demandingness + low responsiveness. Little engagement or supervision. Associated with the worst outcomes across all domains.

**Note**: parenting style effects are real but modest and bidirectional — children's temperament influences parenting, and parenting influences children's behavior. Cultural context also matters; authoritarian parenting is more normative and has less negative effects in some cultural contexts.

### Peer Relationships

By middle childhood, peer relationships become increasingly important. Social acceptance or rejection by peers predicts later adjustment.

**Play** is the primary vehicle of social development in early childhood:
- **Parallel play** (2–3 years): playing alongside without interaction
- **Associative play** (3–4 years): similar activities with some interaction but no coordination
- **Cooperative play** (4+ years): coordinated, structured play with shared goals

**Friendships** in middle childhood are based on shared interests and loyalty; best friendships involve self-disclosure and emotional support.

## 3. Puberty

**Puberty** is the biological transition to sexual maturity, driven by the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis. Timing varies:

- **Girls**: average onset 8–13 years (breast development, pubic hair); menarche (first menstruation) average ~12–13 years
- **Boys**: average onset 9–14 years (testicular enlargement); voice change, pubic hair, first ejaculation follows

**Secular trend**: the average age of puberty onset has declined in Western countries over the past 150 years — probably due to improved nutrition, reduced childhood illness, and possibly environmental endocrine disruptors.

**Early vs. late maturation**: timing relative to peers has psychological consequences. Early-maturing girls face elevated risk for depression, eating disorders, and early sexual behavior — they may be physically ahead of their emotional maturity and attract unwanted attention. Early-maturing boys tend to enjoy higher peer status initially. Late-maturing boys may experience lower self-esteem during adolescence but catch up.

## 4. Brain Development in Adolescence

The adolescent brain is not simply an adult brain in a smaller body — it is undergoing a distinctive developmental transition.

**Synaptic pruning** proceeds most dramatically in the prefrontal cortex during adolescence, making thinking more efficient but temporarily disrupting some functions.

**The dual systems model** (Steinberg): two brain systems develop on different timescales:
- **Reward/socioemotional system**: the limbic system (especially the striatum/nucleus accumbens) surges in sensitivity to reward during early adolescence — driven by pubertal hormones
- **Cognitive control system**: the prefrontal cortex (which regulates impulse and modulates reward) continues developing through the mid-20s

The mismatch — heightened reward sensitivity before the regulatory system fully matures — explains why adolescents are prone to:
- Risk-taking and sensation-seeking
- Heightened peer influence (others' approval is intensely rewarding)
- Poor decisions in emotionally charged situations (even adolescents who can reason perfectly well in calm settings make poor decisions under emotional arousal or peer presence)

## 5. Identity Development

**Erik Erikson** proposed a lifespan theory of psychosocial development, with each stage involving a central tension:

Key stages through adolescence:
- **Trust vs. mistrust** (infancy): does my world provide reliable care?
- **Autonomy vs. shame and doubt** (toddlerhood): can I control my own body and actions?
- **Initiative vs. guilt** (preschool): can I take initiative and pursue goals?
- **Industry vs. inferiority** (middle childhood): can I master skills and achieve?
- **Identity vs. role confusion** (adolescence): who am I?

**Adolescence** is Erikson's most famous stage. The central task is developing a coherent **identity** — a stable sense of who one is, one's values, and one's direction in life. Failure to resolve this leads to role confusion.

**James Marcia** (Erikson's student) operationalized identity development in terms of two dimensions:
- **Exploration**: actively considering different possibilities for identity
- **Commitment**: making a choice and committing to it

Four identity statuses:
- **Identity diffusion**: neither exploring nor committed (drifting)
- **Identity foreclosure**: committed without exploration (accepting parents' identity without questioning)
- **Identity moratorium**: actively exploring but not yet committed (the "college years" crisis)
- **Identity achievement**: commitment following exploration — the most adaptive status

## 6. Adolescent Risk-Taking and Judgment

Adolescents are not simply irrational — in cool, non-emotionally charged settings, they often reason as well as adults. But in emotionally arousing situations, especially with peers present, they make riskier decisions.

**Peer influence**: the presence of peers dramatically increases risk-taking in adolescents but not in adults (study using a simulated driving game). Peers make risky behavior more rewarding — peer approval activates reward circuits.

**Developmental appropriateness**: some adolescent risk-taking serves developmental functions — exploring identity, separating from parents, experiencing the consequences of choices. The goal is to support autonomy while maintaining enough structure to prevent serious harm.

---

## Summary

Physical development across childhood involves steady growth, motor skill acquisition, and ongoing brain maturation. Authoritative parenting (warm + demanding) is consistently associated with the best outcomes. Puberty initiates biological sexual maturity; timing relative to peers has psychological consequences. The adolescent brain's mismatch between reward sensitivity (early maturation) and cognitive control (later maturation) underlies elevated risk-taking, peer sensitivity, and emotional reactivity. Erikson's identity vs. role confusion crisis defines adolescence's central psychological task; Marcia identified four identity statuses reflecting different combinations of exploration and commitment.
