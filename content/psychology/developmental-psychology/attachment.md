Attachment is the deep emotional bond that forms between infants and their caregivers. It is not merely fondness or dependence — it is a fundamental biological system that shapes social and emotional development across the lifespan. The quality of early attachment relationships influences how people relate to others, regulate emotions, and navigate stress throughout their lives.

---

## 1. Bowlby's Attachment Theory

**John Bowlby** (1958–1969) proposed that attachment is a biologically based behavioral system that evolved to keep infants close to caregivers — increasing their chances of survival. He was influenced by ethology (the study of animal behavior) and by observations of children separated from their parents during World War II.

**Key ideas:**

**Attachment behavioral system**: infants are born with a set of behaviors (crying, clinging, following, smiling) that elicit caregiving from adults and maintain proximity. The caregiver is a **secure base** from which the child can explore and a **safe haven** to return to when stressed.

**Internal working models**: through repeated interactions with caregivers, infants construct mental representations of themselves and others — the **internal working model**. This model captures whether others are trustworthy and available, and whether the self is worthy of care. These models become templates for future relationships.

**Monotropy**: Bowlby proposed that infants form a primary attachment to one figure (usually the mother), with secondary attachments to others. Later research suggests infants can form multiple simultaneous attachments, though they do show hierarchical preferences.

**Phases of attachment development:**
- **Pre-attachment (0–6 weeks)**: infants respond to social stimuli but don't differentiate caregivers
- **Attachment in the making (6 weeks–6 months)**: infants recognize familiar people and respond more to them
- **Clear-cut attachment (7 months–2 years)**: intense attachment to specific figures; stranger anxiety and separation anxiety emerge
- **Goal-corrected partnership (2+ years)**: child understands the caregiver's perspective and can negotiate proximity-seeking

## 2. Ainsworth's Strange Situation

**Mary Ainsworth** developed the **Strange Situation** procedure (1978) to assess attachment quality in one-year-olds. The procedure involves a series of separations and reunions between infant, caregiver, and a stranger:

1. Mother and infant in room (2 min)
2. Stranger enters and interacts (3 min)
3. Mother leaves; stranger remains (3 min or less if infant highly distressed)
4. Mother returns; stranger leaves (3 min)
5. Mother leaves; infant alone (3 min or less)
6. Stranger returns (3 min)
7. Mother returns; stranger leaves (3 min)

**The key behavior is reunion behavior** — how the infant responds when the caregiver returns after separation.

### Secure Attachment (~60%)

- **Behavior**: uses parent as a secure base to explore; shows distress at separation but is quickly comforted upon reunion; balances closeness with exploration
- **Underlying experience**: caregiver is consistently available and responsive
- **Internal working model**: "I am lovable; others are trustworthy"

### Anxious-Ambivalent (Resistant) Attachment (~15%)

- **Behavior**: clingy; distressed even before separation; difficult to soothe at reunion; alternates between contact-seeking and angry rejection ("push-pull" pattern)
- **Underlying experience**: caregiver is inconsistently responsive — sometimes available, sometimes not; infant can't predict when needs will be met
- **Internal working model**: uncertainty about the self and others; hyperactivation of attachment system

### Avoidant Attachment (~20%)

- **Behavior**: little distress at separation; ignores or avoids the caregiver at reunion; appears independent; actually physiologically aroused despite behavioral calm
- **Underlying experience**: caregiver is consistently emotionally unavailable or rejecting; attachment behavior has been learned to be ineffective or punished
- **Internal working model**: "I must rely on myself; others are untrustworthy"

### Disorganized Attachment (~15%, higher in high-risk samples)

- **Behavior**: contradictory, confused, or frightened behavior at reunion; may approach caregiver with head turned away, freeze, or engage in odd stereotyped behavior
- **Underlying experience**: caregiver is both a source of fear and the solution to fear (as in abuse or severe neglect) — the attachment system cannot organize a coherent strategy
- **Outcomes**: most strongly associated with later psychopathology

## 3. Adult Attachment

Attachment patterns established in infancy do not simply disappear — they shape adult romantic relationships.

Hazan and Shaver (1987) proposed that adult romantic love is an attachment process:
- **Secure adults**: comfortable with intimacy, trust partners, balance closeness and independence
- **Anxious-preoccupied adults**: crave closeness but fear abandonment; emotionally volatile in relationships
- **Dismissing-avoidant adults**: uncomfortable with intimacy; value independence; suppress attachment needs
- **Fearful-avoidant adults**: desire closeness but fear it; distrusting; high emotional volatility (corresponds roughly to disorganized)

Adult attachment is assessed through:
- **Adult Attachment Interview (AAI)**: asks about childhood experiences with parents; scores are based on the *coherence and organization* of the narrative, not the content
- **Adult attachment questionnaires**: self-report of relationship patterns

The **continuity from infant to adult attachment** is modest (correlation ~.30–.40) — early attachment shapes working models that influence later relationships, but significant life events and later relationships can also revise working models.

---

## 4. What Shapes Attachment Security?

**Sensitivity and responsiveness**: the most consistent predictor of secure attachment is **maternal sensitivity** — the caregiver's ability to perceive and respond appropriately and promptly to the infant's signals. Sensitive caregiving communicates that the infant's needs matter and will be addressed.

**Caregiver mental health**: maternal depression, anxiety, and trauma history are risk factors for insecure attachment — they can impair the caregiver's ability to read and respond to the infant.

**Transmission of attachment across generations**: parents' own Adult Attachment classification (coherent vs. incoherent narratives) predicts their infant's attachment classification. The mechanism is thought to be parenting behavior — how securely attached parents mentalize (understand their child's experience) and respond to distress.

**Child temperament**: plays a role, though modest. A difficult temperament doesn't cause insecure attachment; rather, it may influence the *type* of insecure attachment that forms.

---

## 5. Long-Term Consequences

Attachment security in infancy predicts:
- **Social competence**: secure children show more effective peer relationships in preschool and beyond
- **Emotional regulation**: secure children are better at managing difficult emotions
- **Cognitive functioning**: secure children are more willing to explore and more persistent in problem-solving
- **Mental health**: insecure, especially disorganized, attachment is associated with elevated risk for psychopathology

These effects are real but not deterministic — later secure relationships (with a teacher, partner, or therapist) can provide a "corrective emotional experience" and revise working models.

---

## Summary

Bowlby proposed that attachment is a biologically based system that keeps infants close to caregivers, with lasting effects mediated through internal working models of self and others. Ainsworth's Strange Situation identified four patterns: secure (consistent caregiving), anxious-ambivalent (inconsistent caregiving), avoidant (rejection), and disorganized (frightening caregiving). Adult romantic relationships reflect attachment working models. Caregiver sensitivity is the primary predictor of secure attachment. Security in infancy predicts better social, emotional, and cognitive outcomes — but later relationships can modify working models throughout life.
