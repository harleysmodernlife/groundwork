How does thinking change as children grow? Jean Piaget proposed the most influential answer in psychology's history: children are not simply smaller, less-informed adults — they think in qualitatively different ways at different stages of development. Modern research has both confirmed many of Piaget's insights and significantly revised his timeline and mechanisms.

---

## 1. Piaget's Core Ideas

**Jean Piaget** (1896–1980) was a Swiss developmental psychologist who spent decades carefully observing children's thinking — including his own three children — and building a comprehensive theory.

**Schemas**: mental frameworks that organize knowledge and experience. Infants have simple sensorimotor schemas (grasping, sucking); adults have complex abstract schemas (democracy, evolution).

**Adaptation**: how schemas change in response to new experiences:
- **Assimilation**: fitting new information into existing schemas. A child who knows about dogs may call a horse a "doggie" — assimilating it into the existing dog schema.
- **Accommodation**: changing a schema to fit new information. When corrected, the child accommodates by creating a new "horse" schema.

**Equilibration**: the drive toward cognitive balance. Disequilibrium — encountering something that doesn't fit existing schemas — motivates the child to accommodate and restore equilibrium. This is the engine of cognitive development.

## 2. Piaget's Four Stages

### Sensorimotor Stage (Birth to ~2 years)

Infants know the world through sensory experience and motor action — they lack the ability to represent the world mentally.

**Key achievement: Object permanence** — the understanding that objects continue to exist when out of sight. Piaget tested this by hiding a toy under a cloth — infants under ~8 months would not search for the hidden object ("out of sight, out of mind"). By 12 months, most infants search for hidden objects. By 18 months, they can track an object through multiple invisible displacements.

**Critique**: looking-time methods (measuring how long infants stare at scenes that violate expectations) suggest rudimentary object permanence by 3–4 months — much earlier than Piaget believed. The standard search task may underestimate competence because it requires motor planning, not just mental representation.

### Preoperational Stage (~2–7 years)

Children can now represent the world mentally through language, imagery, and symbolic play. But thinking has important limitations:

**Egocentrism**: difficulty taking another's perspective. Classic demonstration: the **three mountains task** — a child is shown a model of three mountains from one viewpoint and asked what a doll sitting across from the model sees. Preoperational children describe their own view, not the doll's.

**Lack of conservation**: failure to understand that quantity remains the same despite changes in appearance. Pour water from a short wide glass into a tall narrow glass in front of a 5-year-old — they will typically claim the tall glass has more water, even though they saw the same water transferred. Piaget interpreted this as indicating the child can't hold multiple dimensions (height and width) in mind simultaneously and focuses on the most salient feature (**centration**).

**Irreversibility**: inability to mentally reverse an operation. The preoperational child cannot think through "if I pour this back, will it be the same?"

**Animism**: attributing life to inanimate objects ("the sun is sleeping").

### Concrete Operational Stage (~7–11 years)

Children acquire **logical operations** that can be applied to concrete objects and events:

- **Conservation**: understanding that quantity is preserved through transformations
- **Classification**: organizing objects into hierarchies (dogs are a subset of animals)
- **Seriation**: arranging objects in order of a dimension (length, weight)
- **Reversibility**: mentally undoing an action

**Limitation**: these operations require concrete objects; abstract hypothetical reasoning is still difficult. If you ask a 9-year-old "if Mary is taller than Sue, and Sue is taller than Joan, who is taller — Mary or Joan?" without physical objects to manipulate, many struggle.

### Formal Operational Stage (~12+ years)

Abstract, hypothetical, and systematic reasoning becomes possible. Adolescents can:
- Reason about hypothetical situations ("What if there were no gravity?")
- Think about thinking (metacognition)
- Reason systematically through all possible combinations
- Consider abstract concepts like justice, love, and identity

**Critique**: Piaget thought most people achieve formal operations by late adolescence. Research suggests formal operational thinking is more domain-specific and context-dependent than Piaget believed — many adults don't use formal reasoning in all contexts, and its development depends heavily on education and experience.

## 3. Vygotsky's Sociocultural Theory

**Lev Vygotsky** (1896–1934) offered an important alternative to Piaget's model, emphasizing the social and cultural nature of cognitive development.

**Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)**: the gap between what a child can do independently and what they can do with guidance from a more capable person (teacher, parent, peer). Learning happens in this zone.

**Scaffolding**: the support provided by a more capable person — instructions, demonstrations, prompting, questions — that enables the child to perform at the outer edge of their current ability. As competence develops, scaffolding is gradually withdrawn.

**Language and thought**: Vygotsky believed language is the primary tool of thought. Private speech (children talking to themselves) is not autistic — it is an internalized version of social dialogue that regulates thinking. It gradually becomes inner speech (thinking in words silently).

**Cultural tools**: cognitive development is shaped by the cultural tools available — language, number systems, literacy, computers. Cultures differ in what cognitive skills they value and teach; development is not universal in the Piagetian sense.

## 4. Information Processing Approach

Rather than describing discrete stages, the **information processing** approach describes development as gradual improvement in:
- **Processing speed**: older children process information faster
- **Working memory capacity**: expands from early childhood through adolescence
- **Attention control**: ability to inhibit irrelevant information and sustain focus improves
- **Strategy use**: older children spontaneously use memory strategies (rehearsal, organization, elaboration) that younger children don't
- **Metacognition**: thinking about one's own thinking improves; older children and adults can monitor comprehension, regulate strategies, and plan

---

## 5. Theory of Mind

**Theory of mind** is the ability to attribute mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions, emotions — to others, understanding that their minds may differ from one's own.

**False belief task** (Wimmer & Perner, 1983): a child watches a character put an object in location A. The character leaves; the object is moved to location B. The child is asked: where will the character look for the object when they return? Children under ~4 say location B — where the object actually is — not realizing the character has a false belief. By 4–5, most children correctly answer location A.

Theory of mind is impaired in autism spectrum disorder — contributing to difficulties with social understanding, pretend play, and communication.

---

## Summary

Piaget proposed children progress through four qualitatively distinct cognitive stages: sensorimotor (action-based), preoperational (symbolic but egocentric and illogical), concrete operational (logical about concrete things), and formal operational (abstract reasoning). Modern research shows competence emerges earlier than Piaget believed. Vygotsky emphasized the Zone of Proximal Development and scaffolding — cognitive development is inherently social and cultural. The information processing approach describes gradual improvements in processing speed, working memory, attention, and metacognition. Theory of mind — understanding others have beliefs that may be false — develops around age 4.
