Development begins before birth. From the moment of conception, a complex interplay of genetic programming and environmental influence shapes the growing organism. The prenatal period and infancy are particularly critical — experiences during these early stages can have lasting effects on physical, cognitive, and emotional development.

---

## 1. Prenatal Development

**Fertilization** occurs when a sperm cell penetrates an ovum, forming a **zygote** — a single cell containing the full genetic blueprint (46 chromosomes, 23 from each parent).

**Three stages of prenatal development:**

### Germinal Stage (0–2 weeks)

The zygote divides rapidly as it travels down the fallopian tube. By day 4–5, a **blastocyst** (hollow ball of ~100 cells) forms and implants in the uterine wall. Implantation failure — the most common form of early pregnancy loss — affects an estimated 30–50% of fertilized eggs.

### Embryonic Stage (weeks 2–8)

The most critical period for organ formation. The embryo develops from three tissue layers:
- **Ectoderm** → nervous system, skin, sense organs
- **Mesoderm** → muscles, skeleton, circulatory system
- **Endoderm** → digestive and respiratory organs

By week 8, all major organs are present in rudimentary form. The embryo is about 2.5 cm long and weighs about 1 gram. This is the period of greatest vulnerability to **teratogens** — agents that disrupt development.

### Fetal Stage (week 8 to birth)

Growth and refinement of existing structures. Major brain development occurs throughout this stage. By month 5, the fetus moves and the mother can feel kicks. Lungs mature enough for survival outside the womb by approximately 24–28 weeks (viability threshold, with intensive medical support).

---

## 2. Teratogens

A **teratogen** is any agent that can disrupt prenatal development. The impact depends on **timing** (embryonic stage is most critical), **dose**, and **genetic vulnerability**.

**Alcohol**: the leading preventable cause of intellectual and developmental disability in the world. **Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (FASDs)** range from mild learning difficulties to severe **Fetal Alcohol Syndrome (FAS)**: intellectual disability, characteristic facial features, growth retardation, and behavioral problems. No safe level of alcohol consumption during pregnancy has been established.

**Tobacco**: nicotine constricts blood vessels, reducing oxygen and nutrient delivery. Associated with low birth weight, premature birth, and increased risk of SIDS (Sudden Infant Death Syndrome).

**Prescription and illicit drugs**: some medications are teratogenic (thalidomide, a 1950s sedative, caused severe limb malformations). Many illicit drugs are harmful; opioid-exposed newborns may experience neonatal abstinence syndrome.

**Infections**: rubella (German measles), cytomegalovirus, toxoplasmosis, and Zika virus can cause severe birth defects, particularly during the embryonic period.

**Environmental toxins**: lead (associated with cognitive deficits), mercury, and some pesticides are known teratogens.

**Maternal nutrition**: folic acid deficiency in early pregnancy is associated with neural tube defects (spina bifida, anencephaly). Adequate nutrition is critical throughout pregnancy.

**Maternal stress**: severe, chronic prenatal stress is associated with preterm birth, low birth weight, and elevated cortisol in infants — which may have lasting effects on stress reactivity.

## 3. The Newborn

Newborns are remarkably capable despite their helplessness. They arrive with:

**Reflexes**: involuntary, automatic responses to stimulation:
- **Rooting reflex**: turning the head and opening the mouth when the cheek is stroked
- **Sucking reflex**: sucking when something touches the lips
- **Moro (startle) reflex**: extending arms and crying in response to sudden movement or noise
- **Grasp reflex**: closing the fingers around an object placed in the palm
- **Babinski reflex**: fanning the toes when the sole is stroked
Most neonatal reflexes disappear in the first months as cortical development inhibits them. Their presence and disappearance on schedule is used to assess neurological health.

**Sensory abilities**: newborns see best at ~20–30 cm (about the distance to a nursing mother's face); prefer faces to other patterns; prefer their mother's voice (having heard it in utero) and their mother's smell; have functional taste and are sensitive to pain.

## 4. Physical and Brain Development in Infancy

**Brain growth**: at birth, the brain is about 25% of its adult size; by age 2, it reaches 75%. The brain grows through:
- **Myelination**: wrapping axons in myelin to speed transmission (continues through young adulthood, with frontal lobes last)
- **Synaptic proliferation**: explosive growth in synaptic connections in the first two years — more synapses than will ever be needed
- **Synaptic pruning**: "use it or lose it" — connections that are not activated are eliminated; begins around age 2 and continues through adolescence. Experience shapes which connections survive.

**Motor development**: follows a predictable cephalocaudal (head to tail) and proximodistal (center to periphery) sequence:

| Age | Milestone |
|-----|-----------|
| 2 months | holds head up |
| 3–4 months | rolls over |
| 6–7 months | sits without support |
| 8–10 months | pulls to stand, crawls |
| 11–13 months | walks |
| 18 months | runs (roughly) |

These norms show substantial individual variation. Timing of motor milestones has modest predictive value for development.

## 5. Perceptual Development

Infants are not blank slates perceptually — they actively make sense of their environment.

**Visual cliff**: Eleanor Gibson placed infants on a platform with a thick glass floor, half of which appeared to be a cliff drop. Even 6-month-olds refused to crawl over the "deep" side, suggesting depth perception is present by the age of independent locomotion.

**Object permanence**: knowing that objects continue to exist when out of sight. Young infants (before ~8 months) fail Piaget's tasks requiring them to search for hidden objects — suggesting they don't yet understand objects as permanent. (Though modern methods using looking-time suggest some object permanence earlier.)

**Cross-modal perception**: infants can recognize objects across senses — an object felt but not seen can be recognized visually. This suggests early ability to integrate information across modalities.

---

## Summary

Prenatal development proceeds through germinal (implantation), embryonic (organ formation — most sensitive to teratogens), and fetal (growth and refinement) stages. Teratogens including alcohol, tobacco, drugs, infections, and environmental toxins can cause lasting harm, with impact depending on timing and dose. Newborns arrive with reflexes, sensory abilities, and preferences shaped by prenatal experience. Infancy involves explosive brain growth through myelination, synaptic proliferation, and pruning — shaped by experience. Motor development follows a predictable head-to-tail, center-to-periphery sequence. Perceptual abilities — including depth perception and early object permanence — develop through the first year.
