Every thought you've ever had, every memory you've stored, every emotion you've felt, every movement you've made was generated by electrochemical activity in your nervous system. Understanding how neurons work and how the brain is organized is foundational to understanding psychology from the biological ground up.

---

## 1. The Neuron

The **neuron** is the basic cellular unit of the nervous system — a cell specialized for transmitting information. The human brain contains an estimated 86 billion neurons, each connected to thousands of others in a network of extraordinary complexity.

### Neuron Structure

- **Dendrites**: branching extensions that receive incoming signals from other neurons
- **Cell body (soma)**: contains the nucleus and metabolic machinery; integrates incoming signals
- **Axon**: a long projection that carries signals away from the cell body to other neurons, muscles, or glands. Can range from millimeters to over a meter long (in spinal motor neurons).
- **Myelin sheath**: a fatty insulating coating around many axons, produced by glial cells (Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system; oligodendrocytes in the CNS). Myelin dramatically speeds signal transmission by allowing the signal to jump from node to node (**saltatory conduction**). Damage to myelin (as in multiple sclerosis) impairs neural transmission.
- **Axon terminals (synaptic knobs)**: the end of the axon, which releases neurotransmitters

### Glial Cells

Neurons are outnumbered roughly 10 to 1 by **glial cells** (glia, from Greek for "glue"), which support, nourish, and protect neurons — forming myelin, clearing waste, guiding neural development, and participating in signaling.

## 2. How Neurons Communicate: Action Potentials

**Electrical signaling within a neuron:**

Neurons maintain a **resting potential** of about −70 mV (inside negative relative to outside), maintained by ion pumps. When stimulated sufficiently, the membrane rapidly depolarizes — sodium channels open, Na⁺ rushes in, and the inside becomes briefly positive. This is the **action potential** — an all-or-nothing electrical impulse that travels down the axon.

- **All-or-nothing**: the action potential either fires completely or not at all; stronger stimuli produce more frequent action potentials, not larger ones
- **Refractory period**: brief period after firing during which the neuron cannot fire again (sets a maximum firing rate of ~1,000 times per second)

## 3. Chemical Signaling at the Synapse

The gap between one neuron's axon terminals and the next neuron's dendrites is the **synapse** (synaptic gap). Electrical signals cannot jump this gap — communication is chemical.

**Neurotransmission sequence:**

1. Action potential arrives at the axon terminal
2. **Neurotransmitters** (chemical messengers) are released from **vesicles** into the synaptic cleft
3. Neurotransmitters diffuse across the cleft and bind to **receptor proteins** on the postsynaptic cell's dendrites
4. Binding either excites the next neuron (makes it more likely to fire) or inhibits it (makes it less likely to fire)
5. Neurotransmitters are removed from the synapse by **reuptake** (absorbed back into the presynaptic neuron), **enzymatic degradation**, or diffusion

**Key neurotransmitters:**

- **Dopamine**: movement, reward, motivation, pleasure. Deficit → Parkinson's disease; dysregulation → schizophrenia; excess stimulation → addiction
- **Serotonin**: mood, sleep, appetite. Low serotonin associated with depression. SSRIs block reuptake, keeping serotonin available longer.
- **Norepinephrine**: alertness, arousal, stress response. Involved in "fight or flight." Also implicated in depression.
- **GABA** (gamma-aminobutyric acid): the main **inhibitory** neurotransmitter — reduces neural activity. Benzodiazepines (anti-anxiety drugs) enhance GABA's effects.
- **Glutamate**: the main **excitatory** neurotransmitter — increases neural activity. Involved in learning and memory.
- **Acetylcholine (ACh)**: involved in movement (neuromuscular junction), attention, memory, sleep. Alzheimer's disease involves deterioration of acetylcholine-producing neurons.
- **Endorphins**: the brain's natural opioids; reduce pain and produce euphoria; released during exercise ("runner's high")

## 4. The Brain: Organization and Structure

The brain is organized hierarchically, with older, more primitive structures at the core and newer, more sophisticated structures at the surface.

### The Brainstem

The most ancient structure, continuous with the spinal cord. Controls basic life-support functions: breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, and sleep-wake cycles. Major components: **medulla oblongata** (autonomic functions), **pons** (relays signals, involved in sleep), **reticular formation** (arousal, attention, consciousness).

### The Cerebellum

Located at the back of the brain. Coordinates **smooth motor movement**, balance, and procedural learning (riding a bike, playing an instrument). Damage produces ataxia — uncoordinated, jerky movements.

### The Limbic System

A set of structures involved in **emotion, memory, and motivation** — often called the "emotional brain":

- **Amygdala**: threat detection, fear responses, emotional memory. The alarm system. Hyperactive in PTSD.
- **Hippocampus**: critical for forming new explicit (declarative) memories; also involved in spatial navigation. Patient H.M., who had his hippocampi removed for epilepsy, could not form new long-term memories.
- **Hypothalamus**: regulates basic drives — hunger, thirst, body temperature, sexual behavior, circadian rhythms. Interfaces between the nervous system and the endocrine system by controlling the pituitary gland.
- **Thalamus**: the brain's sensory relay station — routes incoming sensory information (except smell) to the appropriate cortical area.

### The Cerebral Cortex

The outermost layer — the wrinkled gray matter that gives humans our cognitive capacities. Divided into two **hemispheres** (left and right) connected by the **corpus callosum**. Each hemisphere has four **lobes**:

- **Frontal lobe**: executive functions (planning, decision-making, impulse control), personality, voluntary movement (**motor cortex**), language production (**Broca's area** in the left hemisphere). Largest contributor to what makes us human.
- **Parietal lobe**: processes somatosensory information (touch, temperature, pain, body position — **somatosensory cortex**); spatial processing.
- **Temporal lobe**: processes auditory information, language comprehension (**Wernicke's area** in the left hemisphere), face recognition, memory consolidation (through the hippocampus beneath it).
- **Occipital lobe**: processes visual information. Primary visual cortex at the back of the head.

## 5. Neural Plasticity

The brain is not fixed after childhood. **Neural plasticity** — the brain's ability to change in response to experience — continues throughout life:

- **Synaptic plasticity**: synaptic connections strengthen (long-term potentiation, LTP) or weaken (long-term depression) with use
- **Neurogenesis**: new neurons are generated in certain brain regions (especially the hippocampus) throughout adulthood
- **Cortical remapping**: the cortex reorganizes in response to injury or learning. Blind individuals show expanded auditory cortex; taxi drivers show enlarged hippocampal regions; amputees may experience phantom limb sensations as the cortex reorganizes

---

## Summary

Neurons communicate through action potentials (electrical) within the cell and neurotransmitters (chemical) across synapses. Key neurotransmitters — dopamine, serotonin, GABA, glutamate — mediate mood, movement, learning, and more. The brain is organized from the brainstem (basic life functions) through the limbic system (emotion and memory) to the cerebral cortex (cognition, language, perception). The four cortical lobes have specialized functions. Neural plasticity allows the brain to change throughout life in response to experience.
