The brain is the most complex structure in the known universe — three pounds of tissue containing roughly 86 billion neurons forming trillions of synaptic connections. Different regions of the brain are specialized for different functions, and understanding this organization helps explain everything from why a stroke in one location affects speech but not movement, to why certain drugs produce euphoria, to why damage to the frontal lobe changes personality.

---

## 1. How We Study the Brain

Our understanding of brain function has been revolutionized by technology:

- **Lesion studies**: examining patients with brain damage to infer the function of the damaged region. Patient H.M. (bilateral hippocampal removal) revealed the hippocampus's role in memory. Phineas Gage's frontal lobe damage revealed personality and impulse control functions.
- **Electroencephalography (EEG)**: records electrical activity across the scalp; excellent temporal resolution (millisecond timescale); poor spatial resolution. Shows rhythmic patterns (alpha, beta, theta, delta waves) correlated with different mental states.
- **Computed Tomography (CT/CAT scan)**: X-ray cross-sections showing brain structure; good for detecting tumors, hemorrhage, or structural abnormalities.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)**: uses magnetic fields and radio waves to produce detailed structural images of brain tissue without radiation.
- **Functional MRI (fMRI)**: measures blood flow changes as a proxy for neural activity; shows which regions are active during specific tasks. Good spatial resolution; poor temporal resolution. Has transformed cognitive neuroscience.
- **Positron Emission Tomography (PET)**: tracks radioactively tagged glucose or neurotransmitter analogs; shows metabolic activity and neurotransmitter binding sites.
- **Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS)**: uses magnetic fields to temporarily disrupt activity in a specific brain region, allowing researchers to infer its function.

## 2. The Brainstem

The **brainstem** connects the spinal cord to the rest of the brain and controls the body's most fundamental functions — those that must operate even during unconsciousness:

- **Medulla oblongata**: regulates heart rate, breathing, blood pressure, and reflexes like vomiting and sneezing. Damage here is often fatal.
- **Pons**: "bridge" connecting different brain regions. Involved in sleep, dreaming (REM sleep), facial sensation and movement, and relaying signals.
- **Reticular formation / Reticular Activating System (RAS)**: runs through the brainstem; crucial for arousal, alertness, and consciousness. Anesthetics work partly by suppressing the RAS.

---

## 3. The Cerebellum

The **cerebellum** ("little brain") sits behind and below the cerebral hemispheres, containing more neurons than the entire rest of the brain.

**Functions:**
- Fine motor coordination and smooth, accurate movement
- Balance and posture
- Procedural learning (motor skills like riding a bike)
- Increasingly recognized to play roles in attention, language, and emotion

**Damage effects:** **ataxia** — loss of coordination; staggering gait, jerky movements, difficulty with precise actions. A cerebellar drunk walk looks similar to cerebellar damage.

---

## 4. The Diencephalon: Thalamus and Hypothalamus

### Thalamus

The **thalamus** is the brain's central relay station, a paired structure at the top of the brainstem. All sensory information except smell passes through the thalamus before being routed to the appropriate cortical areas. It also relays information between cortical regions and plays a role in consciousness and alertness.

### Hypothalamus

Despite being the size of a peanut, the **hypothalamus** controls the most basic aspects of survival behavior:
- **Homeostasis**: regulates body temperature, thirst, hunger, circadian rhythms
- **Endocrine control**: interfaces between the nervous and endocrine systems via the **pituitary gland** — the master endocrine gland, which releases hormones that regulate growth, stress response, reproduction, and more
- **Autonomic nervous system**: coordinates fight-or-flight (sympathetic) and rest-and-digest (parasympathetic) responses
- **Reward**: the hypothalamus contains reward circuits involving dopamine; stimulation here produces intense pleasure

## 5. The Limbic System

The **limbic system** is a set of structures encircling the brainstem, sometimes called the "emotional brain":

### Hippocampus

**Function:** essential for forming new **declarative memories** (facts and events) and spatial navigation.

**Evidence:** Patient H.M. had both hippocampi removed in 1953 to treat severe epilepsy. He could not form new long-term memories — he could learn a maze (procedural memory, stored elsewhere) but could not remember having learned it. He met his doctors hundreds of times but never recognized them.

The hippocampus is especially vulnerable to stress hormones (cortisol) — chronic stress can damage it, contributing to memory problems.

### Amygdala

**Function:** the brain's alarm system. Detects and responds to threats, particularly fear-provoking stimuli. Processes emotional memories — especially negative ones. Contributes to fear conditioning.

**Evidence:** bilateral amygdala damage produces the **Klüver-Bucy syndrome** — inability to recognize threatening stimuli; inappropriate fearlessness; hypersexuality. The amygdala is hyperactive in PTSD.

Fear responses can be conditioned very rapidly and persist long after the danger is gone — adaptive when survival matters; problematic in PTSD and phobias.

### Cingulate Cortex

Wraps around the corpus callosum. Involved in emotional processing, error detection, pain perception, and regulating attention. The **anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)** is often called the "conflict monitor" — active when you make an error or when competing responses conflict.

## 6. The Cerebral Cortex

The **cerebral cortex** — the folded outer layer of the brain — is the seat of higher cognitive functions. Its folds (**gyri** = ridges, **sulci** = grooves) dramatically increase surface area.

**Two hemispheres** connected by the **corpus callosum** (a thick band of ~250 million axons). Each hemisphere processes the opposite side of the body.

**Lateralization**: the two hemispheres have somewhat different specializations:
- **Left hemisphere**: language production and comprehension (in most right-handed people), analytical reasoning, detail-focused processing
- **Right hemisphere**: spatial processing, holistic pattern recognition, emotional processing of faces, prosody (emotional tone of speech)

### Frontal Lobe

**Largest lobe; the most distinctly human:**

- **Primary motor cortex** (precentral gyrus): controls voluntary movement; each body region is mapped (the "motor homunculus" — a distorted map with large hands and face representing regions with fine motor control)
- **Premotor cortex**: planning and sequencing movements
- **Prefrontal cortex (PFC)**: executive functions — planning, decision-making, working memory, impulse control, social behavior, personality. The PFC is the last brain region to fully mature (~25 years old).
- **Broca's area** (left inferior frontal gyrus): language production. Damage → **Broca's aphasia**: halting, effortful speech with preserved comprehension.

**Phineas Gage** (1848): a railroad worker survived a tamping iron through his frontal lobe. His intellectual abilities were preserved but his personality was transformed — he became impulsive, profane, and socially inappropriate. First evidence that the frontal lobe underlies personality and social behavior.

### Parietal Lobe

- **Primary somatosensory cortex** (postcentral gyrus): processes touch, temperature, pain, body position (**proprioception**). Also mapped as a sensory homunculus.
- **Posterior parietal cortex**: integrates sensory information and guides movement; spatial processing. **Neglect syndrome** (often from right parietal damage): patients ignore the left side of space and their own left body.

### Temporal Lobe

- **Primary auditory cortex**: processes sounds
- **Wernicke's area** (left posterior temporal lobe): language comprehension. Damage → **Wernicke's aphasia**: fluent but meaningless speech; the patient doesn't understand language and produces "word salad."
- **Inferior temporal cortex**: object recognition, face recognition (**fusiform face area**). Damage → **prosopagnosia**: inability to recognize faces (can recognize a face as a face, but can't identify whose).

### Occipital Lobe

- **Primary visual cortex** (V1): processes basic visual features — orientation, motion, spatial frequency. Located at the very back of the brain. Receives input from the opposite visual field.
- **Secondary visual areas**: higher-level processing — color, motion, object recognition, depth

---

## 7. Association Areas

Much of the cortex is **association cortex** — not primary sensory or motor, but integrating information across modalities and supporting complex functions like reasoning, language, attention, and memory. These areas expand dramatically in primates and especially humans.

---

## Summary

Brain function is studied through lesions, EEG, MRI, fMRI, and other technologies. The brainstem controls life-support functions; the cerebellum coordinates movement; the thalamus relays sensory information; the hypothalamus regulates basic drives and the endocrine system. The limbic system (amygdala, hippocampus) processes emotion and memory. The cerebral cortex — divided into frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes — handles perception, movement, language, and executive function. Left and right hemispheres have specialized roles, connected by the corpus callosum.
