Psychology's claim to being a science rests on its methods. Unlike armchair philosophy or clinical intuition, scientific psychology tests claims about behavior and mental processes using systematic observation and rigorous experimental design. Understanding these methods — and their limitations — is essential for evaluating psychological claims critically.

---

## 1. The Scientific Method in Psychology

The process follows the same cycle as any empirical science:

1. **Observation** → notice a phenomenon or pattern
2. **Hypothesis** → propose a testable, falsifiable explanation
3. **Research design** → choose a method to test the hypothesis
4. **Data collection** → gather evidence systematically
5. **Analysis** → apply statistics to evaluate whether results support or contradict the hypothesis
6. **Conclusion** → interpret results, identify limitations, generate new questions

**The goal**: move beyond anecdote and intuition toward conclusions we can trust because the methodology minimizes bias and allows others to replicate the findings.

---

## 2. Research Designs

### Experimental Research

Experiments are the gold standard for establishing **causation**. The researcher:
- Manipulates the **independent variable (IV)**
- Measures the effect on the **dependent variable (DV)**
- Controls all other variables (holds them constant)
- Randomly assigns participants to conditions

**Random assignment** is the key feature that allows causal inference: if participants are randomly assigned to conditions, any differences in outcomes can be attributed to the manipulation rather than pre-existing differences between groups.

**Example:** To test whether exercise reduces anxiety, randomly assign participants to either an exercise program (experimental group) or a waitlist control (control group). Measure anxiety at the end. If the exercise group shows significantly lower anxiety, the manipulation (exercise) is likely the cause.

**Limitation:** many important questions can't be experimentally manipulated for ethical or practical reasons (you can't randomly assign people to abuse, poverty, or trauma).

### Quasi-Experimental Designs

Resemble experiments but lack random assignment. Participants are assigned to conditions based on pre-existing characteristics (gender, diagnosis, age). Useful but limited — without random assignment, pre-existing differences between groups may explain results rather than the variable of interest.

### Correlational Research

Measures the relationship between two variables as they naturally exist, without manipulation. Expressed as a **correlation coefficient** (r) ranging from −1.0 to +1.0:
- **Positive correlation** (r > 0): variables increase together (hours studied, grade)
- **Negative correlation** (r < 0): as one increases, the other decreases (stress, immune function)
- **No correlation** (r ≈ 0): variables are unrelated

**Critical rule:** correlation does not establish causation. Both variables might be caused by a third variable (confound). Ice cream sales correlate with drowning rates — both are caused by hot weather, not each other.

### Descriptive Research

Describes behavior without attempting to explain or manipulate it.

- **Case studies**: in-depth examination of a single individual or small group. Valuable for rare phenomena (Phineas Gage, H.M.); not generalizable.
- **Surveys**: self-report questionnaires administered to larger samples. Efficient; limited by social desirability bias, inaccurate self-knowledge, and question wording.
- **Naturalistic observation**: observing behavior in natural settings without intervening. Ecological validity is high; cannot control extraneous variables; observer effect.

### Longitudinal vs. Cross-Sectional Designs

- **Longitudinal**: follows the same participants over time. Shows developmental change within individuals. Expensive, time-consuming, subject to attrition.
- **Cross-sectional**: compares different age groups at the same point in time. Faster and cheaper; confounded by **cohort effects** (differences due to birth year, not age).

## 3. Measurement Issues

### Operational Definitions

Every variable must be defined in specific, measurable terms — an **operational definition**. "Aggression" must be defined as something countable (e.g., number of punches thrown, decibels of noise delivered). Without operational definitions, research cannot be replicated.

### Reliability and Validity

- **Reliability**: consistency of measurement. A reliable measure gives the same result under the same conditions. Internal consistency, test-retest reliability, inter-rater reliability.
- **Validity**: does the measure actually measure what it claims to? A reliable measure can be systematically wrong (consistently measuring the wrong thing).

### Sampling

Research participants should represent the population of interest. Most psychology research has historically used **WEIRD** (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) samples — often college students — limiting generalizability. This is a recognized problem in the field.

## 4. Statistics in Psychology

### Descriptive Statistics

Summarize data:
- **Mean, median, mode**: central tendency
- **Standard deviation**: spread around the mean
- **Frequency distributions** and visual graphs

### Inferential Statistics

Determine whether findings generalize beyond the sample:
- **Null hypothesis significance testing (NHST)**: tests whether observed results are likely to occur by chance if there's no real effect
- **p-value**: probability of obtaining results at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. Conventionally, p < .05 is considered statistically significant.
- **Effect size**: how large is the effect? Statistical significance doesn't indicate practical importance. A tiny effect can be statistically significant with a large enough sample.
- **Confidence intervals**: range within which the true population parameter likely falls

## 5. Ethics in Psychological Research

The history of psychology includes ethically troubling studies. Today, research must follow strict ethical guidelines:

- **Informed consent**: participants must be told what the study involves before agreeing to participate
- **Debriefing**: after a study involving deception, participants are fully informed of the true purpose
- **Confidentiality**: data is kept private and participants' identities protected
- **Minimizing harm**: no unnecessary physical or psychological risk
- **Voluntary participation**: right to withdraw at any time without penalty

All institutional research must be reviewed by an **Institutional Review Board (IRB)** that evaluates ethical standards before a study begins.

**Landmark ethical cases:**
- **Milgram's obedience studies** (1961): participants believed they were administering potentially lethal shocks; caused significant psychological distress; would not pass modern IRB review
- **Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment** (1971): participants assigned roles as "guards" or "prisoners" rapidly exhibited disturbing behavior; ended early; also would not pass modern review

---

## Summary

Psychology uses the scientific method to test claims about behavior and mental processes. Experimental designs with random assignment establish causation; correlational designs reveal relationships without establishing causality. Descriptive methods document phenomena. Measurement requires operational definitions, reliability, and validity. Statistics help determine whether findings reflect real effects or chance. Ethical standards — informed consent, debriefing, confidentiality, minimizing harm — protect participants and are enforced through institutional review processes.
