Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behavior. It investigates how we think, feel, perceive, learn, remember, decide, and relate to each other — and what happens when these processes go wrong. It occupies a unique position among the sciences, bridging the biological study of the brain and the social study of human culture and behavior.

---

## 1. Defining Psychology

The word psychology comes from the Greek *psyche* (mind or soul) and *logos* (study). The modern definition emphasizes two elements:

- **Mind**: internal mental processes — thoughts, feelings, perceptions, memories, dreams
- **Behavior**: observable actions — what people do and say

Psychology is a science: it uses systematic empirical methods to generate and test hypotheses. It is not merely introspection, self-help advice, or common sense — it subjects human experience to the same rigorous inquiry applied to any other natural phenomenon.

---

## 2. What Psychologists Study

Psychology encompasses an enormous range of topics:

- **Biological bases of behavior**: how genetics, brain structure, hormones, and the nervous system shape behavior
- **Sensation and perception**: how we detect and interpret sensory information
- **Learning**: how experience changes behavior (conditioning, habituation, observational learning)
- **Memory**: how information is encoded, stored, and retrieved
- **Cognition**: thinking, reasoning, problem-solving, decision-making, language
- **Developmental psychology**: how people change across the lifespan
- **Motivation and emotion**: what drives behavior and how feelings arise and influence us
- **Personality**: stable patterns of thought, feeling, and behavior that characterize individuals
- **Social psychology**: how people influence and are influenced by each other
- **Abnormal psychology**: psychological disorders — their nature, causes, and treatments
- **Health psychology**: the relationship between psychological factors and physical health

---

## 3. Psychology's Goals

Psychology aims to:

1. **Describe** behavior — what is happening (observation, measurement)
2. **Explain** behavior — why it happens (identifying causes)
3. **Predict** behavior — what will happen under given conditions
4. **Influence** behavior — apply knowledge to improve lives (therapy, training, policy)

These four goals reflect psychology's dual nature as both a basic science (seeking knowledge for its own sake) and an applied science (solving real-world problems).

---

## 4. Major Subfields

Psychology is not monolithic — it has dozens of specialized branches:

- **Clinical psychology**: assessment and treatment of psychological disorders
- **Counseling psychology**: helping people cope with life challenges (not necessarily disorders)
- **Cognitive psychology**: mental processes — memory, attention, language, problem-solving
- **Neuropsychology**: relationship between brain and behavior, often studying patients with brain injuries
- **Developmental psychology**: psychological change across the lifespan
- **Social psychology**: social influence, attitudes, group behavior
- **Industrial-organizational (I-O) psychology**: applying psychology to workplace settings
- **Educational psychology**: learning, motivation, and instructional methods
- **Forensic psychology**: psychology in legal and criminal justice contexts
- **Health psychology**: how psychological factors affect physical health and illness

---

## 5. Psychology vs. Psychiatry

These terms are often confused:

- **Psychologist**: typically holds a doctoral degree (Ph.D. or Psy.D.) in psychology; trained in research and psychological assessment; in most U.S. states, cannot prescribe medication (some exceptions exist)
- **Psychiatrist**: holds an M.D. (medical degree) and completed a psychiatric residency; can prescribe medication; often focuses on biological treatments (pharmacotherapy)

Both treat psychological disorders, but from different frameworks. A person with severe depression might see both a psychiatrist (for medication) and a psychologist or therapist (for talk therapy).

---

## 6. Why Study Psychology?

Understanding psychology matters far beyond the clinic:

- **Self-knowledge**: understanding your own cognition, biases, and emotional patterns
- **Relationships**: insight into how people communicate, form attachments, influence each other, and resolve conflict
- **Decision-making**: awareness of cognitive biases that distort judgment
- **Health**: understanding the psychological dimensions of physical health and illness
- **Society**: informing education, criminal justice, public health, organizational management, and policy

Psychology research has challenged many "common sense" beliefs — demonstrating, for example, that eyewitness testimony is unreliable, that people readily obey authority figures even to harmful ends, and that unconscious processes strongly influence behavior.

---

## Summary

Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behavior — encompassing everything from neural activity to social dynamics. Its goals are to describe, explain, predict, and change behavior. It encompasses dozens of subfields from clinical practice to cognitive science to social psychology. Unlike casual folk psychology, the discipline rigorously tests hypotheses using empirical methods. Understanding psychology provides insight into how we think, feel, relate, and make decisions — individually and collectively.
