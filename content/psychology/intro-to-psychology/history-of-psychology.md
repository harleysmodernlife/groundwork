Psychology as a formal science is relatively young — founded in 1879 — but its intellectual roots stretch back to ancient Greek philosophy. The history of psychology is a story of competing visions of what the mind is, what methods are appropriate for studying it, and what it means to explain human behavior.

---

## 1. Philosophical Roots

Before psychology became a science, questions about the mind were the province of philosophy.

**René Descartes** (1596–1650) proposed **substance dualism** — mind and body are fundamentally different substances. The body is a physical machine; the mind is a non-physical substance. This framing haunted psychology for centuries: how can the non-physical mind influence the physical body?

**John Locke** (1632–1704) argued that the mind begins as a *tabula rasa* (blank slate) — all knowledge comes from sensory experience. This **empiricist** tradition later influenced behaviorism.

**René Descartes' opponent**: **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** argued for **innate ideas** — some knowledge is built in. This **nativist** tradition later influenced cognitive psychology and linguistics (Chomsky's universal grammar).

---

## 2. The Birth of Scientific Psychology

**Wilhelm Wundt** (1832–1920) is widely considered the father of scientific psychology. In 1879, he established the **first psychology laboratory** in Leipzig, Germany — a landmark that marks psychology's birth as an independent science separate from philosophy and physiology.

Wundt's approach was **structuralism** — breaking conscious experience into its basic elements through **introspection**: trained observers carefully reporting their mental experiences in response to stimuli. His student Edward Titchener brought structuralism to the United States.

**William James** (1842–1910) founded the American tradition of psychology. His approach, **functionalism**, rejected the search for mental elements and focused instead on the *purpose* of mental processes — how they help organisms adapt and survive. His 1890 textbook *Principles of Psychology* remained influential for decades. James also developed the **James-Lange theory of emotion**: we don't tremble because we're afraid; we're afraid because we tremble — emotion is the perception of physical states.

## 3. Major Schools of Thought

### Psychoanalysis

**Sigmund Freud** (1856–1939) proposed that behavior is driven largely by **unconscious** processes — desires, conflicts, and memories outside of conscious awareness. His theory of **psychoanalysis** emphasized:
- The **unconscious**: the iceberg beneath conscious awareness
- **Defense mechanisms**: strategies the ego uses to protect against anxiety (repression, denial, projection, rationalization)
- **Psychosexual development**: personality is shaped by how we navigate stages of early development
- **Psychoanalytic therapy**: bringing unconscious conflicts into consciousness through free association and dream analysis

Freud's ideas were enormously influential but are now viewed critically — many are not scientifically testable, and his specific claims (the Oedipal complex, penis envy) have not held up to empirical scrutiny. His broader contributions — the importance of early experience, unconscious processes, and psychological defenses — remain influential in modified form.

### Behaviorism

**John B. Watson** (1878–1958) argued that psychology should study only what is directly observable: **behavior**. The mind, consciousness, and introspection were off-limits — they couldn't be measured. Learning, he argued, is all conditioning.

**B.F. Skinner** (1904–1990) extended behaviorism with **operant conditioning** — behavior is shaped by its consequences (reinforcement and punishment). Skinner believed all behavior, including language, could be explained by reinforcement histories.

Behaviorism dominated American psychology from ~1920–1960. Its insistence on rigorous, measurable methods strengthened psychology as a science. Its dismissal of mental processes was eventually overturned by the cognitive revolution.

### Humanistic Psychology

**Abraham Maslow** (1908–1970) and **Carl Rogers** (1902–1987) reacted against both behaviorism (which treated people like pigeons) and psychoanalysis (which focused on pathology). They emphasized:
- Human potential and growth
- **Self-actualization**: the drive to realize one's fullest potential
- The importance of **subjective experience**
- **Positive regard**: accepting people unconditionally

Humanistic psychology contributed to **client-centered therapy** and influenced the positive psychology movement.

### The Cognitive Revolution

By the 1950s and 60s, the computer provided a new metaphor for the mind — an information-processing system. **Cognitive psychology** re-legitimized the study of mental processes: attention, memory, language, problem-solving, and decision-making. Key figures: **George Miller** (memory limits), **Noam Chomsky** (language acquisition), **Jean Piaget** (cognitive development in children), and **Herbert Simon** (problem-solving).

---

## 4. Modern Psychology

Contemporary psychology is pluralistic — multiple approaches coexist and are often combined:

- **Neuroscience and biological psychology**: brain imaging, genetics, pharmacology
- **Cognitive science**: the interdisciplinary study of mind and intelligence
- **Evolutionary psychology**: how evolutionary pressures shaped psychological mechanisms
- **Cultural psychology**: how culture shapes mind and behavior
- **Positive psychology**: studying what enables people and communities to flourish (Seligman, Csikszentmihalyi)
- **Social-cognitive approaches**: how thoughts, social context, and behavior interact

The **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)** provides standardized diagnostic categories — a product of the empirical turn in abnormal psychology.

---

## Summary

Psychology evolved from Greek philosophy through Descartes' dualism and Locke's empiricism. Wundt's 1879 laboratory marked its birth as a science. Major schools — structuralism, functionalism, psychoanalysis, behaviorism, humanistic psychology, and cognitive psychology — each contributed insights and sparked reactions. Modern psychology is pluralistic, combining biological, cognitive, social, cultural, and evolutionary perspectives. Understanding this history reveals why psychology looks the way it does — a product of both scientific progress and recurring debates about what the mind is and how to study it.
