Are we born or made? The debate over whether genes or environment shape behavior is one of psychology's oldest questions — and also one of its most misframed. The answer is almost always: both, interacting in complex ways. Behavioral genetics studies how genetic variation contributes to differences in behavior, while molecular genetics begins to identify which specific genes are involved.

---

## 1. Genes and the Genome

**Genes** are sequences of DNA that encode proteins — the building blocks of cells and the catalysts of biochemical reactions. The human genome contains approximately 20,000–25,000 genes, located on 23 pairs of chromosomes. Each pair contains one chromosome from each parent.

**Alleles** are variant forms of a gene. Having different alleles at a given location contributes to individual differences in physical and psychological traits.

However, most behaviorally relevant genes don't have simple, one-to-one effects. Behavior is **polygenic** — influenced by many genes, each contributing a small effect. Complex traits like intelligence, personality, and depression involve hundreds or thousands of variants, each with tiny individual effects.

## 2. Methods in Behavioral Genetics

### Twin Studies

**Identical (monozygotic, MZ) twins** develop from a single fertilized egg and share 100% of their genetic material. **Fraternal (dizygotic, DZ) twins** develop from two separate eggs and share on average 50% of their genes — like any two siblings.

**Key logic:** if a trait is more similar in MZ than DZ twins, genetics is implicated (since MZ twins share more genes). The difference in correlation between MZ and DZ twins estimates **heritability** — the proportion of population variance in a trait attributable to genetic variance.

**Heritability estimates** (approximate):
- Intelligence: ~50–80% (varies by age; heritability increases with age as genetic influences accumulate)
- Personality traits: ~40–60%
- Schizophrenia: ~80% (high heritability, but not 100% — environment clearly also matters)
- Depression: ~40%

**Critical clarification:** heritability does NOT mean:
- "Fixed" or "unchangeable by environment"
- That genes "cause" the behavior directly
- That a trait is mostly genetic at the individual level (heritability is a statement about *population variance*, not about individuals)

### Adoption Studies

Compare adopted individuals to their biological parents (shared genes, different environment) and adoptive parents (shared environment, different genes). If adopted children resemble biological parents more than adoptive parents for a trait, genetics is indicated.

**Example:** adoption studies of alcoholism show that sons of alcoholic biological fathers are 3–4× more likely to develop alcoholism even when raised by non-alcoholic adoptive parents — suggesting genetic vulnerability.

### Family Studies

Relatives share genes proportionally to their genetic relationship (parents/siblings = 50%, grandparents/aunts/uncles = 25%, cousins = 12.5%). If a trait runs in families, it may be genetic — but families also share environments, so family studies alone can't disentangle genes from shared environment.

## 3. Gene-Environment Interaction

Genes and environment are not independent — they interact in complex ways.

### Reaction Range

The same genotype can produce different phenotypes depending on environment — and different genotypes respond differently to the same environment. The **reaction range** is the range of possible phenotypes a given genotype can produce.

**Example:** children with high genetic potential for intelligence reach that potential only in enriched environments; children from the same genetic background in impoverished environments show significantly lower scores.

### Gene-Environment Correlation (rGE)

Genes influence the environments people find themselves in:
- **Passive rGE**: parents provide both genes and environment (musically talented parents give children their genes and a music-rich home)
- **Evocative rGE**: people's genetically influenced traits evoke responses from others (an easy-going infant elicits more positive parenting than an irritable one)
- **Active rGE**: people seek environments that fit their genotypes ("niche-picking") — a genetically athletic child gravitates toward sports

### Gene-Environment Interaction (G×E)

The effect of a gene depends on the environment, and the effect of the environment depends on the genotype.

**Classic example (Caspi et al., 2003):** a variant of the monoamine oxidase A (MAOA) gene interacts with childhood maltreatment. Boys with the low-activity MAOA variant who were maltreated had much higher rates of adult antisocial behavior than maltreated boys with the high-activity variant — or non-maltreated boys with either variant. Neither the gene alone nor the maltreatment alone predicted antisocial behavior as powerfully as the combination.

## 4. Epigenetics

**Epigenetics** refers to changes in gene expression that don't alter the DNA sequence itself — mechanisms that turn genes "on" or "off." Environmental experiences — stress, nutrition, trauma — can produce epigenetic modifications (such as DNA methylation) that alter which genes are expressed.

Critically, some epigenetic changes can be transmitted across generations — meaning the environment experienced by parents or grandparents can influence the biology of their descendants.

**Example:** children and grandchildren of Holocaust survivors show altered cortisol regulation, consistent with epigenetic transmission of stress responses.

---

## 5. GWAS and Molecular Genetics

**Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS)** scan hundreds of thousands of locations in the genome across large samples, looking for **single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)** — single-base DNA differences — associated with specific traits or disorders.

Results: for most behavioral traits, GWAS finds hundreds or thousands of variants each with tiny effects. No single "gene for" intelligence, depression, or schizophrenia exists — instead, polygenic scores (aggregating effects of many variants) explain modest proportions of population variance.

**Polygenic scores** can predict school achievement, risk for depression, or cognitive ability — but at the population level, not for individuals, and always in combination with environmental factors.

---

## 6. Heritability Is Not Destiny

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a genetic disorder in which the body can't metabolize phenylalanine, leading to intellectual disability. Heritability is 100% — if you have the gene mutation, you will develop PKU. Yet a simple environmental intervention (phenylalanine-free diet from infancy) prevents intellectual disability entirely.

This example illustrates a critical principle: **high heritability doesn't mean unmodifiable**. Knowing a trait has a genetic component tells us very little about the effectiveness of environmental interventions. Environment can override, modulate, or compensate for genetic tendencies.

---

## Summary

Genes influence behavior through complex, polygenic pathways — no behavior is caused by a single gene. Twin, adoption, and family studies estimate **heritability** — the proportion of population variance explained by genetic variance. Most behavioral traits show moderate to high heritability while remaining substantially influenced by environment. Genes and environment interact: genes influence the environments people experience, and environments influence how genes are expressed (epigenetics). GWAS reveals that behavioral traits involve hundreds of variants with tiny individual effects. Heritability is not destiny — environmental interventions can profoundly modify genetically influenced traits.
