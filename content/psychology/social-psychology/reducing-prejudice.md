If prejudice emerges from cognitive, emotional, and structural sources, then reducing it requires interventions at each level. Social psychologists have studied this question for decades. Some strategies are well-supported; others are popular but ineffective or even counterproductive. Understanding the evidence matters.

---

## 1. Contact Theory

Gordon Allport's **Contact Hypothesis** (1954) proposed that prejudice between groups can be reduced through direct contact — but only under specific conditions. Contact alone is insufficient; the quality of contact determines whether it helps or hurts.

### Allport's Conditions for Effective Contact

1. **Equal status**: Both groups must meet as equals, not in hierarchical roles (e.g., employer/employee)
2. **Common goals**: Groups must work together toward shared objectives, not compete
3. **Cooperation**: Achieving the goals requires interdependence — groups need each other
4. **Institutional support**: Authority figures (teachers, employers, governments) endorse and support the contact

When these conditions are met, contact reduces prejudice reliably. Meta-analyses covering hundreds of studies confirm that intergroup contact with Allport's conditions reduces prejudice across many group types — racial, ethnic, religious, and disability-based.

### The Jigsaw Classroom

Elliot Aronson developed the **jigsaw classroom** as a direct application of contact theory. Students are placed in small, diverse groups. Each student is given a unique piece of information that other group members need to complete a project — creating genuine interdependence.

Research shows jigsaw classrooms reduce racial prejudice, increase liking across groups, and improve academic performance — especially for minority students. It's one of the best-supported interventions in applied social psychology.

## 2. Perspective-Taking and Empathy

**Perspective-taking** — deliberately imagining another person's inner experience — reduces automatic prejudice and increases helping behavior across group lines. It works by:
- Reducing perceptions of out-group homogeneity ("they're all the same")
- Activating empathic concern, which competes with dehumanization
- Producing more individualized, less stereotyped impressions

Perspective-taking is most effective when it's active and imaginative, not just intellectual. "Imagine being in their situation" outperforms "consider their perspective abstractly."

**Empathy induction** — through storytelling, direct interaction, and media — can extend this effect at scale. Research shows exposure to humanizing narratives about stigmatized groups reduces prejudice toward those groups.

---

## 3. Cognitive Approaches

### Individuation

Stereotypes apply to groups, not individuals. **Individuation** — attending to a person's specific characteristics rather than their group membership — counteracts stereotyping. It requires:
- Motivation (wanting to see someone as an individual)
- Cognitive capacity (stereotyping increases under cognitive load or stress)
- Information (enough personal information to distinguish the individual from the group)

### Counter-Stereotypical Exemplars

Repeated exposure to individuals who violate group stereotypes (e.g., highly competent members of a stigmatized group) can weaken those stereotypes over time — but only if the exemplar is seen as representative of the group, not as an exception.

### Implicit Bias Training

Many organizations now offer **implicit bias training** to address unconscious prejudice. The evidence for its effectiveness is mixed. While training can increase awareness of bias, awareness alone doesn't reliably translate into changed behavior. More effective are structural changes — blind hiring, structured interviews, and decision checklists — that reduce opportunities for bias to operate rather than trying to eliminate the bias itself.

---

## 4. Structural and Institutional Approaches

Individual attitude change is important but insufficient if institutions continue to produce discriminatory outcomes.

**Structural interventions** include:
- **Blind auditions/applications**: Removing identifying information from resumes, auditions, or submissions reduces bias in selection decisions
- **Diverse representation**: Increasing the presence of out-group members in organizations normalizes contact and can shift norms
- **Anti-discrimination laws**: Legal enforcement changes behavior even without changing attitudes, and behavioral compliance sometimes leads to attitude change over time
- **Affirmative action and targeted programs**: Structural approaches to counteract systemic disadvantage remain politically contested but have documented effects on representation

---

## 5. What Doesn't Work (or Can Backfire)

Not all anti-prejudice interventions are effective:

- **Diversity awareness training focused on blame or guilt** can produce reactance — increasing defensiveness and resistance rather than openness
- **Mandatory diversity training** shows weaker effects than voluntary training and can produce backlash
- **Simply telling people their biases** without giving them tools to address them may increase anxiety without changing behavior
- **Token representation** — superficial inclusion without structural change — can reinforce stereotypes if tokens are seen as unqualified or exceptional

---

## 6. Long-Term Change

Large-scale reductions in prejudice over time — as measured by opinion surveys — do occur, suggesting that social norms and cultural context powerfully shape prejudice. In the United States, explicit racial prejudice has declined substantially since the mid-20th century, though implicit biases and systemic inequalities persist.

Social norms matter: when prejudice is publicly unacceptable, people express it less. This isn't merely hiding — public non-expression can gradually shift private attitudes, especially for younger generations absorbing anti-prejudice norms as default.

---

## Summary

Reducing prejudice requires interventions at cognitive, emotional, and structural levels. Contact theory — under conditions of equal status, common goals, cooperation, and institutional support — is one of the most effective individual-level approaches. Perspective-taking, individuation, and exposure to counter-stereotypical exemplars also help. Structural approaches like blind hiring and anti-discrimination policies reduce discriminatory outcomes independent of attitude change. Some popular interventions (mandatory training, blame-focused awareness programs) can backfire, underscoring the importance of evidence-based approaches.
