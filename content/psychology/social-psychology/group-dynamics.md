Groups are more than the sum of their members. When people come together, something changes — decision-making shifts, performance changes, and new patterns of influence emerge. Social psychologists have studied these dynamics to understand how groups shape individual behavior and how groups themselves function (and malfunction).

---

## 1. Social Facilitation and Social Loafing

### Social Facilitation

When others are watching, how do we perform? Early research by Norman Triplett (1898) found cyclists rode faster when racing alongside others than alone — one of psychology's first published experiments.

Robert Zajonc explained this with a unifying principle: the presence of others increases **arousal**, which strengthens the emission of the **dominant response** — whatever behavior is most practiced or automatic for that task.

- If the task is **easy or well-practiced**, the dominant response is correct — performance *improves* with an audience.
- If the task is **difficult or novel**, the dominant response is often incorrect — performance *worsens* with an audience.

This explains why expert athletes perform better before crowds while novices choke — and why studying for a new topic is harder in public spaces.

### Social Loafing

When people contribute to a **collective effort** — where individual contributions are pooled — they often put in less effort than when working alone. This is **social loafing**.

Bibb Latané demonstrated this by having participants shout or clap either alone or in groups. Noise per person dropped as group size increased, even when people believed their individual contribution was lost in the group output.

Social loafing is reduced when:
- Individual contributions are identifiable
- The task is meaningful and challenging
- Group members feel accountable to each other
- The group is cohesive and members care about the outcome

## 2. Group Polarization

Groups don't just average out individual opinions — they tend to push them further. **Group polarization** is the finding that group discussion makes initial tendencies more extreme.

If group members initially lean toward a risky decision, post-discussion they become riskier. If they lean cautious, they become more cautious. The direction shifts; the extremity amplifies.

### Why Does This Happen?

**Persuasive arguments account**: Discussion introduces more arguments in the direction of the initial lean — because most members share that lean. Hearing new arguments shifts individuals further in that direction.

**Social comparison**: People want to appear at least as committed as the group average. When they discover that others hold similar views, they shift their expressed position further to maintain distinctiveness.

Group polarization helps explain how online communities develop extreme views, why jury deliberations often amplify initial majority sentiments, and how political movements radicalize.

## 3. Groupthink

**Groupthink**, identified by Irving Janis (1972), is a mode of thinking that prioritizes group cohesion and unanimity over accurate, realistic appraisal of alternatives. It tends to occur in highly cohesive, isolated groups under pressure.

### Symptoms of Groupthink

- **Illusion of invulnerability**: Excessive optimism and risk-taking
- **Collective rationalization**: Dismissing warnings that contradict group assumptions
- **Belief in moral superiority**: Assuming the group's decisions are inherently right
- **Stereotyping of out-groups**: Viewing opponents as weak or evil
- **Self-censorship**: Members suppress doubts to avoid rocking the boat
- **Illusion of unanimity**: Silence is interpreted as agreement
- **Mindguards**: Members actively protect the group from contradictory information

Janis applied this framework to policy disasters like the Bay of Pigs invasion, where a cohesive White House advisory group made catastrophically bad decisions partly because no one challenged the prevailing view.

### Preventing Groupthink

- Assign a **devil's advocate** — someone whose explicit role is to challenge the group's assumptions
- Encourage the leader to withhold their own opinion initially
- Invite outside experts
- Hold **second-chance meetings** where members reconsider final decisions
- Use structured processes (anonymous voting, written dissent channels)

## 4. Leadership

Not all group members influence equally. Research on leadership has identified two major orientations:

- **Task-oriented leadership**: Focuses on defining roles, setting goals, and ensuring completion. Effective when tasks are clear and groups need structure.
- **Relationship-oriented leadership**: Focuses on building cohesion, supporting members, and managing interpersonal dynamics. Effective when morale or cooperation is the central challenge.

Effective leaders often adapt — neither style alone is universally superior. Context matters.

## 5. Deindividuation

In large groups — especially anonymous ones — individuals can lose their sense of personal identity and responsibility. This state, called **deindividuation**, is associated with decreased self-monitoring and increased impulsive or antisocial behavior.

Philip Zimbardo demonstrated this in a lab study where participants wore hoods and believed they were anonymous — they delivered stronger shocks than identified participants. Deindividuation helps explain crowd violence, online mob behavior, and looting during riots.

The mechanism isn't the loss of all self — it's a shift from private, internal standards to group norms. If the group norm is aggressive, deindividuation makes people more aggressive. If the norm is generous (e.g., an anonymous charity event), it can increase prosocial behavior.

---

## 6. Social Networks and Group Structure

Groups are not just collections of individuals — they are structured by relationships. Network research shows that:
- **Centrality** determines influence: people with more connections, or connections that bridge otherwise separate clusters, shape group norms and information flow more than peripheral members
- **Homophily** ("birds of a feather flock together") produces groups that are less diverse and more vulnerable to groupthink
- Information and misinformation spread faster through dense networks with high connectivity

---

## Summary

Groups change behavior in consistent and predictable ways. Social facilitation improves easy-task performance but impairs difficult-task performance. Social loafing reduces effort in collective settings. Group polarization amplifies initial tendencies through discussion. Groupthink produces poor decisions in cohesive, isolated groups. Deindividuation reduces personal accountability. Understanding these dynamics is essential for designing effective teams, preventing collective failures, and recognizing when group processes are distorting judgment.
