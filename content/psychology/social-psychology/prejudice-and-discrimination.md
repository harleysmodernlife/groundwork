Prejudice and discrimination are among the most consequential topics in social psychology. They shape individual lives, drive intergroup conflict, and underlie systemic inequalities. Understanding where they come from — cognitively, emotionally, and socially — is the first step toward addressing them.

---

## 1. Defining the Terms

These three concepts are related but distinct:

- **Stereotype**: A cognitive generalization about the characteristics of a group. Stereotypes can be positive or negative, and everyone uses them — they're how the mind categorizes a complex social world. The problem arises when they're inaccurate, overgeneralized, or applied rigidly.

- **Prejudice**: An *attitude* — typically negative — toward members of a group based solely on their group membership. Prejudice involves both beliefs and emotions (hostility, contempt, disgust, fear).

- **Discrimination**: *Behavior* — treating people differently based on group membership. Discrimination is the behavioral expression of prejudice, but the two don't always align. Someone can hold prejudiced beliefs without acting on them (due to social pressure), or discriminate without conscious prejudice (due to habit or policy).

---

## 2. Cognitive Origins: Why the Mind Stereotypes

The human brain is a pattern-recognition machine. **Categorization** — grouping things — is fundamental to efficient thinking. We couldn't function if we had to evaluate every object and person from scratch.

The problem is that social categorization triggers automatic processes:
- **In-group/out-group distinction**: People spontaneously categorize others as "us" (in-group) or "them" (out-group)
- **Out-group homogeneity effect**: We see out-group members as more similar to each other than in-group members ("they all seem the same")
- **Illusory correlation**: We overestimate the association between rare groups and rare behaviors, even with no actual correlation — leading to exaggerated stereotypes about minority groups

These are not moral failures; they are cognitive defaults. But defaults can be overridden with awareness and effort.

### Implicit Bias

Many biases operate unconsciously — below the level of deliberate thought. The **Implicit Association Test (IAT)** measures how quickly people associate concepts (e.g., "black" or "white" + "good" or "bad"). Most people, including members of stigmatized groups, show some degree of implicit bias favoring socially dominant groups.

Implicit biases influence behavior in subtle but measurable ways — hiring decisions, medical recommendations, police stops — even among people who consciously endorse egalitarian values.

## 3. Emotional and Motivational Origins

Not all prejudice is coldly cognitive. Prejudice often serves emotional functions:

### Scapegoating

When people experience frustration and can't identify or confront the real cause, they displace aggression onto convenient, powerless targets. Scapegoating becomes more common under economic stress and social threat.

### Terror Management Theory

Awareness of our own mortality generates existential anxiety. People manage this anxiety partly by clinging to their cultural worldview and derogating groups that threaten or challenge it. This helps explain why prejudice intensifies after reminders of death.

### Social Identity Theory

Henri Tajfel's **Social Identity Theory** (1979) showed that simply being categorized into groups — even randomly — leads people to favor their in-group and discriminate against out-groups. This occurs even with minimal groups (assigned arbitrarily, with no history or real difference).

Why? Because group membership is part of self-concept. Enhancing the in-group's status enhances one's own sense of self-worth. This is one reason prejudice doesn't require individual malice — it can emerge from the ordinary human need to feel good about one's groups.

---

## 4. Systemic Discrimination

Individual prejudice is not the only driver of unequal outcomes. **Systemic discrimination** — discrimination embedded in policies, institutions, and social structures — can perpetuate inequality even when most individuals hold no conscious bias.

Examples:
- Hiring algorithms trained on historically biased data
- School funding tied to property taxes in unequally valued neighborhoods
- Drug sentencing laws that produce racially disparate outcomes despite race-neutral wording

Understanding systemic discrimination requires looking beyond individual attitudes to the structures that distribute opportunity and harm.

---

## 5. Costs of Prejudice and Discrimination

The harms fall on multiple levels:

**To targets:**
- Psychological harm: Chronic stress, reduced self-esteem, anxiety, depression
- **Stereotype threat**: The fear of confirming negative stereotypes about one's group impairs performance on relevant tasks — students underperform on tests when their stigmatized identity is made salient
- Economic harm: Reduced employment, income, housing, and healthcare access

**To perpetrators:**
- Reduced accuracy in social judgment
- Missed opportunities through homogeneous social networks
- Moral inconsistency and cognitive dissonance

**To society:**
- Wasted human potential, reduced social trust, increased conflict

---

## Summary

Prejudice is a negative attitude toward group members; discrimination is differential behavior; stereotypes are cognitive generalizations. Prejudice emerges from cognitive shortcuts (categorization, illusory correlation), emotional needs (scapegoating, social identity protection), and social structures. Implicit biases operate unconsciously and influence real-world decisions. Systemic discrimination persists independent of individual prejudice. The harms — including stereotype threat, economic inequality, and psychological damage — fall disproportionately on stigmatized groups.
