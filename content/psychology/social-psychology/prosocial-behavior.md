Prosocial behavior refers to voluntary actions intended to benefit others — helping, sharing, cooperating, donating, volunteering. Understanding why people help (and why they often don't) has been one of social psychology's most practically important questions.

---

## 1. The Bystander Effect

In 1964, a woman named Kitty Genovese was murdered outside her New York City apartment. Media reports (later contested) claimed 38 neighbors witnessed the attack and none called police. Psychologists John Darley and Bibb Latané set out to understand how such a thing could happen.

Their research revealed the **bystander effect**: people are *less* likely to help in an emergency when other bystanders are present.

### Why? Two Key Processes

**Diffusion of responsibility**: When others are present, personal responsibility to act feels diluted. Everyone assumes someone else will handle it. The more bystanders, the less each individual feels obligated.

**Pluralistic ignorance**: In ambiguous situations, people look to others to gauge whether the situation is really an emergency. If no one else is reacting (perhaps because they're all doing the same thing), individuals conclude nothing serious is happening — and fail to act.

### The Evidence

Darley and Latané (1968) staged an "emergency" — a confederate appeared to have a seizure — over an intercom. When participants believed they were the only witness, 85% helped within 90 seconds. When they believed six others were also listening, only 31% helped.

The bystander effect is one of the most replicated findings in social psychology and has been documented in emergency rooms, online contexts, and real-world crime data.

## 2. Why People Help: Motivations

### Empathy-Altruism Hypothesis

C. Daniel Batson proposed that **genuine empathic concern** produces **true altruism** — helping motivated by the other person's welfare, not one's own. When we feel empathy for someone, we're moved to help even at personal cost and even when we could easily escape the situation.

This contrasts with **egoistic helping** — helping to reduce your own distress at seeing someone suffer, or to gain social approval.

### Kin Selection and Reciprocal Altruism

Evolutionary perspectives offer complementary explanations:

- **Kin selection**: We are biologically disposed to help genetic relatives more than strangers — helping them increases the chances that shared genes are passed on.
- **Reciprocal altruism**: Helping non-relatives makes evolutionary sense if they're likely to help us in return. This explains cooperation in stable communities where people interact repeatedly.

### Social Norms

Two norms powerfully shape helping:
- **Norm of reciprocity**: Help those who have helped you
- **Norm of social responsibility**: Help those who depend on you and cannot help themselves

These norms vary across cultures and are activated more strongly in some situations than others.

## 3. Factors That Increase or Decrease Helping

**Increases helping:**
- Fewer bystanders (or clear assignment of responsibility: "You in the red shirt, call 911")
- Similarity to the victim (shared identity, appearance, background)
- Good mood (people in positive emotional states help more)
- Low cost or high safety of helping
- Witnessing others help first
- Urban vs. rural environment (mixed findings, but small communities show stronger norms of helping)

**Decreases helping:**
- Many bystanders (diffusion of responsibility)
- Time pressure (seminary students on their way to give a talk on the Good Samaritan walked past a person in need)
- Ambiguity (is this actually an emergency?)
- Fear of embarrassment or doing it wrong
- Perceived undeserved need (less help for those seen as responsible for their situation)

---

## 4. The Good Samaritan Study

Darley and Batson (1973) conducted a revealing study. Seminary students were told to walk across campus to give a talk — half on the Good Samaritan parable, half on career opportunities. Half were told they were late; half had plenty of time.

Along the way, a person (confederate) was slumped in a doorway, apparently in distress.

Results: Being in a hurry was by far the strongest predictor of whether students helped — more important than the content of their upcoming talk. Students about to discuss compassion helped barely more than those discussing careers. **Situational factors overwhelmed dispositional ones.**

---

## 5. Encouraging Prosocial Behavior

Research suggests several approaches to increase helping:

- **Reduce ambiguity**: Make the need clear ("this person needs help")
- **Assign responsibility directly**: Point to a specific person to act
- **Model helping**: Seeing others help increases helping norms
- **Teach the bystander effect**: Simply knowing about it reduces its power — people who learn about diffusion of responsibility are more likely to intervene
- **Make moral identity salient**: Reminding people of their own values increases prosocial action
- **Reduce barriers**: Simplify how people can help (easy donation buttons, specific volunteer slots)

---

## Summary

Prosocial behavior — helping, cooperating, sharing — is influenced by both internal motivations (empathy, social norms, reciprocity) and situational factors. The bystander effect demonstrates that the presence of others paradoxically reduces helping through diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance. Time pressure, ambiguity, and perceived cost reduce helping; clear need, direct assignment of responsibility, and witnessing others help all increase it. Understanding these dynamics allows for deliberate design of situations that make helping more likely.
