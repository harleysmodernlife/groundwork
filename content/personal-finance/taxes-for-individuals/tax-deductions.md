## Common Deductions Most People Miss

Most people take the standard deduction and call it done — which is often the right move. But there are also **above-the-line deductions** (also called adjustments to income) that reduce your AGI regardless of whether you itemize. These are separate from itemized deductions and frequently overlooked.

---

## Above-the-Line Deductions (Available to Everyone)

These reduce your adjusted gross income (AGI) before you even choose between standard and itemized deductions. Lower AGI can also help you qualify for other credits and deductions that phase out at higher incomes.

**Student loan interest:** You can deduct up to $2,500 of interest paid on qualified student loans, as long as your income is below the phase-out threshold ($80,000 single / $165,000 married filing jointly in 2024). You don't have to itemize to claim this.

**Self-employed health insurance:** If you're self-employed and paid for your own health, dental, or long-term care insurance premiums, you can deduct 100% of those premiums. This is a significant deduction that many freelancers don't know about.

**Self-employment tax deduction:** You pay both employer and employee shares of self-employment tax. The IRS lets you deduct half of your self-employment tax — reducing the income that's taxed.

**Contributions to a traditional IRA:** If you contributed to a traditional IRA, you may be able to deduct those contributions (subject to income limits and whether you have a workplace retirement plan). Up to $7,000/year ($8,000 if 50+) in 2024.

**Contributions to an HSA (Health Savings Account):** If you have a high-deductible health plan and contributed to an HSA, those contributions are deductible. Limits are $4,150 (individual) and $8,300 (family) for 2024. The HSA triple tax advantage (deductible contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals for medical expenses) makes it one of the best tax-advantaged accounts available.

**Alimony paid:** For divorces finalized before January 1, 2019, alimony payments are deductible for the payer. Divorces after that date: the 2017 tax law change eliminated this deduction.

**Educator expenses:** Teachers and certain other educators can deduct up to $300 of unreimbursed classroom expenses.

**Moving expenses:** Only for active-duty military. The general moving deduction was eliminated by the 2017 tax law for most people.

## Tax Credits (Even Better Than Deductions)

Credits reduce your tax bill dollar-for-dollar. They're more valuable than deductions.

**Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC):** A substantial credit for lower-to-moderate income workers. The amount varies significantly based on income and number of children. The maximum credit for 2024 is $7,830 (with three or more children). Many eligible people don't claim it because they don't know they qualify. Check the IRS EITC assistant at irs.gov.

**Child Tax Credit:** Up to $2,000 per qualifying child under 17. Partially refundable (up to $1,600 per child in 2024).

**Child and Dependent Care Credit:** If you paid for childcare or day camp while you worked (or looked for work), you may be able to claim a credit of 20-35% of qualifying expenses, up to $3,000 for one child or $6,000 for two or more.

**American Opportunity Tax Credit:** Up to $2,500 per eligible student for the first four years of higher education. 40% is refundable (up to $1,000). Requires enrollment at least half-time.

**Lifetime Learning Credit:** Up to $2,000 per return for qualifying education expenses. Less restrictive than the AOTC — no enrollment requirement, no limit on years.

**Retirement Savings Contributions Credit (Saver's Credit):** A credit for lower-income individuals who contribute to a retirement account. Up to $1,000 single / $2,000 married, 10-50% of contributions. Often overlooked.

**Energy efficiency credits:** Credits for installing solar panels, energy-efficient windows, heat pumps, and other improvements. The Inflation Reduction Act expanded these significantly for 2023 and beyond.

## Self-Employed Business Deductions (Schedule C)

If you're self-employed, your deductible business expenses reduce the income on which you pay both income tax and self-employment tax — making them particularly valuable.

Any legitimate, ordinary, and necessary business expense is deductible:
- Office supplies, software, equipment (computers, cameras, tools)
- Home office (dedicated space used exclusively for business)
- Business vehicle mileage ($0.67/mile in 2024) or actual vehicle costs
- Business travel and accommodation
- Professional development (courses, books, conferences)
- Professional services (accountant, attorney, business services)
- Advertising and marketing
- Business insurance premiums
- Retirement contributions (SEP-IRA, Solo 401(k), SIMPLE IRA)

Keep receipts. Document the business purpose of each expense. The IRS expects documentation if audited.
