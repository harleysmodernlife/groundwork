## When to Get a Tax Professional

Most people can file their taxes correctly using free or low-cost software. But there are situations where professional help — from an enrolled agent, CPA, or tax attorney — is worth the cost and potentially saves you far more than it costs.

---

## When You Probably Don't Need a Professional

If your tax situation is straightforward, you can file confidently with software (or free options):

- W-2 income from one or two employers
- Standard deduction
- Basic credits (child tax credit, EITC)
- Simple savings interest or small dividend income
- No life events that significantly changed your tax situation

The complexity most people feel about taxes often comes from unfamiliarity with the process, not genuine complexity in their returns. VITA volunteers, IRS Free File, and free software are genuinely adequate for most of these situations.

---

## When Professional Help Is Worth It

**Self-employment with meaningful income:** Schedule C deductions require judgment calls. Which expenses qualify? How to handle a home office? Vehicle deductions? Depreciation? A CPA who works with self-employed clients routinely can identify deductions you'd miss and ensure you're not claiming things incorrectly.

**Multiple business interests or a growing small business:** S-corporations, partnerships, and multi-member LLCs file separate business returns that interact with your personal return in complex ways. Professional preparation here is standard practice.

**Rental real estate:** Depreciation, passive activity rules, like-kind exchanges (1031 exchanges), and the interaction of rental income with your overall tax situation have enough complexity to justify professional review.

**Major life events:**
- Marriage or divorce (especially with asset division or alimony)
- Sale of a business
- Receiving a large inheritance (especially from an estate with complex assets)
- Significant equity compensation (stock options, RSUs) from an employer
- Moving internationally or earning income from foreign sources

**IRS notices and audits:** If you receive a letter from the IRS beyond a routine confirmation, respond carefully. If you're being audited, don't navigate it alone — hire a CPA or enrolled agent who handles IRS representation.

**Prior-year unfiled returns:** If you're behind on filing, a professional can help you get current efficiently and handle the IRS interactions involved.

---

## Types of Tax Professionals

**Enrolled Agent (EA):** Licensed by the IRS and authorized to represent taxpayers before the IRS. Specializes in taxation — it's their entire focus. Often less expensive than CPAs for pure tax work.

**Certified Public Accountant (CPA):** A broader credential covering accounting, auditing, and tax. CPAs who specialize in tax are excellent for complex situations and often provide the most comprehensive planning.

**Tax Attorney:** Handles legal aspects of taxation — tax disputes, estate planning, business structuring with significant tax implications. Higher cost; appropriate for specific legal situations.

**Unlicensed preparers:** H&R Block storefront employees, independent "tax professionals" without credentials, etc. Useful for simple returns. For complex situations, verify credentials — anyone can call themselves a tax professional without a license.

---

## How to Find One

**State CPA societies:** Many have "find a CPA" directories. Look for someone who handles individual tax clients (not just corporate/audit work).

**NAEA (National Association of Enrolled Agents):** Find an EA at naea.org.

**Referrals:** Ask people in similar financial situations who they use.

**Questions to ask before engaging:**
- What's your experience with situations like mine? (self-employed, rental properties, whatever applies)
- What do you charge and how is it structured?
- Who specifically will prepare my return?
- How do you handle questions between filings?

---

## What to Bring

If you do work with a professional, have these ready:
- All information returns (W-2s, 1099s, 1098s for mortgage interest, etc.)
- Prior year return
- Social Security numbers for you, spouse, and dependents
- Records of any significant financial events during the year
- Business income and expense records if self-employed

Good records make the process faster and cheaper — most preparers charge by the hour or complexity, and disorganized records add time.

---

## The Cost Perspective

A CPA preparing a straightforward individual return with Schedule C might charge $300-600. A complex return with multiple business entities might cost $1,000-3,000+.

If you're paying $400 for tax preparation and you have a straightforward return, you may be overpaying for something a free tool could handle equally well. If you're paying $600 and the CPA finds a $3,000 deduction you'd have missed, it paid for itself seven times over.

The value of a tax professional is in the complexity they navigate and the things they catch — not in the mechanical task of entering numbers into a form.
