## Tax Forms You'll Actually See

The tax code is complex, but most people's tax situations involve only a handful of forms. Knowing what each one is and where it comes from eliminates the anxiety of getting a form you don't recognize.

---

## Forms That Come to You (Information Returns)

These are sent by employers, banks, and institutions to report income you received. You don't generate these — you receive them, use them to fill out your return, and the IRS receives copies too.

**W-2:** Your employer sends this by January 31. It shows your wages, tips, and other compensation, plus how much was withheld for federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare. If you had multiple employers, you receive a W-2 from each.

**1099-NEC (Nonemployee Compensation):** Sent by clients and platforms if you received $600+ in freelance or contractor payments. If you drove for Uber, sold on Etsy, or did freelance work, you may receive one. Shows your gross 1099 income — you calculate and deduct your business expenses on Schedule C.

**1099-INT (Interest Income):** From banks, reporting interest earned on savings accounts, CDs, etc. You probably won't receive one unless you earned $10 or more in interest in a year.

**1099-DIV (Dividend Income):** From brokerages, reporting dividends received from stocks or funds.

**1099-B (Proceeds from Broker Transactions):** From brokerages, reporting sales of investments. Shows what you sold, what you received, and (often) your cost basis. Used to calculate capital gains and losses.

**1099-G:** Used to report government payments — most commonly unemployment compensation, which is taxable income.

**1099-R:** Reports distributions from pensions, 401(k)s, IRAs, and similar retirement accounts. Withdrawals are generally taxable.

**SSA-1099:** Reports Social Security benefits received. Up to 85% of Social Security benefits may be taxable depending on total income.

**1095-A:** From the Health Insurance Marketplace (healthcare.gov). Required to reconcile any premium tax credits you received. If you received marketplace subsidies, you need this form to file correctly.

## Forms You Generate (Your Tax Return)

**Form 1040:** The main individual income tax return. All income, adjustments, deductions, credits, and final tax liability flow through here. Most people's entire return starts and ends on the 1040 and its associated schedules.

**Schedule 1:** Reports additional income (alimony received, business income, rental income) and above-the-line deductions (student loan interest, self-employed health insurance, IRA contributions). Attached to the 1040 when needed.

**Schedule A:** Reports itemized deductions (mortgage interest, SALT, charitable contributions, medical expenses). Only needed if you itemize rather than taking the standard deduction.

**Schedule B:** Reports interest and dividend income in detail. Needed if you received over $1,500 in interest or dividends.

**Schedule C:** Reports profit or loss from a self-employed business. If you have any 1099-NEC income, you'll use Schedule C to report income, deduct expenses, and calculate net profit. Schedule SE then calculates self-employment tax.

**Schedule D:** Reports capital gains and losses from investment sales. Uses information from your 1099-B.

**Schedule SE:** Calculates self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) for self-employed people.

## Important Deadlines

**January 31:** Employers and most payers must send W-2s and 1099s to recipients.

**April 15:** The standard federal tax filing and payment deadline. If you owe taxes, payment is due April 15 regardless of extension.

**October 15:** Deadline with a tax extension. Filing an extension gives you 6 more months to file — but not to pay. If you expect to owe, you should estimate and pay by April 15 to avoid penalties and interest.

**Quarterly estimated tax deadlines:** April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15 — for self-employed individuals and others with non-W-2 income.

## When You're Missing Forms

If you haven't received a form by early February, contact the issuer. If an employer has gone out of business or isn't cooperating, the IRS can help obtain records.

You can also access wage and income transcripts from prior years directly through IRS.gov (sign into your account). These show what information the IRS already received about your income — useful if forms are missing or you're reconstructing old returns.
