## W-2 vs 1099: Employee vs Self-Employed

How you earn money determines how you're taxed on it. The difference between being a W-2 employee and a 1099 contractor affects how much you owe, when you pay it, what forms you file, and what deductions you can take.

---

## W-2 Employees

When you work for an employer as a regular employee, they withhold taxes from each paycheck and send those taxes to the government on your behalf. At the end of January, they send you a W-2 form showing:
- How much you earned
- How much was withheld for federal income tax
- How much was withheld for Social Security (6.2%)
- How much was withheld for Medicare (1.45%)
- State income tax withheld (if applicable)

The employer also pays a matching 6.2% Social Security and 1.45% Medicare tax out of their own pocket — you never see this, but it's part of your total compensation cost.

**The W-2 advantage:** Taxes are handled automatically. As long as your W-4 withholding is set up correctly, you typically don't owe a surprise bill at tax time (you might get a small refund or owe a small amount depending on your situation).

---

## 1099 Contractors (Self-Employed)

If you work as a freelancer, independent contractor, consultant, gig worker, or run your own business, clients who pay you $600 or more in a year send you (and the IRS) a **1099-NEC form** instead of a W-2.

What's different:

**No withholding.** The full payment hits your account. No taxes are taken out. This feels great until you realize you now owe taxes that were never withheld.

**Self-employment tax.** As a self-employed person, you pay both sides of Social Security and Medicare — the employee share (7.65%) and what would have been the employer share (7.65%). Total: 15.3% on your net self-employment income. You can deduct half of this on your return, but it's still a significant additional burden compared to W-2 employment.

**Quarterly estimated taxes.** Since nothing is being withheld automatically, you're required to pay taxes as you go — in quarterly installments due in April, June, September, and January. If you don't make estimated payments and you owe more than $1,000 at filing, you may face an underpayment penalty.

**Quarterly deadlines (approximate):**
- April 15 — for January through March income
- June 15 — for April through May income
- September 15 — for June through August income
- January 15 — for September through December income

**How much to set aside:** A reasonable starting estimate is 25-30% of net self-employment income for federal taxes (including self-employment tax). Higher earners or those in high-tax states may need more. Lower earners may need less, especially if they qualify for deductions that reduce taxable income.

## Business Deductions for the Self-Employed

The significant upside of 1099 income: you can deduct legitimate business expenses that W-2 employees generally cannot.

Common deductible business expenses for contractors and freelancers:
- Home office (must be used regularly and exclusively for business)
- Business portion of phone and internet
- Equipment (computers, cameras, tools) — can often be deducted in full in year one
- Software and subscriptions used for business
- Mileage for business travel ($0.67/mile in 2024)
- Professional development, courses, books
- Professional services (accountant, attorney)
- Health insurance premiums (self-employed health insurance deduction)
- Retirement contributions (SEP-IRA, Solo 401(k) — very generous limits)

Keeping careful records of all business expenses — receipts, documentation — is essential. The IRS can request them if you're audited.

---

## Mixed Income

Many people have both W-2 and 1099 income — a full-time job plus freelance work, or multiple part-time jobs. This complicates taxes but isn't difficult to handle:

- The W-2 income is reported from your W-2 form
- The 1099 income is reported on Schedule C (or Schedule SE for self-employment tax)
- All income is added together to determine total taxable income

If your 1099 income is significant, you should be making quarterly estimated payments on that portion. Your W-2 withholding covers only the W-2 income.

---

## The Misclassification Issue

Some employers misclassify workers as independent contractors when they should legally be employees. This saves the company money (no payroll taxes, no benefits) but shifts the tax burden to the worker.

If you work for someone who controls how, when, and where you work — and you have only one "client" — you may be an employee, not a contractor. Misclassification can be reported to the IRS (Form SS-8) and may result in the employer owing back taxes and penalties.
