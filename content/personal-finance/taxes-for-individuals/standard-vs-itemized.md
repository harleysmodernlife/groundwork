## Standard Deduction vs Itemizing

One of the most consequential decisions in your tax return is whether to take the standard deduction or itemize. Most people take the standard deduction and never think about it again — which is correct for most people. But understanding what you're choosing and why helps you know when the calculation might be different.

---

## What a Deduction Does

A tax deduction reduces your **taxable income** — the income that's actually taxed. It doesn't reduce your taxes dollar for dollar.

If you're in the 22% bracket and take a $1,000 deduction, you save $220 in taxes (22% × $1,000). The deduction reduces the amount of income that gets taxed, not the tax itself.

This is different from a tax credit, which reduces your tax bill directly, dollar for dollar. Credits are more valuable than equivalent deductions.

---

## The Standard Deduction

The standard deduction is a flat amount that anyone can deduct without documentation or proof. You simply claim it, and that amount is subtracted from your income.

**2024 standard deduction:**
- Single: $14,600
- Married filing jointly: $29,200
- Head of household: $21,900

Additional amounts for age 65+, and for blindness.

The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act roughly doubled the standard deduction, which is why approximately 90% of filers now take it. For most people, the standard deduction is larger than the total of their itemizable deductions.

---

## Itemizing

Instead of the standard deduction, you can itemize — add up specific qualifying expenses and deduct the actual total, if it exceeds the standard deduction.

Common itemized deductions:

**State and local taxes (SALT):** Property taxes plus either state income tax or sales tax — whichever is higher. Currently capped at $10,000/year total.

**Mortgage interest:** Interest paid on your primary and secondary home mortgages, up to a loan limit of $750,000 ($1 million for loans before December 2017).

**Charitable contributions:** Cash donations to qualifying nonprofits. Non-cash donations have specific documentation requirements.

**Medical expenses:** Only the portion exceeding 7.5% of your adjusted gross income. Only matters for people with very high medical costs relative to income.

---

## When Itemizing Makes Sense

You should itemize only if your total itemizable deductions exceed your standard deduction.

**Example: A homeowner in California:**
- Paid $15,000 in mortgage interest
- Paid $10,000 in state and local taxes (SALT, capped at $10,000)
- Made $3,000 in charitable donations
- Total itemizable: $28,000

Their standard deduction as a single filer is $14,600. Their itemized total is $28,000. Itemizing saves taxes on $13,400 more of income — a real benefit.

**Example: A renter:**
- Paid $6,000 in state income tax (SALT)
- Made $500 in charitable donations
- Total itemizable: $6,500

The standard deduction at $14,600 is more than double their itemizable total. Take the standard deduction without hesitation.

**Who typically itemizes:**
- Homeowners with significant mortgage interest
- High-income residents of high-tax states (though the SALT cap limits this)
- People with large charitable contributions

Most renters, most people in their 20s-30s without mortgages, and people in lower-cost states will take the standard deduction.

---

## Both/And: The Bunching Strategy

If your itemizable deductions are close to but below the standard deduction, a technique called "bunching" can help.

Instead of donating to charity every year, you give two or three years' worth of donations in a single year, pushing your itemizable total above the standard deduction for that year. In the off-years, you take the standard deduction.

Example: You normally donate $5,000/year, pay $8,000 in SALT, and have a $14,600 standard deduction. Total itemizable: $13,000 — just under the standard deduction.

If you donate $10,000 (two years at once) in year one: $18,000 itemizable. Take itemized. In year two, donate $0 and take the standard deduction. Over two years, you've deducted more total.

Donor-Advised Funds (DAFs) make this strategy practical — you contribute a lump sum to the DAF in a high-deduction year, get the full charitable deduction immediately, and then direct the grants from the fund to your chosen charities over multiple years.
