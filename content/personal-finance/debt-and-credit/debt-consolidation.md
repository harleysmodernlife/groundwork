## Debt Consolidation: When It Helps and When It Doesn't

Debt consolidation is the process of combining multiple debts into a single debt — typically with a lower interest rate, a single monthly payment, and a defined payoff timeline.

It sounds like an obvious win. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it's a way of rearranging the furniture without fixing the underlying problem. The difference is important.

---

## How It Works

The basic mechanism: you take out a new loan or open a new credit account, use those funds to pay off your existing debts, and then repay the single consolidated debt.

If the new interest rate is meaningfully lower than your current average rate, you save money. If the term is shorter or the payment is more manageable, you may pay off debt faster.

**Common consolidation methods:**

**Personal loan.** You apply for a fixed-rate personal loan from a bank, credit union, or online lender. If approved at a rate below your current credit card rates, you use the loan to pay off the cards. You then repay the personal loan in fixed monthly installments over 2-5 years.

Credit unions often offer better rates than banks for personal loans, especially for borrowers without excellent credit.

**Balance transfer credit card.** Some credit cards offer 0% APR promotional periods — often 12-21 months — for transferred balances. You move your high-interest balances to the new card and pay them down during the promotional window.

If you can pay the balance in full before the promotional period ends, this is an excellent deal. If you can't, the rate reverts to a standard rate (often 20%+) on whatever remains — and some cards backcharge interest retroactively.

Balance transfer cards typically charge a transfer fee of 3-5% of the transferred amount.

**Home equity loan or HELOC.** Homeowners can borrow against their home's equity at rates far lower than credit cards. This can dramatically reduce interest.

The risk: your home is now collateral. If you can't make payments, you can lose the house. Converting unsecured credit card debt (where the worst outcome is credit damage) into secured debt (where the worst outcome is foreclosure) is a serious step. Only appropriate if you're disciplined and the financial math is compelling.

---

## When Consolidation Actually Helps

Consolidation makes genuine sense when:

1. **You can qualify for a meaningfully lower rate.** If your cards are at 22-24% and you can get a personal loan at 10-12%, the math works in your favor.

2. **You have a defined payoff plan.** Consolidation works when it replaces revolving debt with a fixed-term loan that's completely paid off in 3-5 years. It doesn't work as a long-term strategy.

3. **You've addressed the spending habits that created the debt.** This is critical and often overlooked.

---

## The Trap: Consolidating Without Changing Behavior

Here's what frequently happens:

Someone consolidates $15,000 of credit card debt into a personal loan. Their credit card balances are now zero. The cards feel available. Within 12-18 months, they've run the cards back up to $10,000 — while still paying the personal loan. Now they have more debt than they started with.

This pattern is extremely common. Consolidation doesn't fix the spending patterns that created the debt. It just restructures the debt. If the restructuring isn't accompanied by a budget and a genuine commitment to not reaccumulating debt, it often makes things worse.

**The discipline required:** When you consolidate to a personal loan, close the credit cards (or cut them up and lock them away) to remove the temptation. The credit score hit from closing accounts is real but temporary and worth it if you're prone to running them back up.

---

## What to Watch For

**Fees.** Personal loans may have origination fees (1-8% of the loan amount). Balance transfer cards have transfer fees. Calculate the total cost including fees, not just the interest rate.

**Extended terms.** Some consolidation products lower your monthly payment by extending the term — say, from 3 years to 7 years. You pay less per month but more in total. Read the total interest over the life of the loan, not just the monthly payment.

**Predatory consolidation lenders.** Some companies advertise consolidation services and charge high fees, high rates, and long terms. If the APR isn't substantially lower than your current debts, it's not consolidation — it's just debt with more steps.

**Debt settlement companies posing as consolidation services.** These are different things. Debt settlement involves deliberately defaulting while a company "negotiates" — it wrecks your credit and often leaves people worse off.

---

## The Bottom Line

Debt consolidation is a tool. Used correctly — with a lower rate, a fixed payoff timeline, and behavior change — it can save money and simplify a complex debt situation. Used without addressing underlying habits, it often results in more debt than you started with.

Before consolidating, ask: can I qualify for a genuinely lower rate? Do I have a plan to pay this off completely within a few years? Have I made the budget changes that will prevent reaccumulating debt? If the answer to all three is yes, consolidation can be a smart move.
