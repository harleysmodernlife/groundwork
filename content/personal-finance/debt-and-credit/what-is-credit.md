## What Credit Is and Why It Matters

Credit is the ability to borrow money or access goods and services with the agreement that you'll pay later. When a bank gives you a credit card, they're extending you credit. When a landlord agrees to let you live in their apartment and pay rent monthly, that's also a form of credit. When you get a car loan, same thing.

The concept is straightforward. What's less straightforward is how deeply your credit history affects your life — and how little most people understand about it until they're affected by a problem.

---

## Your Credit History

Every time you borrow money and pay it back (or don't), that information is recorded. Credit bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — collect this information from lenders and maintain files on nearly every American adult who has ever borrowed money.

Your **credit report** is the file. It contains:
- Every credit account you've had and whether it was paid on time
- Your current balances and credit limits
- Any accounts sent to collections
- Public records like bankruptcies
- Recent inquiries — who has requested your credit report and when

Your **credit score** is a number calculated from your credit report — a compressed summary of how risky a borrower you appear to be. The most widely used model is the FICO score, which runs from 300 to 850.

---

## Why It Affects More Than Borrowing

Most people understand that credit affects whether they can get a loan and at what interest rate. That's true and important — the difference between a good and mediocre credit score can mean paying tens of thousands of dollars more over the life of a mortgage.

But credit affects other things people don't expect:

**Renting an apartment.** Landlords routinely pull credit reports. A thin credit history or a low score can mean getting turned down for housing or being required to pay a larger deposit.

**Car insurance.** In most states, insurance companies use credit-based insurance scores to set premiums. People with poor credit often pay significantly higher rates for the same coverage.

**Employment.** Some employers — particularly for positions involving financial responsibility — run credit checks. A history of financial problems can affect your chances.

**Utilities and cell phone service.** Utility companies and carriers may require deposits from customers with no or poor credit, or may deny service.

**The cost of debt.** When you do borrow — car loans, personal loans, credit cards — your interest rate is directly tied to your credit score. Someone with a 750 score might get a 6% auto loan. Someone with a 580 score might be offered 18%. On the same car, over the same loan term, that's thousands of dollars of difference.

---

## The Three Bureaus and Your Reports

Because three separate bureaus maintain files, your credit report isn't one thing — it's three potentially different things. Lenders don't all report to all three bureaus, so your TransUnion report may differ from your Equifax report.

You're entitled to a free copy of each report every year from AnnualCreditReport.com — the official, federally mandated site. Many people also get ongoing access through their bank or card issuer.

Reviewing your reports matters because errors are surprisingly common. A debt that was paid off showing as outstanding, an account that isn't yours, an incorrect missed payment — these can drag down your score and cost you money. Errors can be disputed and corrected.

---

## Credit Is a Tool

Used well, credit is a useful tool — it allows you to buy a home, start a business, handle a financial emergency. The interest you pay for that access can be a reasonable cost.

Used poorly, credit is a trap — it lets you spend money you don't have, at interest rates that compound the problem, in ways that damage your financial future for years.

Understanding how credit works is the starting point for using it as a tool rather than falling into the trap.
