## Student Loans

Student loan debt in the US exceeds $1.7 trillion. For many borrowers, it's the largest debt they'll ever carry — larger than a car loan, sometimes larger than a mortgage. Understanding how it works before you take it on, and how to manage it once you have it, matters enormously.

---

## Federal vs Private Loans

Not all student loans are the same. The difference between federal and private loans is significant.

**Federal student loans** come from the government. They have fixed interest rates set by Congress, income-driven repayment options, and various forgiveness programs. They don't require a credit check (except for PLUS loans). Their protections are strong.

**Private student loans** come from banks, credit unions, and lenders like Sallie Mae. Interest rates are variable or fixed based on your credit and market conditions. They have far fewer protections — limited hardship options, no income-driven repayment, no public service forgiveness.

**Rule:** Exhaust all federal loan options before considering private loans. Private loans should be a last resort.

---

## Types of Federal Loans

**Direct Subsidized Loans:** Available to undergrad students with demonstrated financial need. The government pays the interest while you're in school. These are the best deal available.

**Direct Unsubsidized Loans:** Available regardless of financial need. Interest accrues while you're in school — and that interest gets added to your principal balance (capitalized) when repayment begins, increasing the amount you owe.

**PLUS Loans:** For graduate students or parents of undergrads. Higher interest rate, credit check required. Less favorable than subsidized or unsubsidized loans.

## The Math That Matters Before You Borrow

The single most important question: **will the degree increase my earning enough to justify the debt?**

A rule of thumb: total student loan debt at graduation should be less than your expected first-year salary. If you expect to earn $45,000 starting out, borrowing more than $45,000 makes repayment genuinely difficult.

When debt significantly exceeds annual income, standard 10-year repayment becomes painful or impossible, you end up in income-driven repayment for 20+ years, and forgiveness (if it comes at all) may be taxable.

Borrowing $80,000 for a degree with a $35,000 starting salary is a financial decision that will constrain your life for years. Not impossible to recover from — but the constraint is real.

---

## Repayment Plans

**Standard 10-Year Repayment:** Fixed payments over 10 years. You pay the most per month but the least total — less time for interest to accumulate.

**Income-Driven Repayment (IDR) Plans:** Payments capped as a percentage of your discretionary income (varies by plan: 5%, 10%, or 15%). After 20-25 years of qualifying payments, remaining balance is forgiven. The forgiven amount may be taxable.

The current IDR options include SAVE (Saving on a Valuable Education), PAYE, IBR, and ICR. The specifics change — check StudentAid.gov for current information.

**Graduated Repayment:** Payments start lower and increase over time. Useful if you expect income to grow. You pay more in total than the standard plan.

**Extended Repayment:** Longer term, lower monthly payments, significantly more total interest paid.

---

## Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF)

If you work full-time for a government employer or qualifying nonprofit, and make 10 years (120 payments) of qualifying payments under an IDR plan, remaining federal loan balances are forgiven — tax-free.

This is genuinely valuable for people in those fields: teachers, government workers, nonprofit employees, many healthcare workers.

The requirements are specific. Look them up carefully at StudentAid.gov. Many people have been denied for administrative reasons. If you're pursuing PSLF, submit the Employment Certification Form annually, not just at the end.

## What to Do If You Have Loans Now

**Know what you have.** Log into StudentAid.gov to see all your federal loans — the types, amounts, interest rates, and current servicer.

**Understand your servicer.** Your loan servicer is the company that handles billing and payment. Contact them if you have questions about repayment. Servicers have changed over the years, so confirm who yours is.

**Enroll in autopay.** Most servicers offer a 0.25% interest rate reduction for autopay enrollment. Small, but real.

**Pay more than required when you can.** Specify that extra payments should go to principal on your highest-rate loan.

**Don't ignore it.** Federal loans go into default after 270 days of non-payment. Default has serious consequences: credit score damage, wage garnishment, tax refund seizure. If you're struggling to make payments, contact your servicer about income-driven options before defaulting. They have hardship programs — but you have to ask.
