## Avalanche vs Snowball: Two Debt Payoff Methods

If you're carrying multiple debts — credit cards, car loan, medical bills, student loans — you face a choice: which one do you pay down first?

There are two popular systematic approaches: the avalanche and the snowball. They lead to the same destination but by different routes, and the right choice depends on what motivates you.

---

## The Setup

For both methods, you do the same thing each month:
1. Pay the minimum on every debt
2. Put all remaining available money toward one designated "target" debt

The difference is which debt gets the extra money.

---

## The Debt Avalanche

**How it works:** Target the debt with the highest interest rate first. When that's paid off, redirect all that payment to the debt with the next highest rate.

**Why it works:** High-interest debt costs the most money while it exists. By eliminating it first, you minimize the total interest paid over the payoff period.

**Example:**
- Card A: $3,000 balance, 24% APR, $60 minimum
- Card B: $1,200 balance, 18% APR, $30 minimum
- Card C: $800 balance, 14% APR, $20 minimum

Avalanche targets Card A first. While it takes longer to eliminate that first debt (it has the highest balance), you spend less money overall.

**The math advantage:** If you have $500/month total for debt payments, avalanche will save you the most money in interest compared to any other approach. The difference can be hundreds or thousands of dollars depending on the balances and rates.

**The drawback:** If your highest-rate debt is also your largest balance, it can take a long time to pay off the first debt. Some people find the lack of early progress demotivating.

---

## The Debt Snowball

**How it works:** Target the debt with the smallest balance first, regardless of interest rate. When it's paid off, move to the next smallest.

**Why it works:** Paying off a debt entirely provides a psychological win. It reduces the number of debts and payments you're managing. For many people, this builds momentum.

**Using the same example:**
- Card A: $3,000 balance, 24% APR
- Card B: $1,200 balance, 18% APR
- Card C: $800 balance, 14% APR

Snowball targets Card C first ($800). It's paid off relatively quickly. Then Card B. By the time you reach Card A, you're applying the full $500 (or more) to the balance.

**The psychological advantage:** Early wins matter. For many people, eliminating a debt entirely — even a small one — creates a sense of progress that makes the whole project feel more achievable. People who get discouraged and give up don't pay off any debt; the snowball keeps people going.

**The drawback:** You'll pay more in total interest than the avalanche, because you're not prioritizing the most expensive debt.

---

## Which One Is Right for You?

The avalanche is mathematically optimal. If you're disciplined, can tolerate a slow start, and are motivated by saving money rather than by visible milestones — use the avalanche.

The snowball is psychologically optimized. If you've started debt payoff plans before and abandoned them, if early wins keep you on track, if you're motivated by crossing things off a list — use the snowball.

**The best method is the one you'll stick to.** A snowball plan you actually follow beats an avalanche plan you abandon.

Some people use a hybrid: start with the snowball to clear a couple of small debts and build momentum, then switch to avalanche for the remaining larger balances.

---

## What Both Methods Require

For either approach to work, you need extra money to apply to the target debt. If you're currently spending more than you earn, there's nothing to allocate — the debt grows regardless of strategy.

The prerequisite is a budget where spending is below income, with a defined amount available for debt payoff beyond minimums. Even $50-100 extra per month makes a real difference over time.

---

## Tracking Progress

List all your debts: balance, minimum payment, and interest rate. Calculate your total minimum payments. Identify how much extra you can apply each month.

Update the list monthly as balances change. Watching balances drop — especially when a debt reaches zero — is motivating in a way that's easy to underestimate until you experience it.

---

## The Finish Line

Regardless of which method you use, every debt you pay off reduces your minimum monthly obligations — which means more money is available for the remaining debts, savings, and eventually your own goals.

The math compounds in your favor as you go. The first debt is the hardest. The last one falls quickly.
