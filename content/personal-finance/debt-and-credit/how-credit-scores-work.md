## How Credit Scores Are Calculated

Your FICO credit score — the number that lenders use most often — is calculated from five categories of information in your credit report. Each category is weighted differently. Understanding the weights tells you exactly where to focus if you want to improve your score.

---

## The Five Factors

**Payment History — 35%**

This is the largest factor. It answers one question: do you pay your bills on time?

Every on-time payment is a positive mark. Every late payment — technically 30 days or more past due — is a negative mark. The later a payment is, the worse the damage: a 90-day late payment hurts more than a 30-day late payment. A payment that goes to collections is worse still. Bankruptcy is the worst.

Late payments stay on your credit report for seven years. They have less impact over time, but they don't disappear quickly.

**The takeaway:** Pay every bill on time, every month, without exception. If you're struggling, call the lender and ask about options. A missed payment can hurt your score more than almost anything else.

**Amounts Owed (Credit Utilization) — 30%**

This measures how much of your available credit you're using. The key metric is your **credit utilization ratio** — your total credit card balances divided by your total credit limits.

If you have two cards with $5,000 limits each ($10,000 total) and a combined balance of $3,000, your utilization is 30%.

The general guidance: keep utilization below 30%. Below 10% is better. Maxed-out cards — or even cards over 50% utilization — meaningfully hurt your score even if you pay every payment on time.

**The takeaway:** Keep balances low relative to limits. Paying down a high balance, or asking for a credit limit increase, can improve your score quickly.

**Length of Credit History — 15%**

This measures how long you've had credit. It looks at:
- Age of your oldest account
- Age of your newest account
- Average age of all accounts

Older is better. This is why closing an old credit card can hurt your score — you're removing your oldest account and lowering your average account age.

**The takeaway:** Don't close old accounts just because you're not using them. Leave them open (assuming no annual fee or other reason to close).

**Credit Mix — 10%**

Lenders like to see that you can manage different types of credit responsibly: credit cards (revolving credit), auto loans, student loans, mortgages (installment credit). Having a mix is slightly better than only having one type.

**The takeaway:** Don't open accounts you don't need just to diversify. This factor matters, but not enough to justify taking on debt unnecessarily.

**New Credit — 10%**

Every time you apply for credit, the lender pulls your credit report — a "hard inquiry." Hard inquiries stay on your report for two years and can temporarily lower your score by a few points.

Opening several new accounts in a short time is a red flag. It suggests financial stress or reckless borrowing.

**The takeaway:** Don't apply for credit you don't need. Rate shopping for a mortgage or auto loan within a short window (typically 14-45 days) counts as one inquiry, not many.

## Score Ranges and What They Mean

- **800-850 (Exceptional):** The best rates available. Lenders compete for your business.
- **740-799 (Very Good):** Excellent rates. Very few doors closed.
- **670-739 (Good):** Near or above average. Approved for most things, may not get the absolute best rate.
- **580-669 (Fair):** Will be approved for some products but at higher rates. Some landlords and lenders will turn you down.
- **Below 580 (Poor):** Many lenders won't work with you. Those who do charge high rates. Deposits required for many services.

---

## What Doesn't Affect Your Score

- Income — your score measures borrowing behavior, not earnings
- Checking your own score (a "soft inquiry") — checking doesn't hurt you
- Demographic information — age, race, gender, marital status, address
- Debit card usage — debit is your own money; it doesn't involve borrowing

---

## How Quickly Can It Change?

Paying off a large balance can improve your score noticeably within 30-60 days — that's how quickly updated information reaches the bureaus.

Removing a late payment, on the other hand, takes seven years unless there's an error. A bankruptcy takes ten years.

The good news: even a poor score can improve substantially with consistent on-time payments and lower utilization over 12-24 months. Credit scores are not permanent sentences — they respond to behavior.
