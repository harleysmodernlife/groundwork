## Credit Cards: How the Trap Works

Credit cards aren't inherently bad. Used right, they're convenient, they build credit history, and they offer rewards. Used wrong, they're one of the most effective wealth-destroying tools in existence.

Understanding how the trap works is the only way to avoid it.

---

## How Credit Cards Make Money

Credit card companies make money in three primary ways:

**Interchange fees.** Every time you swipe your card, the merchant pays a fee — typically 1.5-3.5% of the transaction. This is built into the prices you pay regardless of how you pay. Card companies and banks split this.

**Annual fees.** Some cards charge $95-550/year just to have the card. Premium rewards cards particularly.

**Interest.** This is the big one. If you don't pay your full balance by the due date, you're charged interest on the remaining balance — often 20-29% APR. This is how credit card companies make most of their profit.

The business model is deliberately designed around the subset of cardholders who carry balances. Rewards programs are funded by interest charges from people who carry debt. When you pay your balance in full every month and collect rewards, you are effectively being subsidized by the cardholders who don't.

---

## The Minimum Payment Trap

When your statement arrives, it shows a minimum payment — usually 1-2% of the balance or $25, whichever is higher.

Paying the minimum is not the same as paying your bill. It's paying just enough to avoid a late fee while the remaining balance accrues interest.

**Example:** $3,000 balance at 22% APR. Minimum payment of ~$60/month.

If you only pay the minimum, it will take over 20 years to pay this off. You'll pay approximately $6,000 in interest — twice the original balance.

Credit card statements are now required by law to show you how long payoff takes at the minimum payment. Read that number. It's usually alarming.

**The rule:** Pay your full balance every month. If you can't, pay as much above the minimum as possible. Paying the minimum is a way of treading water while drowning slowly.

---

## How Interest Is Calculated

Credit card interest doesn't work like annual interest — it compounds daily.

Your APR is divided by 365 to get your daily rate. That rate is applied to your average daily balance each day. If your APR is 22%, your daily rate is approximately 0.060%. On a $2,000 balance, that's $1.20 per day, or about $36/month.

The balance doesn't just accumulate the original charge — it accumulates the interest on the interest. This is compounding working against you.

**The grace period:** If you pay your full balance by the due date, most cards charge no interest at all — your purchases don't accrue interest between the purchase date and the due date. This is why paying in full each month is so important. It's not just about avoiding some interest — it's about paying zero interest.

When you carry a balance, you lose the grace period. Purchases start accruing interest from the day you make them.

## Cash Advances: Worse Than You Think

Taking cash from an ATM with your credit card is a cash advance. They're dramatically more expensive than regular purchases:

- Higher APR (often 24-28% on cards already charging 20%)
- No grace period — interest starts immediately
- A cash advance fee upfront (typically 3-5% of the amount)

A $500 cash advance from a credit card is an extremely expensive $500. Avoid unless it's a genuine emergency with no other option.

---

## What Credit Card Companies Hope You Don't Notice

**Promotional 0% APR periods.** Many cards offer 0% interest for 12-18 months. This can be genuinely useful for a major purchase if — and only if — you pay the full balance before the promotional period ends.

What happens if you don't? In many cases, the interest that would have accrued during the promotional period is charged retroactively. What seemed like 0% financing becomes a large surprise charge.

**Payment allocation.** When you have multiple balances at different rates (a promotional rate and a regular purchase rate, for example), payments are often applied to the lower-rate balance first, leaving the higher-rate balance to compound. Regulations now require payments above the minimum to go to the highest-rate balance, but it's worth knowing how your card handles this.

---

## The Right Way to Use a Credit Card

1. Treat it like a debit card — only charge what you'd buy with cash
2. Pay the full balance every month, every time
3. If you carry a balance, stop using the card and pay it down aggressively
4. Never use the card for cash advances
5. Know your APR and your limit
6. Set up autopay for the full statement balance — not the minimum

A credit card used this way gives you purchase protection, credit score building, and rewards — all for free. A credit card used carelessly is a slow financial emergency.
