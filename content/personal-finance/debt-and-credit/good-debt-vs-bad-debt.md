## Good Debt vs Bad Debt

Not all debt is equally harmful. Some debt is a tool that, used correctly, can build your financial position. Other debt is a trap that costs you money without building anything.

The distinction isn't always clean, but understanding it helps you make better decisions about when borrowing is reasonable and when it isn't.

---

## What Makes Debt "Good"

Good debt typically has these characteristics:

1. **It finances something that retains or grows in value** — or increases your earning power
2. **The interest rate is relatively low**
3. **The terms are predictable and manageable**

The clearest examples:

**A mortgage.** You're borrowing to buy an asset that, historically, appreciates over time. Your monthly payment builds equity. The interest rate on a mortgage is typically the lowest rate available to individuals. And mortgage interest is currently tax-deductible in many situations.

**Student loans (sometimes).** Education that substantially increases your earning capacity can justify the debt. A nursing degree, an engineering degree, a trade certification — if the income bump is real and the debt is proportionate to the expected salary increase, the math can work. The problem is when the debt is disproportionate to the return: $80,000 for a degree with limited job prospects.

**A business loan.** Borrowing to fund a business that generates revenue and profit can be reasonable. The business pays back the loan with the money it makes.

---

## What Makes Debt "Bad"

Bad debt typically has these characteristics:

1. **It finances things that lose value or are consumed** — experiences, consumables, depreciating goods
2. **The interest rate is high**
3. **It's revolving — the balance can persist indefinitely**

The clearest examples:

**Credit card debt carried month to month.** Average credit card interest rates run 20-25%. If you're carrying a $3,000 balance at 22% APR and only paying the minimum, you'll spend years paying it off and pay hundreds or thousands in interest.

**Payday loans.** Often 300-400% APR when expressed annually. Designed to trap.

**Auto loans on depreciating cars.** Cars lose value the moment you drive them off the lot. Financing a $35,000 car at 8% interest because you wanted something nice is expensive — you're paying a premium on a depreciating asset.

**Buy now, pay later for consumables.** Financing a phone, furniture, or clothes at interest means you're still paying for things long after you've used them, grown out of them, or stopped caring about them.

---

## The Complication: Context Matters

Calling debt good or bad in the abstract oversimplifies things. Context matters.

A car loan isn't inherently bad — if you need reliable transportation to get to work, and you can't buy a functional car outright, financing one is sometimes necessary. The question is whether you're borrowing the minimum necessary for a reliable vehicle, or borrowing for a vehicle you can't afford because it's nicer than you need.

Student loans aren't inherently good — the calculation depends on the degree, the school's cost, your expected starting salary, and what you would have earned without the degree.

The right question isn't "is this debt good or bad?" It's: **is the return on this debt greater than its cost?** And: **can I actually handle the payments without derailing other financial goals?**

---

## The Interest Rate Test

A useful shortcut: look at the interest rate.

Below 6%: Usually reasonable. Likely financing something with real value (mortgage, federal student loans).

6-12%: Worth scrutinizing. Is this financing something valuable? Can you pay it off faster than required?

Above 12%: High. You're paying a meaningful premium. Is the underlying purchase necessary or could it wait? Are there better options?

Above 20%: This is expensive debt. Every month it persists, it's costing you real money. Paying it off should be a priority.

---

## The Bottom Line

There's no universally good or bad debt — there's debt that makes financial sense and debt that doesn't. The factors that matter: interest rate, what you're borrowing for, whether the payment fits your budget, and whether you'll carry the balance for longer than makes sense.

Approaching debt deliberately — rather than reactively, in the moment of wanting something — is what separates people who use debt as a tool from those who get trapped by it.
