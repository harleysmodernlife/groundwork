## Reading Your Credit Report

Your credit report is the source document behind your credit score. It contains the actual history of your borrowing — every account, every payment, every problem. The score is just a compressed summary. The report has the details.

You should read yours at least once a year. Errors are common enough that ignoring your report is a real risk.

---

## How to Get It

**AnnualCreditReport.com** is the only officially authorized site for free credit reports under federal law. You're entitled to one free report from each of the three bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — every 12 months.

Since the bureaus maintain separate files, the three reports may differ. Pull all three.

Many banks, credit unions, and credit card issuers also provide free credit score monitoring — often through your account dashboard or app. These are useful for watching your score regularly, but they may show data from only one bureau. They're not a substitute for pulling the full reports from all three.

---

## What's On Your Report

**Personal information:** Your name, current and previous addresses, date of birth, Social Security number, and employment history (as reported by lenders). Errors here are common and worth correcting, though they don't directly affect your score.

**Account information (the most important section):**

For each account — credit card, loan, mortgage — you'll see:
- The lender's name and account number (partially masked)
- Type of account (revolving or installment)
- Date opened and current status (open/closed)
- Credit limit or original loan amount
- Current balance
- Payment history — typically shown month by month, with notations for on-time payments and late payments
- Date of last activity

**Collections:** If a debt was turned over to a collection agency, it appears here. This is separate from the original account and shows the collection agency's name, the original creditor, and the amount owed.

**Public records:** Bankruptcies appear here. Other judgments that were once reported (civil judgments, tax liens) were largely removed from credit reports in 2017-2018 under new standards.

**Hard inquiries:** Every time someone pulled your credit for a lending decision in the last two years. These are separated from soft inquiries (your own checks, pre-approval screenings), which don't appear in a way that affects your score.

---

## Looking for Errors

Common errors on credit reports include:

- Accounts that don't belong to you (mixed files — especially common with common names, or after identity theft)
- Paid accounts still showing a balance
- Late payments reported inaccurately
- Duplicate accounts
- Accounts that should have fallen off (most negatives have a 7-year limit; bankruptcy is 10 years)
- Wrong personal information

Go through each account and verify: Is this account mine? Are the dates correct? Does the payment history match what I know? Is the balance correct?

---

## How to Dispute an Error

If you find an error, you can dispute it directly with the bureau that's reporting it. All three bureaus have online dispute processes, or you can dispute by mail.

When you file a dispute, the bureau is required to investigate — typically within 30 days. They contact the lender, who must verify the information. If the lender can't verify it or confirms the error, the bureau corrects or removes it.

**Tips for disputing:**
- Dispute each bureau separately — a correction with one doesn't automatically carry to the others
- Keep copies of everything: your dispute letter, any documents you submit, confirmation numbers
- If the lender confirms the incorrect information despite evidence it's wrong, you can file a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)

---

## When Negative Information Comes Off

Most negative items have legal time limits after which they must be removed:

- **Late payments:** 7 years from the date of the missed payment
- **Collections:** 7 years from the date of the original delinquency
- **Chapter 7 bankruptcy:** 10 years
- **Chapter 13 bankruptcy:** 7 years
- **Hard inquiries:** 2 years

As time passes, old negative items hurt your score less and less — even before they fall off completely. A late payment from five years ago matters much less than one from six months ago.

---

## Make It a Habit

Pull your reports annually. Stagger them — pull one bureau every four months — so you're effectively checking your credit three times a year.

If you've been the victim of identity theft, or suspect you might be, you can place a free credit freeze with each bureau. A freeze prevents new accounts from being opened in your name without your explicit unfreeze — the strongest available protection.
