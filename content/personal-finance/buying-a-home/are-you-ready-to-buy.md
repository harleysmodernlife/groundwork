## Are You Actually Ready to Buy?

Wanting to buy a home and being financially ready to buy a home are different things. Buying before you're ready — with insufficient savings, the wrong credit, or at the wrong life stage — can create financial stress that lasts years. Going through a checklist of honest questions is more useful than optimism.

---

## The Financial Checklist

**Credit score: Is it competitive?**

Your credit score determines the interest rate you're offered. At a 760+ score, you'll qualify for the best rates. At 680-700, you'll get approved but pay more. Below 620, many conventional loan programs are closed to you, and you'll pay a significant premium.

Check your score before starting seriously. If it's below 720, a year of focused improvement (pay everything on time, reduce credit card balances) can meaningfully improve your rate — potentially saving tens of thousands of dollars over the life of the loan.

**Down payment: Do you have it, plus more?**

The standard recommendation is 20% down to avoid PMI. This is ideal but not always necessary — FHA loans allow 3.5% down, and some conventional loans allow 3-5% down.

Whatever your down payment, you also need:
- **Closing costs:** 2-5% of the purchase price, due at closing, not financeable
- **Reserves:** 2-3 months of housing payments in savings after closing
- **Moving costs and immediate repairs/purchases**

Stretching to buy with exactly enough for the down payment and nothing else is a warning sign. The furnace that fails two months after closing has to be paid for somehow.

**Debt-to-income ratio: Is it manageable?**

Lenders calculate your **DTI** — monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income.

Front-end DTI: housing costs (mortgage, taxes, insurance, HOA) ÷ gross monthly income. Most lenders want this below 28%.

Back-end DTI: all debt payments (housing + car + student loans + credit cards) ÷ gross monthly income. Most lenders want this below 36-43%.

If your back-end DTI would be 50%+ after a mortgage, you're likely stretched too thin — even if a lender approves you. The maximum approved amount is not the right amount to borrow.

**Stable income: Is it reliable?**

If you've been self-employed less than 2 years, recently changed jobs, or have highly variable income, qualifying for a mortgage can be difficult — and taking one on may be premature. Lenders want 2 years of stable employment history.

## The Lifestyle Checklist

**Are you staying? Really?**

Homeownership has a built-in time commitment. The transaction costs of buying and selling make short-term ownership expensive. If there's meaningful uncertainty about where you'll be in 5 years — career changes, relationship changes, geographic uncertainty — that uncertainty is worth weighing.

**Can you handle the responsibility?**

When something breaks, it's yours to fix. No calling the landlord. No splitting the cost. The roof, the HVAC, the plumbing, the appliances — all on you, at full cost, on whatever timeline is appropriate.

This isn't a reason not to buy — but if the idea of managing maintenance is genuinely unpleasant to you, factor that in.

**Are you buying the right-sized home?**

The easiest time to buy too much house is when a lender approves you for more than you should borrow. Buying at the top of your approval amount is usually a mistake. Consider: what's the minimum house that works for your life? A smaller, cheaper house is lower financial risk and lower maintenance burden.

---

## The Market Timing Question

Don't let "what if prices go up" drive a premature purchase. Home prices have gone up over long periods, but:

- Prices can fall (2006-2011 saw a 30%+ national decline)
- You can't know when they'll rise or fall
- Buying a home when you're financially ready is the right call; timing the market isn't the goal

The goal is: buy when you're financially prepared, staying long enough to cover transaction costs, with a home that fits your life and budget. Not: buy before you're ready because prices might go up.

---

## Signs You're Ready

- Credit score 720+
- Down payment saved plus 2-5% for closing costs plus 3+ months of reserves
- Stable employment for 2+ years
- Back-end DTI below 36% with the new mortgage
- Plan to stay 5+ years
- Emergency fund intact after the purchase
- The monthly payment is genuinely affordable, not just technically possible
