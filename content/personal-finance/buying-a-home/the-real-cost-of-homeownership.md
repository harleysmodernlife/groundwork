## The Real Cost of Owning a Home

"Owning is always better than renting" is one of the most repeated pieces of financial advice — and one of the least examined. The reality is more complicated. Understanding the true cost of homeownership is the prerequisite to making a sound decision about whether and when to buy.

---

## The Costs People Expect

**Mortgage payment:** The monthly payment on your home loan. This includes principal (reducing your loan balance) and interest (the cost of borrowing).

**Property taxes:** Paid to the local government, typically 1-2% of home value per year. On a $300,000 home, that's $3,000-6,000/year, or $250-500/month, added to your effective housing cost.

**Homeowners insurance:** Required by your lender. Typically $800-2,000/year depending on the home and location.

**HOA fees:** If you're in a homeowners association (condos, many planned communities), monthly fees of $200-700+ are common. These don't build equity.

---

## The Costs People Underestimate

**Maintenance and repairs:** The standard guideline is 1% of home value per year in maintenance costs, on average. For a $300,000 home, that's $3,000/year, or $250/month.

Some years you spend nothing major. Then the furnace dies ($4,000-8,000), the roof needs replacing ($10,000-20,000), the water heater fails ($1,000-1,500), the foundation needs repair ($5,000-15,000). The 1% rule accounts for the average across years.

Renters have no maintenance cost. This is a real economic advantage of renting that's frequently ignored in the rent vs buy comparison.

**Closing costs when buying:** 2-5% of the purchase price in transaction fees — loan origination fees, appraisal, title insurance, attorney fees, prepaid taxes and insurance. On a $300,000 home, that's $6,000-15,000. You need this in cash on top of the down payment.

**Closing costs when selling:** Real estate commissions alone are typically 5-6% of the sale price (though competition is pushing this lower). On a $350,000 sale, that's $17,500-21,000. Plus attorney fees, title fees, transfer taxes. Total selling costs: often 8-10% of the sale price.

**PMI (Private Mortgage Insurance):** If you put down less than 20%, you pay PMI — typically 0.5-1.5% of the loan balance annually. On a $240,000 loan (with 20% down on a $300,000 home), that's $1,200-3,600/year. PMI disappears once you reach 20% equity, but it can cost thousands before you get there.

**Opportunity cost of the down payment:** Money in a down payment is not invested. If you put $60,000 down, that $60,000 isn't growing in the market. At a 7% historical stock market return, that's $4,200/year in foregone investment returns.

## The Complete Monthly Picture

For a $300,000 home with 20% down ($60,000) at a 7% mortgage rate:

| Cost | Monthly |
|------|---------|
| Principal + interest | $1,596 |
| Property taxes (1.5%) | $375 |
| Homeowners insurance | $125 |
| Maintenance (1% rule) | $250 |
| **Total** | **$2,346** |

For context, renting equivalent housing in the same market might cost $1,800-2,200. When you add in the opportunity cost of the down payment, the financial comparison shifts further.

This doesn't mean don't buy — it means buy with accurate numbers, not the simplified payment vs rent comparison.

---

## What Equity Building Actually Means

People often say "paying rent is throwing money away" and "your mortgage builds equity." Both deserve scrutiny.

**Your mortgage payment is mostly interest early on.** On a $240,000 loan at 7%, the first year's payments total ~$19,152. Of that, roughly $16,640 goes to interest and only $2,512 to principal. You're building equity very slowly for many years.

**Rent isn't throwing money away.** You're paying for a service: housing, without maintenance responsibility, without locking up a down payment, without transaction costs when you move.

Homeownership builds equity — eventually. But the break-even point (when buying financially surpasses renting) depends on how long you stay, local market conditions, and the assumptions in your calculations. In high-cost markets with short time horizons, renting is often financially superior.

## The New York Times Buy vs Rent Calculator

The NYT has a publicly available tool (search "NYT buy or rent calculator") that lets you input your specific numbers and assumptions to calculate the financial break-even point. Using it with your actual situation is more useful than any general rule.

The honest answer: in some markets at some life stages, buying is the right financial decision. In others, renting is. Know your real numbers.
