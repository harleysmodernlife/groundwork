## Down Payments and PMI

How much you put down on a home affects your monthly payment, whether you pay PMI, and your overall financial health entering homeownership. Understanding the trade-offs helps you make a deliberate decision rather than just putting down as much as you can.

---

## What the Down Payment Does

**Reduces your loan amount:** More down means a smaller loan, which means lower monthly payments and less total interest paid.

**Affects your interest rate:** Lenders view higher down payments as lower risk. 20%+ down often qualifies for slightly better rates.

**Determines whether you pay PMI:** The critical threshold.

**Affects how much equity you start with:** Immediate equity is both financial safety and psychological comfort.

---

## Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI)

PMI is insurance that protects the lender if you default. It's required when you put less than 20% down on a conventional loan.

You pay PMI — not the lender. The lender uses it to reduce their risk. You get nothing from it directly.

**Cost:** Typically 0.5-1.5% of the loan amount per year, depending on your credit score and down payment.

- $250,000 loan at 1% PMI = $2,500/year = $208/month

PMI is charged monthly with your mortgage payment and continues until you reach 20% equity in the home — either through paying down the principal or appreciation — at which point you can request its removal.

**FHA loans** have their own mortgage insurance premium (MIP). FHA MIP typically continues for the life of the loan regardless of equity, unless you refinance into a conventional loan.

## Common Down Payment Amounts

**3% down (conventional):** Some conventional loan programs allow as little as 3% for first-time buyers. PMI will be required and may be at higher rates with a smaller down payment.

**3.5% down (FHA):** FHA minimum. More flexible credit requirements. MIP required for life of the loan.

**10% down:** A middle ground. Reduces PMI compared to 3-5% down, but still requires it. Lower monthly payment than higher down payments.

**20% down:** The traditional standard. Eliminates PMI entirely. Maximum conventional loan flexibility.

**More than 20% down:** Reduces loan size and monthly payment further. But every additional dollar in down payment is a dollar not invested — opportunity cost increases.

---

## The 20% Dilemma

The conventional wisdom is "save 20% to avoid PMI." This is reasonable advice — PMI adds real cost without benefit to you.

But there's a trade-off that's often ignored:

If it takes you three more years to save from 10% to 20% down, that's three years of:
- Paying rent instead of building equity
- Not benefiting from any home appreciation in your market
- Not being in the home you wanted

The calculation depends on your market, expected appreciation, and how long PMI would actually be in effect before you hit 20% equity.

**PMI calculator exercise:** If you're buying a $300,000 home with 10% down:
- PMI of 0.7% on a $270,000 loan = $1,890/year = $157/month
- Time to reach 20% equity through payments + appreciation depends on both factors
- If you'd reach 20% equity within 3-4 years anyway, total PMI paid might be $5,000-7,000

Compare that to: 3 more years of rent, and potentially higher home prices when you do buy.

There's no universal answer. Do the math with your specific numbers.

## Down Payment Assistance Programs

Many first-time homebuyers don't know that down payment assistance programs exist at the state and local level.

These programs offer:
- Grants (free money, no repayment required)
- Forgivable loans (loans forgiven after a period of time in the home)
- Low-interest second mortgages

Income limits apply. "First-time buyer" often means you haven't owned in 3 years, not that you've never owned.

Where to find programs:
- HUD's directory at hud.gov/buying/localbuying
- Your state's housing finance agency (each state has one)
- NCHFA (in North Carolina), CalHFA (California), etc.
- Some lenders have their own first-time buyer programs

The research takes a few hours. The benefit can be $5,000-20,000+ in assistance. Worth the time.

## Gift Funds

If family is willing to help with your down payment, gift funds are generally allowed on conventional and FHA loans. The gift must come from an acceptable source (family member, not seller), and you'll need a signed gift letter confirming it doesn't need to be repaid.

Larger down payment gifts require documentation of the donor's source of funds to comply with lending regulations. Your loan officer will walk you through the requirements.
