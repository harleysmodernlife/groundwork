## Getting Approved: What Lenders Look At

Getting a mortgage isn't just about having a big enough down payment. Lenders evaluate several factors to determine whether you'll make reliable payments — and at what rate. Understanding what they're looking for helps you present the strongest possible application and avoid surprises.

---

## The Four C's of Mortgage Underwriting

Lenders evaluate borrowers through a framework sometimes called the Four C's:

**Credit:** Your credit score and credit history. Payment record, amounts owed, length of history, new credit, credit mix. Your score largely determines your interest rate tier. Most conventional loans require 620+; better rates at 740+.

**Capacity:** Your ability to repay. Measured by your debt-to-income ratio — how much of your gross monthly income goes to debt payments. Lenders look at two DTI ratios:
- *Front-end:* Housing costs ÷ gross monthly income. Most lenders want below 28%.
- *Back-end:* All debt payments ÷ gross monthly income. Most want below 36-43%.

**Capital:** Assets and reserves. How much you have in savings after the down payment and closing costs. Lenders want to see that you could handle several months of payments if income were disrupted. Two months of housing payments in liquid reserves is a typical requirement.

**Collateral:** The property itself. An appraisal ensures the home is worth at least the loan amount. Lenders won't loan $350,000 on a home worth $320,000.

## Documentation You'll Need

Mortgage underwriting is document-intensive. Gather these before starting:

**Income verification:**
- Last 2 years of federal tax returns (all pages)
- Last 2 years of W-2s
- Last 30 days of pay stubs (all employers)
- If self-employed: 2 years of business returns, profit/loss statements, possibly a CPA letter

**Asset verification:**
- Last 2-3 months of bank statements (all accounts, all pages — even blank ones)
- Most recent statements for investment/retirement accounts
- Documentation for down payment sources (explain any large deposits)

**Employment:**
- Two-year employment history
- Contact information for employers
- For gap in employment: explanation letter

**Credit:**
- Lender pulls your credit (a single hard inquiry for mortgage shopping)
- Be prepared to explain derogatory marks

**Property:**
- Accepted purchase contract (after you're under contract)

## What Hurts Your Application

**Recent credit inquiries and new accounts:** Opening new credit in the months before applying raises flags. Don't apply for new credit cards, car loans, or anything else from pre-approval to closing.

**Job change:** Lenders want 2 years of employment stability. Changing employers — even for more money — during the loan process can delay or complicate approval. Wait until after closing if possible.

**Large deposits without explanation:** Unexplained large deposits in your bank accounts create underwriting questions. Keep your finances stable and clean during the process.

**New debt:** Taking on any new debt before or during the loan process changes your DTI calculations. Financing furniture, a car, or anything else before closing is a significant risk to your approval.

**Undisclosed debt:** Underwriters pull credit at the beginning and sometimes again just before closing. New debt that appeared after pre-approval can tank the loan at the last minute.

---

## Pre-Approval vs Pre-Qualification

**Pre-qualification:** A quick, informal estimate of what you might borrow, based on self-reported information. Requires no documentation. Carries little weight with sellers.

**Pre-approval:** A formal process where the lender reviews your documentation, runs your credit, and issues a letter stating they've conditionally approved a specific loan amount. Sellers take this seriously.

In competitive markets, a pre-approval letter before you start shopping is essential. Submitting an offer without one puts you at a significant disadvantage.

---

## How to Strengthen Your Application

If you're not ready now, these steps improve your position:
- Pay down credit card balances to below 30% utilization
- Don't open new accounts or close old ones
- Maintain stable employment
- Save additional reserves
- Address any errors on your credit report
- Pay every bill on time consistently

Six to twelve months of focused credit and savings management before applying can meaningfully improve your interest rate and approval odds.

---

## Shopping for a Lender

Don't take the first offer. Apply with multiple lenders (banks, credit unions, mortgage brokers, online lenders) within a 14-45 day window — all inquiries in that period count as one for credit scoring purposes.

Compare: interest rate, APR (includes fees), origination fees, points offered, estimated closing costs. The Loan Estimate form makes this comparison straightforward.

Your real estate agent may recommend lenders. That recommendation is worth considering, but also get independent quotes. Agents sometimes have referral relationships that benefit them financially.
