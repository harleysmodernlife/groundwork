## Fixed vs Adjustable Rate Mortgages

When you take out a mortgage, one of the first decisions is the loan type. Fixed and adjustable rate mortgages have different structures with meaningfully different risk profiles. Understanding both helps you choose the right option for your situation.

---

## Fixed-Rate Mortgages

A fixed-rate mortgage locks in your interest rate for the life of the loan. If you get a 30-year mortgage at 6.5%, that rate never changes — regardless of what happens to interest rates in the broader economy.

**30-year fixed:** The most common mortgage. Lower monthly payment than a shorter term. Higher total interest paid. Good for people who prioritize payment stability and plan to stay long-term.

**15-year fixed:** Higher monthly payment, but you pay off the loan in half the time and pay significantly less in total interest. Typically offered at a lower interest rate than 30-year loans.

**Example comparison on a $280,000 loan:**

| Loan | Rate | Monthly Payment | Total Interest |
|------|------|-----------------|----------------|
| 30-year fixed at 7% | 7% | $1,863 | $390,000 |
| 15-year fixed at 6.5% | 6.5% | $2,441 | $159,400 |

The 15-year saves approximately $230,000 in interest. The monthly payment is $578 higher.

**The fixed-rate advantage:** Certainty. Your principal and interest payment is identical on month 1 and month 360. You can plan around it.

## Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARMs)

An adjustable-rate mortgage starts with a fixed rate for an initial period, then adjusts periodically based on a market index.

Common ARM structures:
- **5/1 ARM:** Fixed rate for 5 years, then adjusts once per year
- **7/1 ARM:** Fixed rate for 7 years, then adjusts annually
- **10/1 ARM:** Fixed for 10 years, then annual adjustments

The initial rate on an ARM is typically lower than a comparable fixed-rate loan — often 0.5-1.5% lower. The trade-off: after the fixed period, the rate adjusts with market conditions.

**Rate adjustment caps:** ARMs have built-in limits:
- *Initial adjustment cap:* Maximum the rate can change at the first adjustment (typically 2%)
- *Periodic cap:* Maximum change per adjustment period (typically 2%)
- *Lifetime cap:* Maximum the rate can ever exceed the initial rate (typically 5-6%)

On a 5/1 ARM at 5.5% with a 5% lifetime cap: the rate can never exceed 10.5%.

---

## When ARMs Make Sense

ARMs often make sense when:

**You're confident you'll move or refinance before the adjustment period.** If you take a 5/1 ARM and plan to sell in 4 years, the rate adjustment risk is irrelevant — you'll be gone before it happens.

**You want maximum purchasing power now with a plan for the future.** The lower initial rate means lower initial payments, which can allow purchase of a more expensive home. This is higher-risk financial planning.

**Interest rates are historically high.** If you believe rates will fall significantly, an ARM positions you to benefit from falling rates without paying refinancing costs — though you can also refinance a fixed-rate loan when rates drop.

---

## When ARMs Don't Make Sense

**If you're not certain you'll move before adjustment.** Life circumstances change. If you're planning to stay long-term and rates rise significantly, an ARM can dramatically increase your payment.

**If you're at the edge of affordability.** The lower initial payment gives you less buffer for future increases. A rate jump from 5.5% to 7.5% could mean hundreds of dollars more per month — potentially unaffordable if your income hasn't grown.

**In a low or rising rate environment.** The appeal of ARMs is greatest when rates are expected to fall. When rates are at historical averages or rising, locking in a fixed rate is safer.

## Other Loan Types

**FHA loans:** Government-backed loans through the Federal Housing Administration. Allow down payments as low as 3.5%, lower credit score minimums (580+), and more flexible qualifying criteria. Require mortgage insurance premium (MIP) for the life of the loan unless you refinance. Good for first-time buyers with limited savings or credit challenges.

**VA loans:** Available to eligible veterans and active duty military. No down payment required, no PMI, competitive rates. One of the best loan programs available for those who qualify.

**USDA loans:** For eligible rural and some suburban properties. No down payment required. Income limits apply.

**Conventional loans:** Not government-backed. Require higher credit scores and more documentation but offer more flexibility on property type and allow PMI cancellation once you reach 20% equity (FHA MIP often doesn't cancel).

---

## The Rate Shopping Rule

Always get quotes from at least 3-5 lenders. Mortgage rates vary between lenders, and comparing them costs you nothing. Even a 0.25% difference in rate saves thousands over the loan's life.

Rate shopping for a mortgage is treated as a single inquiry if all inquiries happen within a 14-45 day window (depending on the credit scoring model) — so shop without worrying about credit score impact.
