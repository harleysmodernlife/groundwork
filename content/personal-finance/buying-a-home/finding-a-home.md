## Finding and Evaluating a Home

The search for a home can be exciting and exhausting. Knowing what to look for — beyond whether you like the kitchen — helps you avoid costly mistakes and find a home that works for your actual life.

---

## Before You Start Looking

**Get pre-approved first.** Know your budget before falling in love with houses you can't afford. Pre-approval also tells you what the lender's maximum is — which shouldn't necessarily be your maximum.

**Define your must-haves vs nice-to-haves.** Bedroom count, school district, commute, outdoor space, accessibility needs, neighborhood — not everything makes the must-have list, but knowing the difference prevents you from dismissing a good home for minor reasons and accepting a bad one for exciting ones.

**Think about the neighborhood, not just the house.** You can renovate a house. You can't renovate a neighborhood, school district, or commute. The surroundings matter as much as the structure.

---

## Where to Search

Real estate listing sites (Zillow, Realtor.com, Redfin) aggregate active listings from MLS (multiple listing service). These are starting points, not the complete picture.

Your real estate agent has direct MLS access and can set up alerts for new listings matching your criteria — sometimes showing you properties before they appear on consumer sites.

In competitive markets, some properties are sold before hitting MLS through agent networks. A well-connected buyer's agent is more valuable in these markets.

---

## Working with a Real Estate Agent

As a buyer, you typically pay nothing directly to your agent — they're compensated through the commission split from the seller's proceeds. (Commission structures are changing following recent legal settlements; ask your agent how compensation works in your specific transaction.)

A buyer's agent:
- Sets up MLS searches and alerts
- Shows you properties and provides market context
- Writes and submits offers on your behalf
- Negotiates repairs and price changes after inspection
- Guides you through the entire process

**Interview agents before committing.** Ask about their experience in your target area, how many buyers they're currently working with, and their communication style. An agent handling 20 active buyers simultaneously has less time for you than one with 5.

## Evaluating a Property

**What to notice:**

*Foundation and structure:* Cracks in foundation walls (especially horizontal ones), uneven floors, doors that stick or don't close properly. These are expensive to repair and may be deal-breakers.

*Roof:* Age and condition. A roof replacement costs $10,000-20,000+. Visible damage, missing shingles, sagging, or staining on interior ceilings suggests problems.

*Water damage:* Staining on ceilings, walls, or floors. Musty smell in basement or crawl space. Water intrusion leads to mold and structural damage. Look carefully.

*HVAC age:* Furnaces and central air systems have 15-25 year lifespans. Ask the age. Factor upcoming replacement into your cost analysis.

*Electrical:* Older homes may have outdated wiring (knob-and-tube or aluminum) that insurance companies won't cover or that requires costly updating.

*Plumbing:* Ask about material and age. Lead pipes (pre-1986), galvanized steel (old homes), or polybutylene (1970s-1990s) are potential problems.

**What to ask the seller:**

- What's the age of major systems (HVAC, roof, water heater)?
- Have there been any known issues with water, foundation, or pests?
- What's the property's utility history?
- Why are you selling?

Sellers are required to disclose known material defects. What they don't know (or what they conceal) is what the inspection is for.

## The Home Inspection

After your offer is accepted (contingent on inspection), hire a licensed home inspector. This typically costs $300-600 and takes 2-4 hours.

**Attend the inspection in person.** The written report is detailed but can't convey the inspector's sense of urgency about specific issues. Walk the house with them. Ask questions. Ask what they'd prioritize if they were you.

The inspection report identifies defects but doesn't tell you what to do about them. You need to evaluate: what's a deal-breaker? What are you willing to accept? What should you negotiate?

Common negotiation strategies after inspection:
- Request seller credit (they reduce price or give you cash at closing to handle repairs)
- Request seller to repair specific items before closing
- Accept as-is and decide whether the issues change your offer price

Some defects — foundation failure, extensive mold, major structural issues — may be cause to walk away from the deal. Use your inspection contingency.

**Additional inspections** you may want:
- Radon test (inexpensive, important in many regions)
- Sewer scope (camera inspection of drain lines — worth it in older homes)
- Pest inspection (required in some states, valuable everywhere)
- Well and septic inspection (for rural properties)
