## How Mortgages Work

A mortgage is a loan secured by real estate. "Secured" means if you stop making payments, the lender can foreclose — take the property — to recover their money. This security is what allows mortgages to carry lower interest rates than unsecured debt like credit cards.

Understanding the structure of a mortgage helps you make better decisions about what to borrow, for how long, and how to pay it off.

---

## The Basic Structure

You borrow a principal amount and agree to repay it over a term (commonly 15 or 30 years) with interest. Each month you make a payment that includes:

- **Principal:** Reduces your outstanding loan balance
- **Interest:** Pays the lender for the privilege of borrowing

Additionally, most mortgage payments include:
- **Property taxes:** Collected monthly and held in escrow by the lender until taxes are due
- **Homeowners insurance:** Also escrowed monthly

This combined payment is often called PITI: Principal, Interest, Taxes, Insurance.

---

## Amortization: Why Early Payments Are Mostly Interest

Mortgage payments are calculated so they stay the same amount every month, but the split between principal and interest changes dramatically over time.

**Example: $240,000 loan at 7%, 30-year term. Monthly payment: $1,597**

| Year | Principal Paid | Interest Paid | Balance Remaining |
|------|---------------|---------------|-------------------|
| 1 | ~$2,500 | ~$16,650 | ~$237,500 |
| 5 | ~$2,900 | ~$16,200 | ~$226,900 |
| 10 | ~$4,100 | ~$15,100 | ~$210,000 |
| 20 | ~$8,200 | ~$10,900 | ~$163,000 |
| 30 | ~$18,900 | ~$200 | $0 |

In year 1, most of your payment goes to interest. Equity builds slowly. In year 30, almost all goes to principal. This front-loading of interest is a structural feature of amortization.

**Total interest paid over 30 years on a $240,000 loan at 7%: approximately $334,000**

That's more than the original loan amount in total interest. This is why the interest rate matters so much, and why paying extra toward principal — especially early — is powerful.

---

## The Power of Extra Principal Payments

Because interest is calculated on your remaining balance, every dollar of extra principal you pay reduces all future interest calculations.

On the $240,000 loan above: paying an extra $200/month toward principal reduces the total interest by approximately $75,000 and pays the loan off in 22 years instead of 30.

Specify "apply to principal" when making extra payments — some servicers will apply extra payments to future payments by default.

## Loan Origination and Costs

When you take out a mortgage, you pay closing costs. These include:

- **Origination fee:** Charged by the lender for processing the loan (0.5-1% of loan amount)
- **Discount points:** Optional upfront payment to buy down your interest rate. One point = 1% of the loan amount = typically 0.25% rate reduction. Worth it if you'll keep the loan long enough to recoup the cost.
- **Appraisal fee:** Required assessment of the home's market value ($400-700)
- **Title search and insurance:** Ensures clear ownership and protects against future claims ($800-2,000)
- **Survey:** Verifies property boundaries ($500-1,000)
- **Prepaid items:** Initial escrow deposit (a few months of taxes and insurance prepaid)
- **Attorney fees:** Required in some states

Get a Loan Estimate from each lender you're considering. This standardized form lets you compare costs accurately across lenders.

---

## The Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure

The **Loan Estimate** is a three-page form lenders must provide within 3 business days of receiving your application. It shows:
- Loan terms (rate, monthly payment, prepayment penalty, balloon payments)
- Projected payments (PITI + mortgage insurance)
- Closing costs broken down by type

The **Closing Disclosure** is the final version, provided 3 business days before closing. Compare it carefully to your Loan Estimate. Costs should be similar; significant increases in fees are a red flag.

You have the right to take your time reviewing these documents. Don't feel rushed.

---

## Escrow Accounts

Most lenders require an escrow account — they collect 1/12 of your annual property taxes and homeowners insurance with each payment, hold it, and pay the bills when due.

The advantage: you never face a large, unexpected tax or insurance bill. The disadvantage: you're giving the lender an interest-free float on that money.

In some cases, once you've built significant equity, you can request to eliminate the escrow account and pay taxes and insurance yourself. Whether to do this depends on your financial discipline and the specific terms your lender offers.
