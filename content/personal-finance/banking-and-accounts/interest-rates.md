## How Interest Rates Work

Interest is the price of money. When you borrow, you pay interest. When you save or invest, you earn interest. Understanding how it's calculated — and especially how it compounds — is foundational to making good financial decisions.

---

## Simple Interest vs Compound Interest

**Simple interest** is calculated on the original principal only.

$1,000 at 5% simple interest for 3 years: $1,000 × 5% × 3 = $150 in interest. Total: $1,150.

**Compound interest** is calculated on the principal plus all interest earned previously. Interest earns interest.

$1,000 at 5% compounded annually for 3 years:
- Year 1: $1,000 × 5% = $50 → balance becomes $1,050
- Year 2: $1,050 × 5% = $52.50 → balance becomes $1,102.50
- Year 3: $1,102.50 × 5% = $55.13 → balance becomes $1,157.63

Total interest: $157.63 — more than simple interest, and the difference grows significantly over longer periods.

Compounding frequency matters. Interest can compound annually, quarterly, monthly, or daily. The more frequently it compounds, the more you earn (or owe).

Most savings accounts and many loans compound daily or monthly. This is why APY (Annual Percentage Yield) and APR (Annual Percentage Rate) are different numbers.

---

## APR vs APY

**APR (Annual Percentage Rate)** is the simple annual rate — what you see on a loan offer. It doesn't account for compounding within the year.

**APY (Annual Percentage Yield)** accounts for compounding. It's the effective annual rate of return (or cost) after compounding is factored in.

For savings accounts, you want to compare APY — it reflects what you'll actually earn. A savings account advertised at 4.5% APY earning interest daily is better than one advertised at 4.5% APR compounding annually.

For loans, the APR tells you the stated rate, but fees may not be included. The CFPB requires lenders to disclose the APR including fees for mortgages — making it a more comprehensive comparison point.

---

## How Savings Account Interest Works

When your savings account says 4.5% APY:
- That means over a full year, your balance will grow by 4.5% (including the effect of compounding)
- On $5,000, that's $225 in interest over the year
- Interest typically accrues daily and is deposited monthly

The rate on savings accounts is variable — it changes with the federal funds rate set by the Federal Reserve. When the Fed raises rates, high-yield savings rates generally go up. When the Fed cuts rates, they come down.

This is why the savings rates of 2023-2024 (4-5% APY) were significantly higher than those of 2020-2021 (0.5% or less). The rate environment changes.

---

## How Loan Interest Works

On most installment loans (auto, personal, mortgage), interest is front-loaded — the early payments are mostly interest, and later payments increasingly go to principal.

This is called amortization. On a $20,000 car loan at 7% over 60 months:
- Monthly payment: ~$396
- Month 1: ~$117 goes to interest, ~$279 goes to principal
- Month 30: ~$72 goes to interest, ~$324 goes to principal
- Month 60: ~$2 goes to interest, ~$394 goes to principal

Total interest paid over the life of the loan: ~$3,760.

This is why paying extra on a loan — and specifying it goes to principal — saves disproportionately more interest than the extra payment alone suggests. You're cutting off the interest that would have accrued on that principal for all remaining months.

---

## The Federal Funds Rate Connection

The Federal Reserve sets the federal funds rate — the rate at which banks lend to each other overnight. This rate ripples through the economy:

- When the Fed raises it: mortgage rates rise, car loan rates rise, credit card rates rise, and savings rates rise
- When the Fed cuts it: borrowing becomes cheaper, but savings earn less

The Fed adjusts this rate to manage inflation and employment. When inflation is high, they raise rates to cool borrowing and spending. When the economy is sluggish, they cut rates to stimulate it.

You can't control the rate environment, but you can benefit from it: when rates are high, lock in savings rates in CDs. When rates are low, that's when locking in a fixed-rate mortgage or refinancing makes more sense.
