## FDIC Insurance and Protecting Your Deposits

The 2008 financial crisis, the 2023 failures of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank, and every bank failure in American history share something in common: individual depositors at FDIC-insured banks got their money back (up to the limit). Understanding how this protection works — and its limits — is basic financial literacy.

---

## What FDIC Insurance Is

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) is an independent U.S. government agency created in 1933 in response to the bank runs of the Great Depression. When banks failed by the thousands and depositors lost their savings, the resulting crisis deepened the Depression and shook public confidence in the entire banking system.

The fix: federal deposit insurance. If your FDIC-insured bank fails, the FDIC guarantees your deposits up to the coverage limit. You don't have to worry about the bank's financial health — your money is backed by the U.S. government.

Banks pay premiums to fund the FDIC insurance system. Your deposits are protected by a pool funded by the industry.

---

## Coverage Limits

The standard FDIC coverage is **$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per account ownership category**.

That phrase has several parts:

**Per depositor:** Coverage applies to you as an individual, not to the account itself.

**Per insured bank:** You can have $250,000 protected at multiple different banks. If you have $250,000 at Bank A and $250,000 at Bank B, both are fully covered.

**Per account ownership category:** This is where it gets more nuanced. The major ownership categories are:
- Single accounts (owned by one person)
- Joint accounts (owned by two or more people)
- Retirement accounts (IRAs and similar)
- Certain trust accounts

A joint account is covered up to $250,000 per co-owner — so a joint account with two owners has $500,000 in coverage at one bank. A traditional IRA has its own $250,000 coverage limit separate from your regular accounts.

**Practical implication:** Most people have well under $250,000 in any single bank, so this coverage is more than sufficient. For people with larger balances, the ownership categories and multi-bank strategy provide ways to extend protection.

---

## What Is and Isn't Covered

**Covered by FDIC:**
- Checking accounts
- Savings accounts
- Money market accounts
- Certificates of deposit (CDs)

**Not covered by FDIC:**
- Stock market investments
- Mutual funds and ETFs
- Annuities
- Life insurance products
- U.S. Treasury securities (though these have their own federal backing)
- The contents of safe deposit boxes

The key distinction: FDIC covers bank deposit products. It doesn't cover investment products, even when sold by a bank. If you buy a mutual fund through your bank's brokerage arm, that's not covered by FDIC — it's covered by SIPC (Securities Investor Protection Corporation), which protects against broker failure but not market losses.

---

## Credit Unions: NCUA Insurance

Credit unions are not insured by the FDIC — they're insured by the NCUA (National Credit Union Administration) through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund (NCUSIF).

The coverage is equivalent: $250,000 per depositor, per insured credit union, per account ownership category. It's federal backing, just through a separate agency.

For depositors, the practical effect is identical to FDIC insurance.

---

## Verifying Your Bank Is Insured

Most banks prominently display their FDIC membership. You can verify any bank using the FDIC's BankFind tool at fdic.gov.

The FDIC's website also has an Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator (EDIE) tool that helps you calculate whether your specific deposits across accounts are fully covered.

Online-only banks and neobanks are also typically FDIC-insured — either directly or through a partner bank. Check the fine print. Some fintech platforms hold deposits at partner banks and pass through FDIC coverage. Others may not. Confirm before depositing significant funds.

---

## When a Bank Fails

If your bank fails, the FDIC acts quickly — typically within a few business days. In most cases:

- A healthy bank assumes the failed bank's operations and your accounts transfer automatically
- Or the FDIC pays out insured deposits directly, usually within a few days

You may have limited access to funds during the transition. For this reason alone, it makes sense to have accounts at more than one institution — so if one has a disruption, you have access to funds elsewhere.

The FDIC has handled hundreds of bank failures over the decades. For depositors within coverage limits, the system has worked without exception.
