## How Banks Make Money Off You

Banks are businesses. Understanding their revenue model helps you avoid being the product and find better alternatives when they exist.

---

## Interest Rate Spread

The most fundamental way banks make money: they pay you a small amount of interest for keeping money with them, then lend that money to others at a higher rate and keep the difference.

Example: You keep $10,000 in a savings account earning 0.5% (the bank pays you $50/year). The bank lends that money to someone at 7% (the bank collects $700). The spread is the profit.

This is why your savings account rate at a big bank is often embarrassingly low — the spread is larger when they pay you less. Online banks with lower overhead pass more of that spread to depositors, which is why their rates are higher.

---

## Fees

Banks charge an enormous variety of fees, many of which are avoidable if you know they exist:

**Monthly maintenance fees:** $10-15/month to maintain a checking account, waived with direct deposit or minimum balance. If you don't meet the requirements and don't notice the fee, you're paying $120-180/year for the privilege of having an account.

**Overdraft fees:** When you spend more than you have and the bank covers it, they charge $25-35 per transaction. Some banks allow you to link a savings account as overdraft protection (usually a smaller transfer fee). Others will decline the transaction — embarrassing at checkout, but free.

**ATM fees:** Using an ATM outside your bank's network costs $2-5 from the ATM operator, plus a fee from your bank for using an out-of-network ATM. Two fees, per transaction.

**Wire transfer fees:** Sending money by wire domestically costs $15-35 at most banks. Services like Zelle are free. Many people pay wire fees when free alternatives exist.

**Paper statement fees:** Some banks charge $2-3/month to mail you a statement. Opt for electronic statements.

**Returned item fees:** If a check you deposited is returned (bounced), you pay a fee — even though someone else's check bounced.

## How Banks Use Your Money

When you deposit money, you're lending it to the bank. The bank is required to keep a fraction on reserve (though reserve requirements for most deposits were reduced to zero in 2020) and can lend the rest.

Banks lend your deposits as mortgages, auto loans, personal loans, and business loans. They also invest in government bonds and other securities.

This means when you deposit $1,000, that $1,000 doesn't sit in a vault somewhere. It's being deployed into the economy — and earning the bank money. The bank owes you your $1,000 on demand, which is how the system works, backed by FDIC insurance in case the bank itself runs into trouble.

---

## Credit Cards

When you carry a credit card balance, you're paying interest directly to the card issuer. This is some of the most profitable business in banking — credit cards carry average interest rates of 20-25% APR.

Even when you pay in full, every purchase generates interchange fees (1.5-3.5% of the transaction) paid by merchants. Rewards programs are largely funded by this revenue.

---

## Avoiding the Fees

Most bank fees are avoidable:

**Monthly maintenance fees:** Choose a bank with no-fee checking. Online banks (Ally, Marcus, SoFi, Capital One 360) often have no monthly fees. Credit unions frequently offer free checking to members.

**Overdraft fees:** Set up overdraft protection linked to a savings account. Or opt out of overdraft coverage entirely (your card will be declined rather than triggering a fee). Or use a bank that doesn't charge overdraft fees — several neobanks have eliminated them entirely.

**ATM fees:** Choose a bank that reimburses ATM fees, or use a bank with a large free ATM network (Allpoint, MoneyPass).

**Wire fees:** Use Zelle, PayPal, Venmo, or Cash App for person-to-person transfers instead of wires.

---

## The Credit Union Alternative

Credit unions are nonprofit cooperatives owned by their members. Because they're not optimizing for shareholder returns, they often offer:

- Higher savings account rates
- Lower loan rates
- Lower fees (or no fees)
- More flexible lending criteria

The trade-off: they often have smaller ATM networks, fewer branch locations, and less advanced digital tools than major banks — though this gap has narrowed considerably.

For people who qualify to join one, credit unions are worth serious consideration for your primary banking relationship.
