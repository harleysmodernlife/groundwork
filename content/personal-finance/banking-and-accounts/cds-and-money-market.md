## CDs and Money Market Accounts

Beyond basic checking and savings, banks offer a few other deposit products worth understanding. They're not essential for most people starting out, but they can be useful once you have money saved and want to optimize how it's held.

---

## Certificates of Deposit (CDs)

A CD is a savings product where you deposit money for a fixed period of time — typically 3 months to 5 years — in exchange for a guaranteed interest rate. The rate is usually higher than a regular savings account, with longer terms typically paying more.

**How they work:** You deposit $2,000 in a 12-month CD at 4.8% APY. At the end of the 12 months, you receive your $2,000 plus approximately $96 in interest. The rate is locked — it won't go up or down with market conditions.

**The trade-off:** That money is locked in. If you withdraw before the maturity date, you pay an early withdrawal penalty — typically 60-180 days of interest, depending on the CD term and institution.

**When CDs make sense:**
- You have money you're confident you won't need for the CD's duration
- CD rates are meaningfully higher than high-yield savings rates
- You want a guaranteed, predictable return

**When they don't make sense:**
- The money might be needed for an emergency (emergency funds should stay liquid)
- You haven't built an emergency fund yet
- Current high-yield savings rates are competitive with CD rates (which fluctuates)

**CD Laddering:** A strategy where you split money across CDs of different terms — say, $1,000 in a 3-month CD, $1,000 in 6-month, $1,000 in 12-month. As each matures, you can spend it or roll it into a new CD. This gives you regular liquidity while earning better rates than keeping everything in savings.

## Money Market Accounts

A money market account (MMA) is a hybrid between a checking and savings account. It typically:
- Pays higher interest than a regular savings account
- Allows limited check writing or debit card transactions (usually 6 per month)
- Requires a higher minimum balance to avoid fees or earn the advertised rate

Money market accounts are FDIC-insured just like regular savings and checking accounts.

**Money market accounts vs. money market funds:** These are different things. A money market account is a bank deposit product. A money market fund is an investment product (offered by brokerages) that invests in short-term, low-risk securities. Money market funds are not FDIC-insured — they're investment accounts. The distinction matters.

---

## When These Products Are Relevant

For most people who are just starting to get their finances in order, these products are secondary to the basics:
- Emergency fund in a high-yield savings account
- Checking account for day-to-day spending

Once you have a solid emergency fund and are beginning to save money beyond it — for a house down payment two years out, for a planned major purchase — CDs or money market accounts can offer slightly better returns than keeping everything in savings, with the trade-off of reduced liquidity.

The interest rate environment matters too. When rates are high (as they've been in recent years), the gap between savings accounts and CDs may be small enough that the liquidity of savings is worth more than the small extra interest of a CD. When rates are low, long-term CDs may lock in rates you'll appreciate later.

---

## The Bottom Line

These are optimization tools, not fundamentals. Get the basics right first — free checking, high-yield savings, emergency fund built — and then explore CDs or money market accounts when you have money that genuinely won't be needed for a defined period of time.
