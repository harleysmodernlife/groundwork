## Asset Allocation by Life Stage

Asset allocation is how you divide your investment portfolio between different types of assets — primarily stocks and bonds. It's the single most important decision in investing, with more impact on your long-term returns and risk than any individual investment choice.

---

## Why Allocation Matters More Than Stock Selection

Studies consistently show that asset allocation explains roughly 90% of the variation in portfolio returns over time. Whether you hold 80% stocks or 50% stocks matters far more than which specific stocks or funds you choose.

Getting allocation right means:
- Taking enough risk that your money grows meaningfully over time
- Not taking so much risk that a market crash forces you to sell at a loss
- Adjusting appropriately as your time horizon shortens

---

## The Core Variables

**Time horizon:** The single biggest factor. More time → can accept more risk. Less time → need more stability.

**Risk tolerance:** Your psychological capacity to handle watching your portfolio fall. Higher tolerance → more stocks. Lower tolerance → more bonds.

**Income stability:** Stable, predictable income (government job, tenured position) supports a more aggressive allocation. Variable income (self-employed, gig work, commission-based) benefits from more stability in the portfolio.

**Goals:** Building long-term wealth vs. preserving capital vs. generating income. Different goals call for different allocations.

---

## Allocation by Life Stage

These are generalizations, not rules. Use them as a starting framework and adjust for your specific situation.

**In your 20s:** Time is your greatest asset. A common allocation is 90% stocks, 10% bonds — or even 100% stocks if you have a stable income and strong tolerance for volatility. A 30% drop in a 100% stock portfolio at 25 is painful to watch but irrelevant if you're not selling for 35 years.

**In your 30s:** Still long horizon. 80-90% stocks remains reasonable. Some people add more bonds as financial commitments (mortgage, family) increase and they become more aware of real-world risk.

**In your 40s:** The time horizon is shortening. A move toward 70-80% stocks is reasonable. The portfolio is likely larger by this point, which means losses are larger in dollar terms — another reason to start adding stability.

**In your 50s:** Retirement within 15 years. 60-70% stocks for most people. The "sequence of returns risk" starts to matter — a bad market in the early years of retirement can significantly damage a portfolio that's being drawn down.

**At or near retirement:** 40-60% stocks, depending on the size of your portfolio, other income sources (Social Security, pension), and how long you need it to last. People retiring at 65 may have 30+ years of portfolio life ahead. A portfolio that's too conservative may not last.

**In retirement:** Variable, and shouldn't go to 0% stocks. A retirement lasting 25-30 years still needs growth to outpace inflation. A bucket strategy — keeping 1-2 years of expenses in cash, the next 5 years in bonds, the rest in stocks — allows you to ride out downturns without selling stocks at the wrong time.

## Target-Date Funds: Automatic Allocation

If choosing and maintaining your allocation feels overwhelming, target-date funds do it automatically.

Pick a fund with the year you expect to retire in the name: Vanguard Target Retirement 2050 (VFIFX), Fidelity Freedom 2050 (FFFHX), etc.

The fund starts aggressive (mostly stocks) and automatically shifts to more conservative (more bonds) as the target date approaches. You contribute, it handles the allocation.

Target-date funds are a legitimate, intelligent choice — not a cop-out. Many sophisticated investors use them. The cost is usually low if you choose from Vanguard, Fidelity, or Schwab.

---

## Rebalancing

Allocation drifts over time. If stocks outperform bonds for several years, your 80/20 portfolio might drift to 90/10 — more risk than you intended.

Rebalancing means selling some of the overweight asset and buying the underweight one to return to your target.

Most people rebalance once a year. Some do it when any asset class drifts more than 5% from its target. In tax-advantaged retirement accounts, rebalancing has no tax consequences. In taxable accounts, selling may trigger capital gains taxes.

The frequency matters less than the consistency. Annual rebalancing is fine.
