## Stocks: Owning a Piece of a Company

A stock is a share of ownership in a company. When you buy one share of a company's stock, you become a part-owner of that business — entitled to a proportional share of its assets and earnings.

This is not a metaphor. You own it. Your ownership is tiny, but it's real.

---

## How Stocks Work

When a company wants to raise money from the public, it conducts an IPO (Initial Public Offering) — issuing shares and selling them to investors. After the IPO, those shares trade on stock exchanges (NYSE, Nasdaq) where buyers and sellers transact throughout the trading day.

The price of a share reflects what buyers are willing to pay for it — which is based on beliefs about the company's current and future value. If investors believe a company will earn more in the future, the price rises. If they're worried about its prospects, the price falls.

This means stock prices are driven by expectations as much as current reality. A company can report record profits and see its stock fall because investors expected even better results.

---

## How You Make (or Lose) Money

**Price appreciation:** If you buy a share for $50 and it rises to $80, you've made $30 per share — a 60% return.

**Dividends:** Some companies distribute a portion of their profits to shareholders as cash payments (dividends), usually quarterly. If a stock pays a $2/year dividend on a $50 stock, that's a 4% dividend yield — real cash, paid to you for holding it.

**Price decline:** If the share falls from $50 to $30, you've lost $20 per share — a 40% loss on paper. It becomes a real loss only when you sell.

---

## What Affects Stock Prices

Many things: company earnings reports, economic data, interest rate changes, industry trends, management decisions, lawsuits, product launches, competitor news, and the general mood of investors (which can be irrational).

In the short term, stocks are highly unpredictable — even professional investors who follow companies closely regularly get it wrong. In the long term, stock prices tend to track business performance. Companies that consistently grow their earnings see their stock prices rise over time.

---

## Individual Stocks vs the Market

Buying individual company stocks means your returns are tied to how that specific company performs. A single stock can double in a year — or go to zero.

Individual stocks are higher risk than owning many stocks because you're concentrated. If you put $10,000 in one company and it goes bankrupt, you lose $10,000. If you spread $10,000 across 500 companies, one bankruptcy is barely noticeable.

For most individual investors, owning individual stocks requires substantial research, time, and risk tolerance. Even then, research consistently shows that most individual investors and active fund managers underperform simple index funds over long periods.

This doesn't mean you should never own individual stocks — but it means understanding what you're taking on when you do.

---

## How to Actually Buy Stocks

You buy stocks through a **brokerage account** — a financial account specifically for holding investments. Setting one up is now similar to setting up a bank account, done entirely online.

Major brokerages include Fidelity, Vanguard, Charles Schwab, and others. All offer commission-free trading on stocks and ETFs. There's no cost to simply hold a brokerage account.

Once the account is funded (transfer money from your bank), you can search for a company by name or ticker symbol and place a buy order.
