## Diversification: Not Putting All Your Eggs in One Basket

Diversification is the practice of spreading investments across many different assets so that the poor performance of any single investment doesn't devastate your portfolio.

It's sometimes called "the only free lunch in investing" — because it reduces risk without necessarily reducing expected return.

---

## Why It Works

Different investments don't always move together. When one goes down, others may hold steady or go up. The more uncorrelated your holdings, the more the gains in some offset the losses in others.

**The extreme example of non-diversification:**

You put $20,000 — your entire investment — into your employer's stock. The company goes through accounting fraud and the stock falls 90%. You have $2,000 left. This happened to Enron employees in 2001. Many lost not just their investments but their retirement savings.

**The diversified example:**

You put $20,000 into an S&P 500 index fund. One company in the fund goes through the same scandal. It represents 0.2% of the index. Your portfolio drops $40. You barely notice.

Same catastrophic event. Completely different outcome.

---

## Types of Diversification

**Across companies:** Instead of one company, own hundreds or thousands. Index funds accomplish this automatically.

**Across sectors:** Technology, healthcare, financials, consumer goods, energy. These sectors perform differently in different economic conditions. An index fund covering the whole market provides this automatically.

**Across geographies:** U.S. stocks represent roughly 60% of global market capitalization. International stocks — Europe, Asia, emerging markets — often move differently from U.S. stocks. Adding international exposure reduces home-country risk.

**Across asset classes:** Stocks, bonds, real estate, cash. These behave differently from each other, especially in down markets. A balanced portfolio with some bonds cushions stock market crashes.

**Across time (dollar-cost averaging):** Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals rather than all at once. You buy more shares when prices are low, fewer when prices are high. Reduces the risk of investing a lump sum at a market peak.

---

## What Diversification Cannot Do

Diversification reduces **unsystematic risk** — the risk specific to individual companies or sectors. It cannot eliminate **systematic risk** — the risk that the entire market declines.

In 2008, nearly all stocks fell together. In March 2020, nearly all stocks fell together. A diversified stock portfolio still fell substantially in both cases.

To reduce systematic market risk, you need diversification across asset classes — particularly stocks and bonds, which often (though not always) move inversely.

---

## How Much Diversification Is Enough?

Research suggests that most of the diversification benefit within stocks is captured by owning 20-30 individual companies across different sectors. Beyond that, additional diversification provides diminishing returns.

An S&P 500 index fund owns 500 companies. A total market fund owns thousands. You're well beyond the point of meaningful diversification.

**The practical answer:** A broad index fund is diversified. You don't need to add dozens of individual stock picks on top of it. Adding more holdings that are highly correlated with what you already own doesn't meaningfully reduce risk.

---

## Over-Diversification

It's possible to spread so thin that your returns are dragged down by mediocre holdings — but for most individual investors, this isn't the real risk. The real risk is under-diversification: concentration in a handful of stocks or in one sector.

A total market index fund or an S&P 500 fund plus an international fund is well-diversified. You don't need 15 funds.

---

## The Diversified Portfolio in Practice

For a simple, well-diversified portfolio:

1. **Total U.S. stock market index fund** — broad domestic exposure
2. **International stock index fund** — exposure to economies outside the U.S.
3. **Bond index fund** — reduces volatility and provides stability

Three funds. Thousands of underlying holdings across dozens of countries and asset classes. Automatic rebalancing once a year.

That's it. Most investors don't need more complexity than this.
