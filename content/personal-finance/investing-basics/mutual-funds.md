## Mutual Funds

A mutual fund pools money from many investors to purchase a collection of securities — stocks, bonds, or both. When you invest in a mutual fund, you buy shares of the fund, and the fund manager invests the pooled capital according to the fund's stated strategy.

Mutual funds are one of the oldest and most widely used investment vehicles. Understanding them — including their costs and limitations — helps you make better choices.

---

## How Mutual Funds Work

You invest $1,000 in a mutual fund. Thousands of other investors do the same. The fund now has millions of dollars to invest, and a fund manager (or management team) decides what to buy.

Your $1,000 buys you shares of the fund at that day's **Net Asset Value (NAV)** — calculated as the total value of the fund's holdings divided by the number of shares outstanding. Mutual funds price once per day, after markets close.

If the fund's holdings go up in value, the NAV rises and your shares are worth more. If they fall, so does your balance.

---

## Types of Mutual Funds

**Actively managed funds:** A portfolio manager makes decisions about what to buy and sell. Higher fees, variable performance. As covered in the index fund lesson, most underperform their benchmark over long periods.

**Passively managed (index) funds:** Track a specific index. Low cost. Better long-term performance for most investors.

**Bond funds:** Hold bonds rather than stocks. Lower risk and return than stock funds. Useful for stability and income.

**Balanced/hybrid funds:** Hold a mix of stocks and bonds in a predetermined ratio (say, 60/40). Simpler than managing separate stock and bond funds.

**Target-date funds:** A type of balanced fund that adjusts its stock/bond ratio automatically over time, becoming more conservative as the target retirement year approaches. If you want a single fund that manages itself, a target-date fund is the simplest option.

**Money market funds:** Invest in short-term, low-risk instruments. Typically very stable (though not FDIC insured). Often used as the "cash" holding in a brokerage account.

## Fees: The Critical Factor

Mutual fund fees — the expense ratio — are taken from your investment automatically. You never see a bill. The fund's returns are simply reduced by the expense ratio.

- An actively managed fund at 1.0% expense ratio: For every $10,000 invested, you pay $100/year
- An index fund at 0.04%: For every $10,000 invested, you pay $4/year

Over 30 years with a 7% gross return, the difference in fees compounding is enormous:

$10,000 at 7% gross, 0.04% expense ratio: ~$73,000
$10,000 at 7% gross, 1.0% expense ratio: ~$57,000

The lower-cost fund produces $16,000 more — not because it earned more, but because less was extracted in fees.

This is why expense ratio is one of the most important numbers to check before investing in any fund.

**Also check for:**
- **Load fees:** Some funds charge a sales commission — either upfront (front-end load, typically 3-5%) or when you sell (back-end load). No-load funds charge no sales commission. Avoid load funds. No-load index funds are superior.
- **Transaction fees:** Some brokerages charge a fee to buy or sell certain funds. Choose funds available commission-free at your brokerage.

---

## Mutual Funds vs ETFs

ETFs and mutual funds often track the same underlying index and have very similar expense ratios. The key differences:

**ETFs** trade throughout the day like stocks. You buy at market price. Minimum investment is the price of one share (sometimes fractional shares are available).

**Mutual funds** trade once per day at NAV. Many have minimum initial investments ($500-3,000 is common, though Fidelity and Schwab have reduced many minimums to $0).

For long-term investing, both work fine. ETFs have slight tax efficiency advantages and no minimums. Mutual funds are simpler for automatic contributions.

---

## Where to Buy

Mutual funds are purchased through brokerage accounts or retirement accounts (IRA, 401(k)). Most major brokerages — Fidelity, Vanguard, Schwab — offer their own mutual funds with no transaction fees.

Buying a Vanguard fund at Fidelity may incur a transaction fee; buying it directly at Vanguard doesn't. Check for transaction fees on the specific funds you want at the brokerage you're using.

---

## The Bottom Line

Mutual funds are fine investment vehicles when you choose the right type: low-cost, passively managed, no-load, tracking a broad index. A target-date fund in a 401(k) or IRA is often the simplest quality choice.

The trap to avoid: high-cost, actively managed funds with load fees — the kind that generate commissions for the brokers who sell them.
