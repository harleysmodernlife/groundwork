## Risk and Return

In investing, risk and return are inseparable. Higher potential returns require accepting higher risk. Lower risk comes with lower potential returns. Understanding this relationship — and how to work with it — is essential to making sound investment decisions.

---

## The Risk-Return Tradeoff

This is a fundamental principle: investors are compensated for taking risk.

A U.S. Treasury bond (backed by the federal government) pays a low but nearly certain return. An individual stock might deliver 50% in a year, or lose 50%. The stock's potential return is higher because you're accepting the real possibility of significant loss.

Investors who don't accept this principle often make the mistake of chasing high returns without understanding the risk — or becoming so risk-averse that they hold everything in cash or savings accounts and lose purchasing power to inflation.

---

## Types of Risk

**Market risk (systematic risk):** The risk that the entire market goes down. A recession, a global crisis, a spike in inflation — these affect nearly all stocks and cannot be avoided by diversifying within the stock market.

**Company-specific risk (unsystematic risk):** The risk that a particular company performs poorly or fails entirely. This can be significantly reduced through diversification — owning many companies rather than one.

**Inflation risk:** The risk that your returns don't keep pace with inflation. Cash and low-yield savings accounts carry this risk.

**Liquidity risk:** The risk that you need money when your investment is hard to sell (real estate, some bonds, illiquid assets).

**Interest rate risk:** When interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall. Longer-duration bonds are more affected.

**Concentration risk:** Putting too much in any single investment, sector, or country.

---

## The Historical Record

The U.S. stock market (measured by broad indices like the S&P 500) has historically returned approximately 10% annually before inflation, or roughly 7% after inflation, over long periods. But that average comes with significant volatility:

- Some years are up 30%+
- Some years are down 30-40% (2008 saw the S&P 500 fall ~37%)
- Multi-year bear markets occur periodically
- Crashes feel permanent when you're in them

The long-term upward trend has held, but it requires actually holding through the downturns — which is psychologically harder than it sounds.

**Bonds** have historically returned less than stocks — around 3-5% annually — with less volatility. They tend to hold value better during stock market downturns.

**Cash and savings accounts** have returned close to inflation historically, with minimal risk. No growth in real terms over long periods.

---

## Time Horizon and Risk

The longer your investment horizon, the more risk you can reasonably accept.

Money you need in 1-2 years should be in cash or stable savings. A market downturn just before you need the money is a real problem.

Money you won't need for 20+ years can ride out multiple market cycles. The 2008 financial crisis saw the S&P 500 lose nearly 50% of its value — and then recover fully within 4 years and go on to new highs.

If you held through the 2008 crash and didn't sell, you were fine. If you sold at the bottom in panic, you locked in the loss.

**The practical rule:** Only invest money in higher-risk assets that you're confident you won't need for at least 5 years — preferably 10+.

---

## Risk Tolerance

Beyond time horizon, there's a psychological component: how well can you tolerate watching your portfolio drop?

Two investors both have $50,000 invested and both have 20-year horizons. One sees a 30% drop ($15,000 loss on paper) and stays calm, knowing it's temporary. The other panics and sells.

The second investor isn't wrong to be uncomfortable — but acting on that discomfort by selling at the bottom is where the real damage happens.

Before investing, be honest about how you'll actually respond to seeing your balance drop 20%, 30%, or 40%. If you'll sell in a panic, that changes the right investment approach. A portfolio you'll hold through volatility is better than an optimal portfolio you'll abandon.

---

## Managing Risk Without Eliminating Return

**Diversification:** Owning many investments rather than concentrating in a few. If one company fails, it's a small fraction of your portfolio.

**Asset allocation:** Holding a mix of stocks, bonds, and other assets that behave differently from each other. When stocks fall, bonds often hold steadier.

**Long time horizon:** Perhaps the most powerful risk management tool. More time means more ability to recover from downturns.

**Low-cost index funds:** Automatically diversified across hundreds or thousands of companies, at low cost. More on this in the index funds lesson.

The goal isn't to eliminate risk — it's to take appropriate risk for your timeline and tolerance, diversify to reduce company-specific risk, and hold long enough that market risk pays off statistically in your favor.
