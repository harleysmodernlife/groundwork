## Bonds: Lending Your Money

A bond is a loan. When you buy a bond, you're lending money to whoever issued it — a company, a city, a state, or the federal government. In return, they promise to pay you interest on a schedule and return the principal at a set future date.

Bonds are the counterweight to stocks in most investment portfolios. They behave differently, carry different risks, and serve a different purpose.

---

## How Bonds Work

When you buy a bond, you're agreeing to specific terms:

- **Face value (par value):** The amount you'll receive when the bond matures, typically $1,000
- **Coupon rate:** The annual interest rate, expressed as a percentage of face value
- **Maturity date:** When the issuer returns your principal

Example: You buy a 10-year U.S. Treasury bond with a face value of $1,000 and a coupon rate of 4%. Every year for 10 years, you receive $40 in interest payments (usually split into two $20 payments). At the end of year 10, you receive your $1,000 back.

Total return over 10 years: $400 in interest + $1,000 principal = $1,400 on a $1,000 investment.

## Types of Bonds

**U.S. Treasury bonds:** Issued by the federal government. Backed by the full faith and credit of the United States government — considered among the safest investments in the world. Lower returns reflect lower risk.

Treasury bills (T-bills) are short-term (under a year), Treasury notes are medium-term (2-10 years), and Treasury bonds are long-term (10-30 years). I-bonds are inflation-adjusted savings bonds, limited to $10,000 per person per year.

**Municipal bonds (munis):** Issued by state and local governments. Interest is often exempt from federal income tax (and sometimes state tax), making them attractive to people in higher tax brackets.

**Corporate bonds:** Issued by companies. Higher yield than government bonds because companies carry more default risk. Companies with weaker financials must offer higher rates to attract buyers — these are called high-yield or "junk" bonds.

---

## Bond Ratings

Credit rating agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch) rate bonds based on the issuer's ability to repay:

**Investment grade:** BBB/Baa and above. Lower risk, lower yield.
**Speculative (high yield):** BB/Ba and below. Higher risk, higher yield.

The rating tells you how much default risk you're accepting. U.S. Treasuries are AAA — the highest rating. A bond from a struggling company might be rated B or lower.

## Bond Prices and Interest Rates

Here's the part people find counterintuitive: bond prices and interest rates move inversely.

When market interest rates rise, existing bonds become less attractive (they pay yesterday's lower rate), so their prices fall. When rates fall, existing bonds paying higher rates become more valuable, so their prices rise.

This matters if you sell a bond before maturity — you might sell for more or less than you paid, depending on where rates moved. If you hold to maturity, you get exactly the face value regardless of what happened to interest rates in between.

## Bonds vs. Stocks: The Role They Play

Stocks: higher expected return, higher volatility, better for long-term wealth growth.

Bonds: lower expected return, lower volatility, better for preserving capital and providing steady income.

In a portfolio, bonds act as ballast. When stocks drop sharply, bonds often hold their value or rise (investors move money to safer assets during panics). Having some bonds reduces the severity of portfolio swings — at the cost of some long-term growth.

The traditional rule of thumb was to hold a percentage of bonds equal to your age: 30% bonds at 30, 60% bonds at 60. Many modern advisors consider this too conservative given longer lifespans and low historical bond yields — but the underlying logic (reduce risk as you approach needing the money) is sound.

---

## How Most People Hold Bonds

Individual bonds require meaningful capital to diversify properly. Most individual investors access bonds through:

- Bond index funds or ETFs (like those tracking the Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index)
- Target-date retirement funds (which automatically include bonds and adjust the mix as you age)
- Treasury Direct (TreasuryDirect.gov) for buying U.S. Treasury bonds directly, without a broker

For most people starting out, the bond allocation in a target-date fund or a simple stock/bond index fund portfolio handles all of this automatically.
