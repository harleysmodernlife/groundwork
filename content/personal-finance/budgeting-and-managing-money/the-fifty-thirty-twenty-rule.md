## The 50/30/20 Rule

The 50/30/20 rule is a simple framework for dividing your take-home pay. It won't work for everyone in every situation, but as a starting point for understanding whether your finances are structured reasonably, it's one of the most useful tools available.

---

## The Three Buckets

**50% — Needs**
This covers expenses that are genuine necessities — things you can't reasonably eliminate.

- Rent or mortgage
- Basic utilities (electricity, heat, water)
- Groceries (not eating out)
- Transportation to work (car payment, gas, insurance, or transit)
- Minimum debt payments (these count as needs even if the debt is from wants)
- Health insurance and essential medications

If your needs consistently consume more than 50% of your take-home pay, you have a structural problem. The issue isn't that you're spending on too many luxuries — it's that your fixed costs are too high relative to your income. The solution is either increasing income or reducing a major fixed expense (housing, car, etc.).

**30% — Wants**
Wants are things that improve your quality of life but aren't required to survive or stay functional.

- Eating out and takeout
- Streaming subscriptions, entertainment, hobbies
- Travel and vacations
- Clothing beyond the basics
- Gym memberships
- Upgrades — a nicer apartment than you need, a newer car than you need

The 30% figure is generous. If you're trying to pay down debt aggressively or build savings quickly, this is the category to compress.

**20% — Savings and Debt Payoff**
This covers everything that builds your future financial position.

- Emergency fund contributions
- Retirement savings (401k, IRA)
- Paying more than the minimum on debt
- Saving for a specific goal (down payment, education, etc.)
- Investments

This 20% is the most important number. If it's zero — if every dollar of income goes to living expenses — you're not making progress. You're treading water.

---

## How to Use It as a Diagnostic

The 50/30/20 rule is most useful as a health check, not a perfect prescription.

Pull up your last month of spending. Categorize everything into needs, wants, and savings. Calculate what percentage each represents.

If your needs are 65%, you know your fixed cost structure needs attention — probably housing or a car payment.

If your wants are 35% and savings are 5%, you know where the slack is.

If savings is 0%, that's your first priority to fix — even moving it to 5% is progress.

---

## When It Doesn't Work

The 50/30/20 rule assumes your income is high enough to cover basic needs at 50%. In many cities, housing alone can be 40-50% of take-home pay for someone at a moderate income. In that case, the math doesn't work as described.

If that's your situation, the framework still has value — you just need to adjust the targets. Maybe it's 65/15/20 while you work on either increasing income or finding cheaper housing. The point is the structure: know your percentages, and make deliberate decisions about each category.

It also doesn't work for people with aggressive debt payoff goals. If you have $40,000 in high-interest credit card debt, directing 20% toward savings and debt may be too slow. You may need to compress wants to 10-15% and throw everything else at the debt.

---

## The Point

The 50/30/20 rule doesn't require a detailed budget. It requires you to know your percentages and make deliberate adjustments when something is off.

That's less than zero-based budgeting demands — and more than most people are currently doing.
