## Where Your Money Comes From

Before you can manage money, you need to be clear about exactly how much of it you actually have. Most people have a rough idea. Rough ideas don't build budgets.

---

## Income Is Not What You Make — It's What You Keep

If you earn $18 an hour and work 40 hours a week, you might think your income is $720 a week. It isn't. Not after taxes, and possibly not after other deductions.

Your **gross income** is what you earn before anything is taken out. Your **net income** — sometimes called take-home pay — is what actually hits your account. For most workers, there's a meaningful gap between the two.

The things that typically come out before you see your paycheck:
- **Federal income tax** — withheld based on the W-4 form you filled out when you started
- **State income tax** — depends on your state; some states have none
- **Social Security tax** — 6.2% of your wages, up to a limit
- **Medicare tax** — 1.45% of your wages
- **Health insurance premiums** — if you're enrolled through your employer
- **Retirement contributions** — if you have a 401(k) with automatic deductions

The number that matters for budgeting is your net income. Use that number. Not the gross.

---

## Types of Income

Most people have one main source of income, but it's worth knowing what kinds exist — because mixing them up causes confusion at tax time and budget time.

**W-2 income** is what you get from an employer. Taxes are withheld automatically, and you get a W-2 form every January showing what you earned and what was taken out.

**1099 income** is what you get from freelance work, gig work, or self-employment. No taxes are withheld. The full amount lands in your account, and it's on you to set money aside for taxes. Many people are caught off guard by this.

**Passive income** includes things like rental income, dividends from investments, or royalties. It's real income — it also has tax implications.

**Government benefits** — SNAP, Social Security, disability payments, child tax credits — are income too. They belong in your income calculation.

## The Variable Income Problem

If your income is consistent — the same amount every two weeks — budgeting is relatively straightforward.

If your income varies — tips, commission, seasonal work, gig work — you need a different approach. The method that works best:

**Budget based on your lowest recent month, not your average.** If you made $2,800, $3,100, $1,900, and $2,600 over the last four months, budget as if you'll make $1,900. In better months, the extra goes to your emergency fund. In worse months, you're not scrambling.

This feels conservative. It's supposed to. Budgeting on optimistic income estimates is how people end up behind every bad month.

---

## Write It Down

Right now, figure out your actual monthly take-home income. Not roughly — actually.

If you get paid biweekly (every two weeks), multiply your net paycheck by 26, then divide by 12. That's your monthly income. Don't use the shortcut of multiplying by 2 — that gives you the wrong number for most months.

If your income varies, look at your last three to six months of bank statements and calculate the actual average. Then shave it down by 10-15% to build in a buffer.

That number — whatever it is — is what your budget has to work within. Everything starts from there.
