## Fixed vs Variable Expenses

Not all expenses work the same way. Some are locked in — they're the same amount every month whether you like it or not. Others move around based on your choices. Understanding the difference changes how you approach budgeting.

---

## Fixed Expenses

A **fixed expense** is one that stays the same every month and that you're obligated to pay.

Common fixed expenses:
- Rent or mortgage payment
- Car payment
- Loan payments (student loans, personal loans)
- Insurance premiums (health, auto, renters)
- Phone plan
- Internet service
- Minimum credit card payments (if you're carrying a balance)

Fixed expenses are predictable, which makes them easy to budget. The problem is that they're also hard to change in the short term. If your rent is $1,100 a month, it's $1,100 a month until you move or renegotiate.

This is why fixed expenses can quietly strangle a budget. Each one you add — a new car payment, a new subscription, a bigger apartment — reduces your financial flexibility for months or years.

**The rule:** Be very careful before adding a new fixed expense. Ask what you'd have to cut elsewhere to cover it. Make sure the answer is acceptable before you sign.

---

## Variable Expenses

A **variable expense** changes month to month based on your behavior and choices.

Common variable expenses:
- Groceries
- Gas
- Eating out and entertainment
- Clothing
- Personal care
- Utilities (electric, water — these fluctuate)
- Household supplies
- Gifts

Variable expenses are where most people's budgets fall apart — not because they're inherently dangerous, but because they feel small in the moment and add up invisibly over time.

The $7 coffee, the $14 lunch, the $30 Amazon order, the $11 movie ticket — none of them feel significant. Together, they can easily be $400-600 a month in a budget that only had $200 allocated for "extras."

**The rule:** Variable expenses require active attention. They don't manage themselves.

---

## Periodic Expenses: The Third Category

There's a third type that trips people up: expenses that aren't monthly, but aren't optional.

Car registration. Annual subscriptions. Holiday gifts. Back-to-school supplies. Car repairs. Medical bills.

These aren't surprising if you think about them in advance. But most people don't, so they feel like emergencies — and often go on a credit card.

The fix is to estimate your annual total for these irregular costs, divide by 12, and set that amount aside each month as if it were a fixed expense. When the bill comes, the money is already there.

For example: car registration is $180/year, Christmas spending is $300, annual subscriptions are $120. Total: $600/year, or $50/month. Set aside $50 every month and you're never caught off guard.

---

## Why This Distinction Matters

When money is tight and you need to cut spending, you need to know what's actually cuttable.

Fixed expenses can't be reduced this month — they require renegotiation, cancellation, or a life change (moving, selling a car). Those are real options, but they take time.

Variable expenses can be reduced starting today. You can spend less on groceries, skip eating out for two weeks, cut entertainment spending, delay a clothing purchase.

Knowing which category your expenses fall into tells you where you have control and where you need a longer-term plan. A budget that treats everything as equally adjustable leads to frustration when you realize half your spending isn't actually negotiable in the short term.

---

## The Practical Step

Go through your spending from the past month and label each expense as fixed, variable, or periodic.

Then ask: what percentage of my income is locked into fixed obligations before I make any choices? If that number is 70% or more, you have limited room to maneuver, and the path forward involves either increasing income or changing one of those fixed expenses — not just "spending less on coffee."

That's an honest assessment. It's where real planning begins.
