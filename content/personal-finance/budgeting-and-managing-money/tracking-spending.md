## Tracking Where It Goes

Most people who are broke don't know why they're broke. They know money comes in and money goes out, but the middle part — where it actually goes — is a blur.

Tracking your spending is uncomfortable. It will probably show you things you don't want to see. Do it anyway.

---

## Why Tracking Works

You can't fix a problem you can't see.

Before people track their spending for the first time, they usually underestimate what they spend on food by 40-50%, underestimate subscriptions by half, and have genuinely no idea what their daily spending on small purchases adds up to.

Tracking doesn't change your habits automatically — but it makes the unconscious conscious. Once you can see exactly where money is going, you can make real decisions about it.

---

## How to Track

**Method 1: Bank and credit card statements**
The most complete picture. Go to your bank's website or app, download or view your last 30-60 days of transactions, and go through them one by one. Label each transaction: rent, groceries, gas, eating out, subscriptions, etc.

This is tedious the first time. It gets faster. And it's accurate because it captures everything you actually spent — not what you remember spending.

**Method 2: A tracking app**
Apps like Mint, YNAB (You Need a Budget), or Copilot link to your accounts and categorize transactions automatically. The auto-categorization isn't perfect — you'll need to review it — but it dramatically reduces the manual work.

**Method 3: A notebook or spreadsheet**
Low-tech but effective. Write down every purchase the day you make it. The act of writing things down makes you more aware of spending in the moment.

Pick the method you'll actually stick to. The best system is the one you use.

---

## Categories That Matter

When you're categorizing your spending, you want buckets that are useful — not so broad that everything disappears into "miscellaneous" and not so narrow that it's exhausting.

A reasonable starting set:
- Housing (rent/mortgage, utilities, renter's insurance)
- Transportation (car payment, gas, insurance, maintenance, parking, transit)
- Food (groceries and eating out — track these separately)
- Healthcare (insurance premiums, copays, prescriptions)
- Subscriptions (streaming services, apps, memberships, gym)
- Debt payments (credit cards, student loans, personal loans)
- Personal care (haircuts, toiletries, clothing)
- Entertainment (movies, concerts, hobbies)
- Savings and investments
- Everything else

**Separate groceries from restaurants.** People who don't do this consistently underestimate how much they're spending eating out — because it gets absorbed into "food" alongside the less-painful grocery number.

---

## What You're Looking For

After you've tracked for 30 days, you want to answer three questions:

1. **Where is the most money going?** Usually housing and transportation are the big two, but often eating out is larger than expected.

2. **What am I paying for that I don't use or don't value?** Subscriptions are the obvious one. But also gym memberships, apps, services.

3. **What's the gap?** How much came in versus how much went out? If you spent more than you made, that gap is debt accumulation — even if it feels normal.

---

## Do This First

Before building any budget, track for at least two weeks — ideally a full month. Don't try to change behavior yet. Just watch.

You can't build an accurate budget out of guesses. You need real numbers from your real life. Once you have them, the next steps become a lot clearer.
