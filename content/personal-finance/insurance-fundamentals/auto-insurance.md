## Auto Insurance

Auto insurance is required by law in 49 states, and for good reason: a car accident can injure people, kill people, and generate liability that exceeds what most individuals can pay. Understanding what coverage you actually have — and what you don't — matters the moment something goes wrong.

---

## The Components of Auto Insurance

Auto policies bundle several different types of coverage. Understanding each one prevents nasty surprises.

**Liability coverage — required in most states**

This covers damage you cause to others — their injuries and property damage — when you're at fault in an accident.

It's expressed as three numbers: 100/300/100 means:
- $100,000 per person for bodily injury
- $300,000 per accident for all bodily injury
- $100,000 for property damage

Most states require minimum coverage far below these limits. If you cause an accident that seriously injures someone and your liability coverage is $25,000 — the minimum in many states — and their medical bills are $150,000, you're personally responsible for $125,000. Your assets and future wages can be garnished to pay this.

**Recommendation:** Carry at least 100/300/100 in liability coverage. If you have significant assets, consider an umbrella policy for additional protection. Liability coverage is cheap relative to its protection.

**Collision coverage**

Covers damage to your own car from a collision — regardless of who's at fault. Subject to your deductible.

If your car isn't worth much, collision may not be worth carrying. The insurer won't pay more than the car's actual cash value (ACV). If your car is worth $4,000 and you have a $1,500 deductible, the max you'd ever collect is $2,500. Run the math: if the annual collision premium is $400+, consider whether it's worth it.

**Comprehensive coverage**

Covers non-collision losses: theft, fire, hail, falling trees, hitting an animal. Also subject to your deductible.

Same logic applies as collision for older, lower-value vehicles.

**Uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage (UM/UIM)**

Covers you if you're hit by someone with no insurance or insufficient insurance. This is more common than most people realize — roughly 13% of drivers nationally are uninsured.

Without UM coverage, if an uninsured driver totals your car and puts you in the hospital, you're collecting nothing from their nonexistent policy. UM covers your injuries and vehicle damage instead. Relatively inexpensive; worth carrying.

**Medical payments (MedPay) / Personal injury protection (PIP)**

Covers medical expenses for you and your passengers regardless of fault. PIP (required in "no-fault" states) may also cover lost wages. Often duplicates health insurance coverage — evaluate whether you need it based on your health coverage.

## Factors That Affect Your Rate

Insurance companies set rates based on risk. Factors that affect what you pay:
- Driving record (accidents, tickets)
- Age (young drivers pay much more)
- Location (urban areas with more claims pay more)
- Vehicle type (expensive cars cost more to insure)
- Credit score (in most states — poor credit means higher rates)
- Mileage
- Deductible level

---

## How to Save Without Sacrificing Coverage

**Shop around regularly.** Rates vary significantly between insurers for the same coverage. Getting quotes every 1-2 years at renewal is worth the time.

**Bundle with renters/homeowners.** Most insurers offer discounts for multiple policies.

**Raise your deductible.** Moving from a $250 to a $1,000 deductible can reduce premiums meaningfully. Fund the higher deductible with your emergency fund.

**Maintain a clean driving record.** Tickets and at-fault accidents increase your rates for 3-5 years.

**Drop comprehensive and collision on older vehicles** if the math doesn't justify them.

**Ask about discounts.** Good driver, good student, low mileage, safety features, defensive driving course — many exist and aren't always automatically applied.

## After an Accident

If you're in an accident:
1. Ensure everyone is safe; call 911 if there are injuries
2. Exchange information: name, license, insurance company and policy number, license plate
3. Document: photograph the scene, damage to all vehicles, other driver's information
4. Don't admit fault at the scene
5. Contact your insurer to file a claim

Filing a claim after a minor accident isn't always the right move. If the damage is close to your deductible amount, paying out of pocket avoids a claims history that raises your future premiums.
