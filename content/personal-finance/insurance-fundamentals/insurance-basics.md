## Premiums, Deductibles, and How Insurance Actually Works

Insurance is a financial product where you pay a company regularly so that when something expensive and unexpected happens, they pay most of the cost instead of you. It's a way of converting unpredictable large losses into predictable small expenses.

Understanding the basic mechanics makes you a better buyer and helps you avoid common mistakes.

---

## The Core Vocabulary

**Premium:** The amount you pay for insurance coverage — usually monthly, quarterly, or annually. You pay this whether or not you make any claims. Think of it as the price of coverage.

**Deductible:** The amount you pay out-of-pocket before the insurance company starts paying. If your car is damaged and repairs cost $3,000, and your deductible is $500, you pay $500 and insurance pays $2,500.

Higher deductibles mean lower premiums (you're taking on more of the risk). Lower deductibles mean higher premiums (the insurer takes on more of the risk).

**Premium-deductible tradeoff:** Choosing between a $250 deductible and $150/month vs. a $1,000 deductible and $90/month means deciding: would I rather pay $60 more per month to reduce my deductible by $750? That's $720 per year extra in premiums. If you don't file a claim in that year, you paid $720 for nothing. If you do file a claim, you saved $750 — barely worth it. This math suggests higher deductibles often make financial sense for healthy people with some savings to cover them.

**Coverage limit:** The maximum the insurer will pay. A $300,000 liability limit on your auto policy means the insurer will pay up to $300,000 if you cause an accident — anything above that is your responsibility.

**Copay:** A fixed amount you pay for a specific service — common in health insurance. "$30 copay for primary care visits" means you pay $30 and insurance covers the rest, regardless of the actual bill.

**Coinsurance:** After meeting your deductible, some policies split remaining costs between you and the insurer. A plan with 20% coinsurance means you pay 20% of covered costs, insurance pays 80%, until you hit your out-of-pocket maximum.

**Out-of-pocket maximum:** The most you'll pay in a policy year for covered services. After you hit this limit, insurance covers 100% of additional covered costs. In health insurance, this protects against catastrophic medical expenses.

**Claim:** A formal request to your insurance company to pay for a covered loss.

## How Insurance Companies Make Money

Insurance companies collect premiums from many people. Most of those people don't have large claims in any given year. The premiums from the many cover the claims of the few, with the insurance company keeping a profit margin.

This works because risks are poolable and predictable at scale. An individual driver can't predict if they'll have an accident, but actuaries can predict with precision how many out of 100,000 drivers will have accidents, and approximately how costly those accidents will be. That statistical predictability is what allows insurance to function.

**The implication:** Insurance is not designed to make you financially ahead on average. On average, you will pay more in premiums than you receive in claims — that's where the insurer's profit margin comes from. Insurance exists to protect against the tail risk of a catastrophic event you couldn't afford to handle alone.

## What Insurance Should Cover

The right insurance protects against losses that would be financially devastating — not losses you could absorb.

Use insurance to protect against:
- Events that would be financially catastrophic (serious accident, major illness, house fire, lawsuit)
- Things that are legally required (auto liability insurance in most states)
- Situations where you have no other safety net (death of a breadwinner with dependents, disability)

Don't buy insurance for:
- Small losses you can cover from savings (this is what deductibles are for)
- Extended warranties on appliances (usually overpriced for the value)
- Credit card payment protection plans (high cost, limited value, many exclusions)
- Small-ticket item replacement insurance

The high-deductible, self-insure-the-small-stuff approach — combined with an emergency fund — is generally the most cost-effective way to handle insurance. Pay the deductible from savings; let insurance handle the catastrophe.

## Why People Make Insurance Mistakes

**Underinsuring to save on premiums:** Tempting, but can result in financial ruin from a covered event where the coverage limit was too low.

**Overinsuring small risks:** Paying high premiums for coverage of things you could self-fund.

**Not shopping around:** Insurance rates vary enormously between companies for equivalent coverage. Getting quotes every 1-2 years at renewal is worth the time.

**Letting coverage lapse:** A gap in insurance is unprotected time. One accident or illness during a coverage gap can be financially catastrophic.
