## Health Insurance: Plans, Networks, and Costs

Health insurance is the most complex type of insurance most people deal with — and one of the most consequential. A poor choice can cost thousands of dollars in out-of-pocket expenses you didn't anticipate.

---

## The Basic Structure

Health insurance requires you to pay premiums (usually monthly) in exchange for coverage of medical expenses. But coverage comes with multiple layers of cost-sharing that most people don't fully understand until they get a bill.

**Premium:** Your monthly payment for coverage. Employer plans often split this with you — your share is typically deducted from your paycheck.

**Deductible:** The amount you pay entirely before insurance begins sharing costs. A $2,000 deductible means you pay the first $2,000 of covered services in a year out of your own pocket.

**Copay:** A fixed amount for specific services (doctor visit, ER, prescription). Often applies even after the deductible is met.

**Coinsurance:** After meeting the deductible, you split remaining costs. 20% coinsurance: you pay 20%, insurance pays 80%.

**Out-of-pocket maximum:** The ceiling on what you pay in a year. Once reached, insurance covers 100% of covered services for the rest of the year. This is the most important protection against catastrophic medical bills.

A typical plan might have: $1,500 deductible / 20% coinsurance / $6,000 out-of-pocket maximum. If you have a major illness requiring $50,000 in covered care, you pay $1,500 (deductible) + 20% of the next $22,500 (until you hit the OOP max) = ~$6,000 total, regardless of how large the bill is.

---

## Plan Types

**HMO (Health Maintenance Organization):** You choose a primary care physician (PCP) who coordinates your care. Referrals required to see specialists. You must use in-network providers (except emergencies). Generally lower premiums and out-of-pocket costs. Less flexibility.

**PPO (Preferred Provider Organization):** More flexibility — you can see any doctor in or out of network without a referral. Out-of-network care costs more but is covered. Higher premiums than HMOs.

**EPO (Exclusive Provider Organization):** Like an HMO without the PCP requirement — you can see specialists directly, but only in-network (except emergencies). A middle ground.

**HDHP (High Deductible Health Plan):** Higher deductible, lower premium. Often paired with an HSA (Health Savings Account), which offers significant tax advantages. Makes sense if you're generally healthy and have the savings to cover the deductible if needed.

---

## Understanding Networks

Your plan's network is the set of doctors, hospitals, and facilities that have contracts with your insurer. In-network care costs significantly less than out-of-network care.

Before scheduling any non-emergency procedure or seeing a new provider, verify they're in-network. This includes the facility itself and all the providers in it — surgeons, anesthesiologists, and assistants may all bill separately and may have different network status.

"Surprise billing" from out-of-network providers has been significantly reduced by federal law (the No Surprises Act), but it hasn't been eliminated entirely. Know your rights.

## Where to Get Coverage

**Employer-sponsored insurance:** If your employer offers health insurance and contributes to premiums, this is almost always the best option. Employer contributions are pre-tax, effectively discounting the cost significantly.

**ACA Marketplace (healthcare.gov):** If you don't have employer coverage, the Affordable Care Act marketplace offers standardized plans from insurers. Premium tax credits and subsidies are available based on income — many people qualify for significant subsidies. Open enrollment runs November 1 - January 15 for coverage starting the following year.

**Medicaid:** Free or very low-cost coverage for people at or below certain income thresholds. In states that expanded Medicaid, coverage is available to individuals earning up to 138% of the federal poverty level. Check eligibility at healthcare.gov.

**COBRA:** If you lose job-based coverage, COBRA lets you continue that coverage for up to 18 months — but you pay the full premium (employer share + your share). Usually expensive. May be worth it for continuity of care for ongoing conditions.

**Short-term plans:** Cover gaps between other coverage but often exclude pre-existing conditions and have limited benefits. Not a long-term solution.

---

## How to Choose

For most people in good health:

An HDHP paired with an HSA is often the best value. Lower premiums, tax-advantaged savings, and the HSA can be invested for long-term growth.

If you have chronic conditions, take regular medications, or anticipate significant medical use, a lower-deductible PPO or HMO may save money despite higher premiums.

Run the math: estimate your expected medical spending for the year. Add expected premiums + deductibles + copays/coinsurance for different plans. The plan that minimizes total expected cost (not just premiums) is usually the right choice.
