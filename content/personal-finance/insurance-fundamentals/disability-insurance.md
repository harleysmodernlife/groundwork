## Disability Insurance: Protecting Your Income

Your ability to earn income is your most valuable financial asset. A 30-year-old earning $50,000/year has $1.75 million in future earnings ahead of them (before raises). No car, investment portfolio, or home comes close to that value.

Disability insurance protects that asset. If you become unable to work due to illness or injury, it replaces a portion of your income. It's the most commonly overlooked insurance for working-age adults — and the gap is often discovered at the worst possible moment.

---

## The Risk Is Real

The statistics are uncomfortable:

- The Social Security Administration estimates that 1 in 4 people who are 20 years old today will become disabled for at least a year before reaching retirement age
- The average long-term disability claim lasts nearly three years
- Most disabilities aren't from dramatic accidents — back problems, cancer, depression, heart disease, and other medical conditions are the most common causes

Most people insure their car ($400+/year for something worth $15,000). Many don't insure their income ($50,000+/year indefinitely). The asymmetry is significant.

---

## Types of Disability Insurance

**Short-term disability (STD):** Covers you for a shorter period — typically 3-6 months — following an illness or injury. Often provided by employers. Replaces 60-80% of income, depending on the plan.

**Long-term disability (LTD):** Kicks in after short-term coverage ends (or after an elimination period if you have no STD). Covers you for years — sometimes until retirement age. Typically replaces 50-70% of income.

The most important coverage is long-term disability. A three-month inability to work is hard; a three-year or permanent disability without income replacement is financially catastrophic.

---

## Where to Get Coverage

**Employer group disability:** Many employers offer long-term disability as a benefit, often covering 60% of salary. If available, enroll. It's usually subsidized and relatively inexpensive.

**Limitations of employer group coverage:** Usually limited to 60% of income, may have caps (e.g., $5,000/month maximum), definitions of disability may be less favorable, and coverage ends when you leave the job.

**Individual disability policy:** Purchased separately from an insurer. More expensive than group coverage but portable (you keep it if you change jobs) and often has better terms. More important for self-employed people and those without employer coverage.

---

## Key Policy Terms to Understand

**Elimination period:** The waiting period before benefits begin — often 90 days for LTD policies. You must be disabled for this long before you collect. Your emergency fund covers this gap.

**Own-occupation vs any-occupation:** This distinction matters enormously.

*Own-occupation:* You're considered disabled if you can't do your specific occupation. A surgeon who can no longer perform surgery is disabled — even if they could technically do other work. More expensive, more valuable.

*Any-occupation:* You're considered disabled only if you can't do any work you're reasonably suited for. The insurer may argue you can do desk work. Harder to qualify for benefits.

Higher-quality policies use own-occupation definitions, at least for a transitional period.

**Benefit period:** How long benefits last if you remain disabled. "To age 65" is preferable to a fixed 5-year benefit — a serious disability at 40 could last 25 years.

**Benefit amount:** The monthly payment if disabled. Policies typically cap at 60-70% of pre-disability income — insurers want you to have incentive to return to work.

## Social Security Disability

Social Security disability (SSDI) is often mistakenly relied upon as a safety net. The reality:

- The average approval process takes 6+ months (often 1-2 years with appeals)
- The denial rate at initial application exceeds 60%
- The average monthly SSDI benefit is around $1,400 — rarely sufficient
- To qualify, you must be severely disabled and unable to do any substantial gainful activity

SSDI is a last resort, not a plan. Don't count on it as your disability insurance.

---

## For the Self-Employed

Self-employed people have no employer disability benefits. Individual disability insurance is not optional if you have dependents or significant financial obligations.

The cost is higher, but the need is arguably greater — there's no employer disability plan to fall back on, no paid sick leave, no workers' comp for most off-the-job injuries and illnesses.
