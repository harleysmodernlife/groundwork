## Rules for Taking Money Out

Getting money into a retirement account is only half the equation. Understanding the rules for taking money out — when you can, what you owe, and how to do it efficiently — is essential for actually turning savings into retirement income.

---

## The Basic Rule: Age 59½

For most retirement accounts (Traditional and Roth IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s), you can take withdrawals without penalty starting at age 59½.

Before that age, withdrawals are generally subject to:
- **10% early withdrawal penalty** on top of any taxes owed
- **Ordinary income tax** on pre-tax money (traditional accounts)

This makes early withdrawal very expensive. $10,000 withdrawn at 40 in a 22% tax bracket costs $3,200 (10% penalty + 22% taxes), leaving only $6,800.

---

## Exceptions to the 10% Penalty

The penalty has exceptions — situations where you can withdraw before 59½ without the 10% hit (though taxes still apply to pre-tax money):

- **Disability:** Permanent and total disability
- **Death:** Funds distributed to beneficiaries
- **Substantially Equal Periodic Payments (SEPP/72(t)):** A complex arrangement where you take calculated, equal payments over a period of years; must be maintained for 5 years or until 59½, whichever is longer
- **Medical expenses:** Exceeding 7.5% of AGI
- **Health insurance premiums** while unemployed (IRA only)
- **Higher education expenses** (IRA only)
- **First-time home purchase:** Up to $10,000 lifetime (IRA only)
- **Qualified birth or adoption:** Up to $5,000
- **Roth IRA contributions:** You can always withdraw your contributions (not earnings) at any time, for any reason, tax and penalty-free — because you already paid taxes on them

The "first home" and "education" exceptions are often used by people who treat their IRA as an emergency fund. Think carefully: retirement accounts grow tax-advantaged. Raiding them early costs not just the withdrawal penalty but decades of tax-free compounding on that money.

---

## Roth IRA Withdrawal Rules

Roth IRAs have a specific ordering rule for withdrawals:

1. **Contributions:** Always available, tax and penalty-free
2. **Conversions (ordered by year):** Penalty-free after 5 years from conversion
3. **Earnings:** Tax-free and penalty-free only after age 59½ AND the account has been open 5 years

The "5-year rule" for Roth earnings: the account must have been established at least 5 years before earnings can be withdrawn tax-free in retirement. The clock starts January 1 of the year you make your first Roth IRA contribution.

For most people who open a Roth IRA in their 20s or 30s, the 5-year clock is satisfied long before they retire.

## Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

Starting at age 73 (as of 2023 rule changes), the IRS requires you to withdraw a minimum amount from certain retirement accounts each year.

**Accounts subject to RMDs:**
- Traditional IRA
- Traditional 401(k) and 403(b) (unless you're still working for that employer)
- SIMPLE and SEP-IRA

**Accounts NOT subject to RMDs:**
- Roth IRA (during the original owner's lifetime)
- Roth 401(k) (as of 2024, no RMDs during owner's lifetime)

The RMD amount is calculated by dividing your account balance (at December 31 of the prior year) by a life expectancy factor from IRS tables.

Failing to take your RMD results in a 25% excise tax on the amount not withdrawn (reduced to 10% if corrected promptly).

---

## Withdrawal Strategies for Retirement Income

**Which accounts to draw from first?**

The conventional wisdom: draw from taxable accounts first, then traditional accounts, then Roth last. This allows Roth accounts the longest period to grow tax-free.

The better answer depends on your tax situation. If drawing from traditional accounts keeps you in a low tax bracket, that may be preferable to letting RMDs eventually force large taxable withdrawals in your 70s.

**Roth conversions:** Between retirement and age 73 (when RMDs begin), many retirees do partial Roth conversions — converting traditional IRA money to Roth at low tax rates, reducing future RMD burden and providing more tax-free income later.

**The bucket strategy:** Keeping 1-2 years of expenses in cash, 3-7 years in bonds, and the rest in stocks. This provides stability (you spend from the cash/bond bucket without selling stocks in a downturn) while maintaining long-term growth.

---

## Inherited Retirement Accounts

Rules for inheriting retirement accounts changed significantly under the SECURE Act (2019) and SECURE 2.0 (2022).

Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited traditional IRA within 10 years of the original owner's death (with some nuance based on whether the original owner had started RMDs).

Spouses have more flexibility — they can treat an inherited IRA as their own.

If you're a beneficiary of a retirement account, the rules are complex enough that a financial advisor or tax professional consultation is worth the cost.
