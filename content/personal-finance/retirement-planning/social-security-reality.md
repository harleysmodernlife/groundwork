## Social Security: What It Is and What It Isn't

Social Security is both misunderstood and politically contested — which makes it hard to plan around. Here's what it actually is, how it works, and how to think about it in your retirement plan.

---

## What Social Security Is

Social Security is a federal insurance program funded by payroll taxes (you pay 6.2%, your employer pays 6.2% — or you pay 12.4% if self-employed). You earn Social Security "credits" by working and paying into the system. You need 40 credits (approximately 10 years of work) to qualify for retirement benefits.

**It's insurance, not a savings account.** Your payroll taxes don't go into a personal account with your name on it. Current workers' taxes fund current retirees' benefits. This is called a pay-as-you-go system. The Social Security Trust Fund holds reserves, but there's no dedicated pot of money waiting specifically for you.

---

## How Your Benefit Is Calculated

Your Social Security benefit is based on your **35 highest-earning years**, adjusted for wage inflation. If you worked fewer than 35 years, zeros are averaged in for the missing years — lowering your benefit.

The calculation:
1. Index your annual earnings to account for wage inflation
2. Average the 35 highest years (AIME — Average Indexed Monthly Earnings)
3. Apply a formula that's weighted to replace a higher percentage of income for lower earners

The result is your **PIA (Primary Insurance Amount)** — your benefit if you claim at full retirement age.

Full retirement age (FRA) is currently 67 for people born in 1960 or later.

---

## When to Claim: The Most Important Decision

You can claim Social Security as early as 62 or as late as 70. The timing dramatically affects your monthly benefit for life.

**Claiming early at 62:** Reduces your benefit by 25-30% from your FIA permanently.

**Claiming at full retirement age (67):** You receive your full PIA.

**Delaying past FRA, up to 70:** Your benefit increases 8% per year. From 67 to 70, that's a 24% increase.

**Example: PIA of $2,000/month at 67:**
- At 62: ~$1,400/month
- At 67: $2,000/month
- At 70: ~$2,480/month

The break-even age — when the total lifetime benefits of waiting beats claiming early — is typically around 78-82. If you live past that age, delaying was financially superior. If you die earlier, claiming sooner would have paid more total.

**General guidelines:**
- If you're in poor health or have a family history of shorter life expectancy: consider claiming earlier
- If you're healthy and can afford to wait: delay to 70 for the higher inflation-indexed benefit
- For married couples: the higher earner should usually delay as long as possible — the survivor's benefit is based on the higher earner's record

---

## The Funding Challenge

This is the uncomfortable part: Social Security faces a long-term funding gap.

The ratio of workers to retirees has shrunk as the population ages. The trust fund is projected to be exhausted in the mid-2030s. At that point, incoming payroll taxes would cover approximately 77-80% of projected benefits.

This doesn't mean Social Security disappears — it means Congress faces a choice: cut benefits, raise the payroll tax, raise the retirement age, means-test benefits, or some combination.

Historical precedent: Congress has always adjusted Social Security before the trust fund was exhausted (most recently in 1983). Politicians on both sides have strong incentives to protect benefits — Social Security is politically sacrosanct.

**How to plan for this uncertainty:**
- Don't plan as if Social Security doesn't exist — that's too pessimistic
- Don't plan as if current benefit levels are guaranteed — that's too optimistic
- Plan assuming benefits at 75-80% of the projected amount if you're younger than 50
- Check your projected benefit at ssa.gov/myaccount — these are personalized projections

---

## Spousal and Survivor Benefits

**Spousal benefit:** A spouse who didn't work (or earned less) may receive up to 50% of the higher-earning spouse's PIA. You're entitled to the higher of your own benefit or 50% of your spouse's.

**Survivor benefit:** When a spouse dies, the surviving spouse receives the higher of the two spouses' benefits. This is why the higher earner delaying to 70 protects the surviving spouse — whoever lives longer receives the higher delayed benefit for the rest of their life.

**Divorced spouses:** If you were married for at least 10 years and are currently unmarried, you may be eligible for benefits based on your ex-spouse's record, without affecting their benefits.

---

## The Bottom Line

Social Security is a valuable, inflation-adjusted lifetime income stream. It's not enough to retire on alone for most people, but it's a meaningful floor that significantly reduces how much portfolio income you need. Plan around it thoughtfully — when you claim, and how it interacts with the rest of your retirement income — because the decisions are largely irreversible.
