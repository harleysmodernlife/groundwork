## How Much Do You Actually Need to Retire?

The most common answer: "More than you have." That's not helpful. Let's get to numbers you can actually work with.

---

## The 4% Rule

The most widely cited retirement guideline is the **4% rule** (more precisely, the 4% safe withdrawal rate). It comes from research by financial planner William Bengen in 1994, later refined by the "Trinity Study."

The finding: a portfolio invested in a mix of stocks and bonds can sustain annual withdrawals of 4% of the initial balance, adjusted for inflation, for at least 30 years — with a historically high probability of success across market conditions.

**The math:** To use the 4% rule to calculate your target:
- Estimate your annual retirement spending
- Multiply by 25

If you expect to spend $50,000/year in retirement: $50,000 × 25 = **$1,250,000**

That's your retirement target.

---

## Estimating Your Retirement Spending

Your retirement spending is typically lower than your current spending — but not always, and not in the same ways people expect.

**Costs that often go down:**
- No mortgage (if it's paid off)
- No commuting or work-related expenses
- No retirement savings contributions (you're spending them, not saving)
- No more raising children

**Costs that often stay the same or increase:**
- Housing (utilities, maintenance, property taxes)
- Healthcare (typically rises significantly — see below)
- Food
- Travel and leisure (often higher in early retirement when you're active)

A reasonable starting estimate: 70-80% of your pre-retirement income. But run your own numbers based on your expected lifestyle — someone who wants to travel extensively or has significant healthcare needs may need closer to 100%.

---

## Healthcare Is the Wildcard

Healthcare costs in retirement are the most commonly underestimated expense.

Medicare eligibility begins at 65. If you retire before 65, you need to bridge healthcare coverage through a spouse's plan, marketplace insurance (often expensive without employer subsidy), or COBRA. These can cost $500-1,500+/month before Medicare.

Medicare itself is not free. Premiums for Medicare Part B, supplemental Medigap coverage, and Part D drug coverage can total $300-700+/month. Plus out-of-pocket costs for copays, deductibles, and non-covered services.

The average American couple retiring at 65 should expect to spend $300,000-400,000 on healthcare costs over retirement, according to Fidelity Research estimates. An HSA built up over your working years is the best tax-efficient tool for this.

## The Role of Social Security

Social Security provides income you don't have to fund from savings. For every dollar of annual Social Security income, your portfolio needs to provide $25 less (using the 4% rule).

If you expect $20,000/year in Social Security, that effectively reduces your savings target by $500,000.

**Caveats:**
- Social Security is not guaranteed at current levels — the trust fund is projected to face funding shortfalls in the 2030s, which could result in benefit cuts unless Congress acts
- When you claim affects your benefit amount significantly (discussed in the Social Security lesson)
- Don't build a retirement plan that requires Social Security to be whole — but do factor in what you realistically expect

## Personalizing the Target

**More conservative than 4% (3-3.5%):** If you retire before 65 and need money to last 35-40 years, a slightly lower withdrawal rate provides more security. Some researchers argue 3.5% is a safer target for early retirees.

**More flexible than 4%:** Many retirees can modestly reduce spending in bad market years, which allows a somewhat higher initial withdrawal rate or a smaller starting balance.

**The floor:** Social Security and any pension income. What you need your portfolio to cover is (total expenses − guaranteed income).

---

## A Simple Calculator Framework

1. Estimate annual retirement spending
2. Subtract expected Social Security and any pension income
3. Multiply the remaining gap by 25 (for 4% withdrawal rate)
4. That's your portfolio target

Example:
- Expected annual spending: $65,000
- Expected Social Security: $20,000
- Gap to cover from portfolio: $45,000
- Portfolio target: $45,000 × 25 = **$1,125,000**

Use a retirement calculator (Vanguard, Fidelity, and others have free tools) to account for inflation, expected investment returns, and your specific timeline. The calculation above is directionally useful; the tools get you closer to precise.
