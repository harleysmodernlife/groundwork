## Why Starting Early Changes Everything

Retirement planning advice can feel abstract when you're 25 and retirement is 40 years away. It becomes painfully concrete at 55 when you realize how much compound growth you left on the table by starting at 35.

The math is unambiguous: time is the single most important factor in retirement savings. More important than how much you earn. More important than how savvy your investments are. Earlier always beats more.

---

## The Numbers That Change Minds

**Two investors, same $5,000 annual contribution, same 7% average annual return:**

- **Investor A** contributes $5,000/year from age 25 to 35 (10 years, then stops). Total contributed: $50,000. At 65: **~$602,000**
- **Investor B** contributes $5,000/year from age 35 to 65 (30 years). Total contributed: $150,000. At 65: **~$472,000**

Investor A contributed $100,000 less and contributed for only 10 years — but ends up with $130,000 more, because of 10 extra years of compounding.

---

## The Comparison Most People Should See

If you're 35 and haven't started: don't despair, but understand the real cost.

**$300/month starting at 25 vs $300/month starting at 35, at 7% to age 65:**
- Starting at 25: ~$909,000
- Starting at 35: ~$454,000

A 10-year head start nearly doubles the outcome on the same monthly contribution. The "I'll start later when I have more money" logic is expensive.

---

## Why Waiting Feels Justified (and Why It Isn't)

Common reasons people delay:

**"I'll save more later when I earn more."** You probably will earn more later. But later contributions, even larger ones, can't fully compensate for lost compounding time. $1,000/month starting at 35 doesn't catch up to $300/month starting at 25 by 65.

**"I have student loans/car payment/rent to worry about."** These are real competing demands. But investing is not all-or-nothing. Even $50-100/month in your 20s captures the most valuable compounding years. Increase contributions as debt is paid off.

**"The market might go down."** It will, periodically. Over 40+ years, every significant downturn has recovered and exceeded previous levels. The risk of being too aggressive at 25 is far less than the risk of starting 10 years late.

**"I don't understand investing yet."** You don't need to. A target-date fund requires zero investment knowledge. You pick the year you expect to retire, contribute regularly, and the fund manages itself.

---

## What "Catching Up" Actually Requires

If you're behind, here's the honest math on what it takes to compensate:

Starting at **35** instead of 25 to reach $1,000,000 at 65 at 7%:
- Starting at 25: ~$380/month
- Starting at 35: ~$820/month
- Starting at 45: ~$1,920/month

Every decade of delay roughly doubles the required monthly contribution to reach the same goal. The relationship isn't linear — it's compounding working against you.

---

## The Practical Message

If you're reading this in your 20s: the most valuable financial action you can take right now is starting to contribute to a retirement account — even $50 or $100 a month. Open a Roth IRA or contribute to your employer's 401(k). Automate it. Increase it as your income grows.

If you're in your 30s or 40s: start now. Every year still matters. Maximize contributions, take advantage of catch-up contributions after 50, and understand that starting today is dramatically better than starting in five years.

If you're in your 50s: maximize everything. 401(k) catch-up contributions, IRA catch-up, HSA if eligible. Consider delaying Social Security for 8 years beyond your full retirement age to increase benefits by 24-32%. Work one to two additional years if health allows — each year of additional work and contributions at peak earning is worth several years of contributions at younger ages when income was lower.

The right time to start was when you were young. The second-best time is now.
