## 401(k) and Employer Plans

A 401(k) is the most widely available retirement account for American workers — and for people who have access to one with an employer match, it's typically the most important place to start saving for retirement.

---

## What a 401(k) Is

A 401(k) is a retirement savings account offered through your employer. You contribute a percentage of your salary — automatically deducted from each paycheck — and the money is invested in a selection of funds available within the plan.

The key benefit: **tax advantages.** In a traditional 401(k), your contributions are pre-tax — they reduce your taxable income in the year you contribute. The money grows without being taxed until you withdraw it in retirement.

**2024 contribution limits:**
- Employee contribution: up to $23,000/year
- Catch-up contribution (age 50+): additional $7,500 = $30,500 total
- Combined employee + employer contributions: up to $69,000

---

## The Employer Match: Free Money

Many employers match a portion of your contributions. A common structure: the employer matches 50% of your contributions up to 6% of your salary.

If you earn $60,000 and contribute 6% ($3,600), your employer contributes $1,800. That's an instant 50% return before any market return — guaranteed by the match.

**Not contributing enough to capture the full match is leaving free money on the table.** This is among the most commonly cited financial mistakes — and one of the easiest to fix.

The match is typically subject to a vesting schedule. You own your own contributions immediately. The employer match vests over time — often 3-4 years of service. If you leave before vesting, you lose the unvested portion.

---

## Traditional vs Roth 401(k)

Many employers now offer both options:

**Traditional 401(k):** Pre-tax contributions. Reduces your taxable income now. Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.

**Roth 401(k):** After-tax contributions. No upfront deduction. Withdrawals in retirement (after age 59½ and with the account open 5+ years) are completely tax-free, including all growth.

The choice: if you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement than now, Roth is better. If lower, traditional is better. If uncertain — which is most people — Roth has the advantage of locking in known tax rates now rather than betting on unknowable future rates.

Some people split contributions between traditional and Roth 401(k) to hedge between approaches.

---

## Investment Options Inside a 401(k)

You typically choose from a menu of funds selected by your employer. Quality varies widely. Some plans offer excellent, low-cost index funds. Others offer only expensive actively managed funds.

**What to look for:**
- Low expense ratios — under 0.2% is good, under 0.05% is excellent
- Index funds tracking broad markets (S&P 500, total market, international, bond index)
- Target-date funds as a simple all-in-one option

**Target-date funds** are often the best choice for people who don't want to manage allocation: you choose the fund with your approximate retirement year, and it automatically adjusts from aggressive to conservative over time.

If your 401(k) only offers high-expense funds, contribute at least enough to capture the match, then prioritize an IRA for additional contributions where you have access to better options.

---

## Rules and Restrictions

**Early withdrawal penalty:** Withdrawals before age 59½ are subject to a 10% penalty plus income taxes. There are exceptions (disability, substantially equal periodic payments, certain hardships), but early withdrawal is expensive in most cases.

**Required minimum distributions (RMDs):** Starting at age 73, you must withdraw a minimum amount each year from traditional 401(k)s. Roth 401(k)s now have the same no-RMD treatment as Roth IRAs after a 2022 rule change.

**Loans:** Many 401(k) plans allow you to borrow from your balance. This is generally a poor idea — you pay back with after-tax dollars, miss market growth while the money is out, and face full repayment if you leave the employer.

---

## When You Leave a Job

You have options when changing jobs:
1. **Leave it:** The money stays in the old plan, invested and growing. Fine option if the old plan has good funds.
2. **Roll it to your new employer's 401(k):** Consolidates accounts; depends on new plan quality.
3. **Roll it to an IRA:** Often the best option — gives you maximum investment choices and control. Do a direct rollover (fund to fund) to avoid triggering taxes.
4. **Cash it out:** Worst option. Triggers taxes and the 10% penalty. Avoid.

When rolling over, confirm it's a direct rollover (check made out to the new institution, not to you). An indirect rollover (check to you) starts a 60-day clock to deposit into another retirement account — and the plan withholds 20% for taxes, which you must replace from other sources to avoid penalties.
