## Retirement Accounts for the Self-Employed

Self-employed people have no employer 401(k) — but they actually have access to retirement accounts with higher contribution limits than most employees. The trade-off: you have to set them up yourself.

---

## Why This Matters More for the Self-Employed

In regular employment, the 401(k) is often automatic — enrollment happens at onboarding and contributions come straight from payroll. Many employees participate without making a deliberate decision.

Self-employed people have no such structure. Nothing happens automatically. Every dollar saved for retirement requires a deliberate act.

This means self-employed people are statistically undersaved for retirement compared to employees — not because they earn less, but because the friction is higher and the decisions fall entirely to them.

---

## SEP-IRA (Simplified Employee Pension)

The easiest self-employed retirement account to set up and maintain.

**Contribution limit:** Up to 25% of net self-employment income, with a maximum of $69,000 in 2024. This is significantly higher than the $23,000 employee 401(k) limit.

For someone earning $100,000 net from self-employment, the maximum SEP-IRA contribution is approximately $18,587 (the 25% calculation adjusts for self-employment tax deductions).

**Tax treatment:** Contributions are deductible (reduce taxable income). Growth is tax-deferred. Withdrawals taxed as ordinary income. Same rules as a Traditional IRA for distributions.

**Pros:** Simple to set up at any major brokerage. No annual filing requirement (unlike a Solo 401k). Contribution deadline is your tax filing deadline (including extensions), so you can determine your final contribution amount after seeing the year's income.

**Cons:** Can't make Roth contributions. Contribution is limited to 25% of income — at lower income levels, the percentage limit constrains how much you can save. If you have employees, you must contribute the same percentage for them.

---

## Solo 401(k)

For self-employed individuals with no employees (other than a spouse), the Solo 401(k) (also called Individual 401k) offers higher effective contribution limits for many income ranges.

**How contributions work:**

You contribute as both "employee" and "employer":
- **Employee contribution:** Up to $23,000 ($30,500 if 50+) — the same limit as a regular 401(k), but based on your net self-employment income
- **Employer contribution:** Up to 25% of net self-employment income
- **Combined total:** Up to $69,000 ($76,500 if 50+) in 2024

The employee contribution makes a significant difference at lower income levels. An employee earning $50,000 from self-employment can contribute up to $23,000 as the "employee" — nearly half their income — versus about $9,000 in a SEP-IRA.

**Additional features:**
- Can designate contributions as Roth (after-tax): Roth Solo 401(k) allows tax-free growth
- Allows loans from the account (generally not recommended but available)
- Higher contribution potential than SEP-IRA for most self-employed people

**Cons:** Annual IRS Form 5500-EZ required once the account balance exceeds $250,000. Must be set up before December 31 of the year you want to contribute for that year (unlike SEP-IRA, which can be opened at filing time).

## SIMPLE IRA

For self-employed people with employees, the SIMPLE IRA (Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees) is an alternative that's easier to administer than a full 401(k) plan.

**Employee contribution limit:** $16,000 ($19,500 if 50+) in 2024.

**Employer matching:** Required — either match up to 3% of compensation or contribute 2% for all eligible employees.

**Lower limits and mandatory employer contributions** make SIMPLE IRAs less attractive than Solo 401(k)s for solo self-employed people, but appropriate for small businesses with employees.

---

## SEP-IRA vs Solo 401(k): Which One?

For most solo self-employed people, the Solo 401(k) is better at incomes below roughly $200,000-250,000 because the employee contribution allows much higher savings. Above that level, both plans hit the same $69,000 cap.

**Choose SEP-IRA if:**
- Simplicity is paramount
- You don't want to deal with annual filing requirements at higher balances
- You have employees (Solo 401(k) doesn't work)

**Choose Solo 401(k) if:**
- You want to maximize contributions at moderate income levels
- You want Roth contribution options
- You're comfortable with slightly more administrative setup

Both accounts can be opened at Fidelity, Vanguard, or Schwab. The process is similar to opening a regular brokerage account.
