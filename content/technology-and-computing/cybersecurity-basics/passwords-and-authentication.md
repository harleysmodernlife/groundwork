## Passwords and Authentication

Most account compromises happen because of weak passwords, reused passwords, or stolen credentials. Strong authentication is the most high-impact thing you can do for your personal security.

---

## What Makes a Password Weak

**Short:** An 8-character password has about 200 trillion possible combinations using all character types. A modern computer can try billions per second. 8 characters falls quickly offline.

**Simple:** "password," "123456," "qwerty," "letmein" — these are tried first. Any common word, name, or phrase is in dictionary attack wordlists.

**Predictable patterns:** "Password1!" follows a common pattern (word + number + symbol) that attackers know. So does "Jessica1987" or "Dallas#Cowboys".

**Reused:** If you use the same password on multiple sites, one breach compromises everything. Credential stuffing attacks rely entirely on this.

---

## What Makes a Password Strong

**Length is the most important factor.** A 16-character random password is vastly stronger than an 8-character one with all the complexity rules in the world.

**Randomness.** Passwords you invent yourself follow patterns. Truly random strings (generated by software) don't.

**Unique.** Every account gets its own password. A breach of one site compromises exactly one account.

A strong password looks like: `kX9#mQ3$vL8nPw2@` or `correct-horse-battery-staple` (the passphrase approach — long but memorable if the words are truly random).

---

## Password Managers

The practical solution: use a password manager. It generates and stores unique, strong passwords for every site. You remember one strong master password; the manager handles the rest.

**How it works:**
- The manager stores your passwords in an encrypted vault
- You unlock the vault with your master password (some also support biometrics)
- When you visit a login page, it fills in the credentials automatically
- When you create an account, it generates a strong unique password

**Good options:**
- **1Password:** Paid, excellent cross-platform support
- **Bitwarden:** Free and open-source, solid option
- **Dashlane:** Free tier available
- **Built-in managers:** Google Password Manager, iCloud Keychain — free and convenient but tied to their ecosystems

**What to put in it:** Every account, starting with the most sensitive (email, banking, healthcare). One compromise of an important account costs more than the effort of setting up a password manager.

**One concern:** The master password. Make it long and memorable. Write it down and store it somewhere physically secure. If you forget it and the company has no recovery option (some are zero-knowledge), you may lose access.

## Two-Factor Authentication (2FA)

A password is something you know. Two-factor authentication adds a second factor — something you have (phone) or something you are (biometric).

Even if an attacker gets your password, they can't log in without the second factor.

**Types of 2FA:**

**SMS codes:** A text message with a 6-digit code. Better than nothing, but weakest form — attackers can SIM-swap (trick your carrier into transferring your number) to intercept codes.

**Authenticator apps (TOTP):** Apps like Google Authenticator, Authy, or built-in OS options generate time-based codes that change every 30 seconds. Not vulnerable to SIM swapping. Enable this when sites offer it.

**Push notifications:** The service sends a notification to your phone asking you to approve the login. You tap "approve" or "deny." Convenient, but vulnerable to "MFA fatigue" attacks — repeatedly sending requests until you accidentally tap approve.

**Hardware security keys:** Physical keys (YubiKey, Google Titan) that you plug in or tap to a phone. The strongest form. Completely immune to phishing — the key verifies the site's identity cryptographically and won't authenticate to a fake site.

**Passkeys (WebAuthn):** A newer standard that replaces passwords entirely. Works like a hardware key but using the secure element in your phone or computer. Supported by major platforms now (Google, Apple, Microsoft). Phishing-resistant.

### Priority

1. Enable 2FA on every account that supports it — especially email, financial accounts, and anything that can be used to reset other passwords
2. Use an authenticator app, not SMS, if you have the choice
3. For highest-value accounts, consider a hardware key

---

## Your Email Is the Master Key

Your email account is the most important account you have. It's used to reset every other password. If an attacker gets into your email, they can take over everything else — bank accounts, social media, work accounts.

Secure your email first:
- Strong unique password (in your password manager)
- 2FA enabled, ideally with an authenticator app or hardware key

---

## Password Reset Questions

"What was your mother's maiden name?" "What city were you born in?" — these answers are often findable online or guessable.

If you have to use security questions, treat them like passwords: give false, random answers and store them in your password manager. "What was your first pet's name?" → "X7#mKqP" (stored in the manager).

---

## When Passwords Are Compromised

**Check for breaches:** HaveIBeenPwned.com (haveibeenpwned.com) lets you enter your email address and see if it appears in known data breaches. If it does, change that password immediately.

**After a breach notification:** Change the password for that site. If you reused it anywhere (which you shouldn't be doing), change it everywhere. Check whether any suspicious activity occurred.

**Signs your account may be compromised:** Login notifications from unfamiliar locations, password reset emails you didn't request, messages you didn't send, accounts created from your email address.
