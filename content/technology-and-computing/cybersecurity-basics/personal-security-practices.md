## Personal Security Practices

Good security doesn't require technical expertise. Most of what protects you comes down to consistent habits. These practices address the vast majority of real threats to ordinary people.

---

## Tier 1: Do These First

These have the highest impact. If you do nothing else, do these.

### Use a Password Manager

Every account gets a unique, strong password. You can't memorize them — that's the point. A password manager generates and stores them. You remember one strong master password.

Setup takes an hour. It eliminates the risk of credential stuffing (which relies on reused passwords) and weak passwords. Most people who take this step consider it one of the best things they've done for their security.

See the Passwords and Authentication lesson for options.

### Enable 2FA on Critical Accounts

At minimum: your email, your bank, your password manager, and any account used to sign in to other services.

Use an authenticator app (Google Authenticator, Authy, built-in iOS/Android option) rather than SMS when possible.

Your email is the priority — it can reset every other account.

### Keep Software Updated

Software updates frequently include security patches. Operating systems, browsers, phone apps, router firmware. Enable automatic updates wherever you can.

The vulnerability was just publicly disclosed. Now every attacker knows about it. If you don't update, you're running known-vulnerable software.

## Tier 2: Strong Habits

### Think Before You Click

The most common attack vector is phishing — getting you to click a link or open an attachment. Before clicking:

- Does this make sense? Did I expect this email?
- Does the sender's address actually match who they claim to be?
- Hover over the link (without clicking) — does the URL match where it claims to go?
- Is there unusual urgency or a threat?

When in doubt, go to the website directly by typing the address yourself, or call the organization using a number you found independently.

### Don't Trust USB Drives You Didn't Buy Yourself

A USB drive found in a parking lot, given away at a conference, or received unsolicited is a potential attack vector. Plugging in an unknown USB can autorun malicious code on some systems.

### Lock Your Devices

Use a PIN, password, fingerprint, or face unlock on your phone and computer. Lock when you walk away. This prevents physical access attacks — someone picking up your unlocked device.

On Windows: Win+L to lock. On Mac: Ctrl+Cmd+Q or close the lid (if set to lock on sleep).

### Use HTTPS

Ensure websites handling sensitive information use HTTPS (the padlock in the address bar). Most browsers now warn about HTTP sites or block mixed content. Don't enter passwords or payment information on HTTP sites.

## Tier 3: Worthwhile Extras

### Back Up Your Data

Ransomware encrypts your data and demands payment. The best defense is a backup that attackers can't reach. Follow the 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies of data, on 2 different media types, with 1 offsite.

Practically: use cloud backup (iCloud, Google Drive, OneDrive, Backblaze) plus an external drive you occasionally update and disconnect.

### Review App Permissions

Apps on your phone request access to your location, contacts, camera, microphone, and more. Regularly review which apps have which permissions.

Does a flashlight app need access to your contacts? Does a game need your microphone? Revoke permissions that don't make sense.

### Be Selective About What You Share Online

Information posted publicly can be used for targeted phishing, password reset questions, or identity theft. Your date of birth, full name, home city, employer, and other details combine to create a profile.

This doesn't mean don't use social media — it means be aware of what's public and what you're sharing.

### Use a Privacy-Respecting Browser and Search Engine

Firefox and Brave have stronger privacy defaults than Chrome. DuckDuckGo and Brave Search don't build search profiles tied to your identity. These don't dramatically change your security against targeted attacks, but reduce the passive data collection happening by default.

### Email Hygiene

Use a different email address for sensitive accounts (banking, health) than for casual signups and newsletters. If your newsletter email is in a breach, it doesn't expose your banking login.

Services like SimpleLogin and Apple's Hide My Email generate unique email addresses that forward to your real address — data breaches expose the alias, not your real email.

## Social Engineering Defense

The most sophisticated technical defense fails if someone can just call you and ask for your password. Train yourself to be appropriately skeptical:

- **Legitimate organizations do not ask for your password.** Ever. Full stop.
- **Verify unexpected requests independently.** If someone calls claiming to be from your bank's fraud department, hang up and call the number on the back of your card.
- **Urgency is a manipulation tactic.** "Your account will be closed in 2 hours" is designed to prevent you from thinking clearly. Slow down.
- **It's okay to be rude to fraudsters.** Hanging up on a phone scammer is appropriate. You don't owe them politeness.

## What Matters Least

Security theater — steps that feel secure but don't actually protect you:

- **Regularly changing passwords** for no specific reason: a pointless practice that leads to weaker passwords (password1 → password2 → password3). Change passwords when there's a reason (breach, suspicious activity).
- **Extremely complex password requirements** that lead users to write passwords down: counterproductive.
- **Security questions with real answers:** The answers are often guessable or findable. Use false answers stored in your password manager.
- **Antivirus alone:** Important but not sufficient. Most compromises happen through social engineering and credential theft, not malware your antivirus would catch.

---

## The Mindset

You're not trying to be unhackable. You're trying to be harder to compromise than the next person, because most attackers go for easy targets. You're also minimizing the damage when (not if) something you use is breached.

Good security is about reducing risk, not eliminating it. The practices in this lesson, consistently applied, protect you against the vast majority of realistic threats.
