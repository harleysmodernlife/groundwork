## AI and Work

AI is changing what jobs look like, which tasks get automated, and what skills matter. This is happening now — not in some speculative future. Understanding what AI is actually doing to work helps you make better decisions about your career and how you work.

---

## What's Actually Being Automated

AI doesn't automate jobs — it automates tasks. Most jobs are bundles of tasks, some of which AI can assist with and some of which it can't (yet).

**Tasks AI does well:**
- Generating first drafts of common documents
- Answering frequently asked questions using existing knowledge
- Classifying and routing information (customer support tickets, emails)
- Data extraction from unstructured text
- Translating documents
- Writing and explaining code
- Summarizing documents
- Creating images and graphics from descriptions

**Tasks AI does poorly:**
- Novel physical work in unpredictable environments (most skilled trades)
- Tasks requiring genuine relationship and trust
- High-stakes decisions requiring accountability and judgment
- Work requiring deep expertise in specific context (your specific organization, your specific client situation)
- Work requiring real-time response to unexpected situations

The pattern: AI is strongest on well-defined, language-based tasks with clear patterns in training data. It's weakest on physical work, novel situations, and tasks requiring accountability.

---

## Jobs Under Pressure

Some roles are seeing significant portions of their task load taken over by AI:

**Writing and content creation:** Marketing copy, boilerplate content, product descriptions, social posts. AI can produce adequate versions of routine content faster than humans. Writers who produce highly distinctive, expert-level, or strategically important content are less affected than those who produce generic content at volume.

**Customer support (Tier 1):** Answering common questions, processing simple requests. AI chatbots handle more of this. Complex, escalated issues still go to humans.

**Data entry and basic data processing:** Extracting structured information from documents, classifying records.

**Basic code:** Boilerplate, simple functions, straightforward implementations. AI writes significant portions of routine code. Software development overall hasn't contracted — but what developers spend time on is shifting.

**Routine legal and administrative work:** Document review, contract redlining, basic research. AI assists significantly with high-volume, pattern-based work.

---

## Jobs Less Affected

**Skilled trades:** Plumbers, electricians, HVAC technicians, carpenters, welders. Physical work in varied, unpredictable environments. AI doesn't fix pipes. These trades are also in high demand and often pay well.

**Direct care:** Nursing, physical therapy, home health aides, childcare. Require physical presence, relationship, and judgment. AI assists but doesn't replace.

**Teaching (at its core):** Managing a classroom, reading individual students, building relationships with young people. AI tutoring supplements but doesn't replace effective teachers.

**Strategic and leadership roles:** Setting direction, navigating ambiguity, building organizations, leading people. AI can inform decisions; it doesn't make them.

**Sales (relationship-based):** Complex sales involving trust, negotiation, and long-term relationships. AI handles prospecting and routine outreach; relationship-intensive sales remain human.

## What AI Changes About Most Jobs

More often than replacing jobs outright, AI changes what people spend time on.

**Before AI:** A marketing manager writes five variations of an ad, spends a day on it.
**After AI:** A marketing manager generates 50 variations in an hour, spends time selecting and refining the best ones, and strategic direction.

The task of writing became faster. Time shifted toward judgment, curation, and strategy.

This pattern repeats: AI handles volume; humans handle judgment. AI handles routine; humans handle exceptions and edge cases. AI handles first drafts; humans handle refinement and accountability.

The skills that become more valuable:
- Judgment about what AI output is good or bad
- Ability to direct AI effectively (knowing what to ask for)
- Domain expertise to catch AI errors
- Communication and relationship skills
- Creative and strategic thinking
- Accountability and ethical decision-making

## Learning and Adaptation

The jobs and tasks where AI helps are changing rapidly. What AI can't do today, it may be able to do in two years. What's useful to know changes alongside the technology.

**Practical implications:**
- Learn to use AI tools in your field — people who use AI effectively will outperform those who don't
- Develop skills that complement AI rather than compete with it — judgment, expertise, relationships
- Be realistic about which tasks in your job are automatable and develop the rest
- Stay current — the landscape shifts quickly enough that checking in on AI capabilities every six months matters

**What to avoid:**
- Assuming your field is immune — most knowledge work is being touched by AI
- Assuming your field will be fully automated imminently — most claims about total automation have been overstated
- Ignoring AI entirely — falling behind on tools your peers are using is a real competitive disadvantage

## AI Won't Make Expertise Worthless

A common fear: AI will make human expertise worthless because AI can produce expert-level output. This misunderstands what expertise is.

Expertise isn't just producing outputs — it's knowing which questions to ask, evaluating whether an output is right, understanding why something works or fails, navigating situations the AI has never seen, taking responsibility for decisions, and building trust with other humans.

AI that can write passable code doesn't make senior engineers worthless — it makes senior engineers who can direct and evaluate AI-generated code more productive than those who can't. AI that can draft a legal contract doesn't make lawyers worthless — it changes what lawyers spend their time on, not whether expert legal judgment is needed.

The question isn't "can AI produce something that looks like this output?" It's "who is accountable for the outcome, and who has the expertise to ensure the outcome is right?"
