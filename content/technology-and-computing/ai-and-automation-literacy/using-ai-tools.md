## Using AI Tools Effectively

AI tools are widely available and genuinely useful — if you know how to use them well. Using them poorly produces mediocre results. Using them well can multiply your productivity for certain tasks. The skill is knowing what they're good for, how to direct them, and how to evaluate what they produce.

---

## What AI Tools Actually Exist

**General-purpose AI assistants (LLM-based):**
- ChatGPT (OpenAI)
- Claude (Anthropic)
- Gemini (Google)

These accept text (and often images) and produce text responses. Used for writing, analysis, coding, research, answering questions.

**Image generation:**
- Midjourney, DALL-E 3 (via ChatGPT), Stable Diffusion, Adobe Firefly
- Generate images from text descriptions

**Code assistance:**
- GitHub Copilot: suggests code as you type, inside your editor
- Cursor, Windsurf: AI-integrated code editors
- LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude) via chat

**Audio/video:**
- Whisper (OpenAI): speech-to-text transcription, very accurate
- ElevenLabs: text-to-speech voice synthesis
- Various video generation tools

**AI in existing software:**
- Microsoft Copilot (in Word, Excel, Outlook, Teams)
- Google Workspace AI features
- Adobe Firefly (in Photoshop, Illustrator)
- Notion AI, Grammarly, and many other productivity tools

## Where AI Tools Genuinely Help

**First drafts:** The blank page problem. AI can produce a rough draft that you then edit, improve, and make your own. This is faster than starting from scratch even if heavy editing is needed.

**Brainstorming:** Generate 20 ideas for a product name, 10 angles for an essay, 5 approaches to a problem. Many will be mediocre; a few might be worth developing.

**Editing and rewriting:** Paste in your draft. Ask for it to be more concise, more formal, clearer, shorter. Ask what's unclear or weak.

**Explaining unfamiliar things:** "Explain this concept like I'm not an expert." Often excellent — patient, clear, adjustable to your level.

**Summarizing:** Paste a long document and ask for key points. Saves time, though you should verify the summary doesn't drop critical nuances.

**Code:** Write boilerplate, explain unfamiliar code, suggest how to implement something, debug errors. Works best for common patterns; less reliable for novel problems.

**Research starting point:** Not a final source — AI hallucinations mean you can't trust specific facts without verification. But a useful starting point for knowing what to look up and what questions to ask.

---

## Where AI Tools Don't Help (or Hurt)

**Anything requiring verified factual accuracy:** Don't trust AI for specific statistics, citations, quotes, dates, or facts without verification. It will invent plausible-sounding details.

**Legal, medical, and financial decisions:** AI can provide general information. It cannot replace professional advice specific to your situation. An AI might give you general information about a legal concept correctly but fail to flag that your specific jurisdiction has a different rule.

**Highly specialized domains:** AI performs better on topics well-represented in training data. Cutting-edge research, niche fields, specialized technical problems — reliability drops.

**Creative work you want to be distinctly yours:** AI writing is often competent but generic. If your goal is a piece that reflects your specific perspective and voice, AI-generated text needs substantial editing to get there.

## Prompting That Works

**State what you want specifically:**
- Weak: "Write about climate change."
- Better: "Write a 500-word explanation of the scientific consensus on climate change for a general audience with no science background. Use simple language, avoid jargon, and end with two things an individual can do."

**State the format:**
- "Give me a bulleted list of..."
- "Write this as a formal letter."
- "Format this as a table with columns for X, Y, Z."
- "Keep the response under 200 words."

**Give it a role or context:**
- "You are reviewing this resume as a hiring manager in the software industry. What are the weaknesses?"
- "Explain this to me as if I'm a smart 12-year-old."

**Iterate:**
- "Make it more concise."
- "The second paragraph is confusing — rewrite it."
- "Give me three alternative versions of the opening sentence."
- "That's too formal. Make it sound more conversational."

**Provide examples:**
- "Here's an email I wrote that I liked the tone of: [example]. Write a similar email for this situation: [situation]."

## Verifying AI Output

Anything an AI produces that you plan to use for something important should be verified.

- **Facts, statistics, citations:** Look them up independently. Don't cite a source an AI gave you without verifying it exists and says what the AI claims.
- **Code:** Test it. AI-generated code often has bugs, edge cases, or uses outdated APIs.
- **Legal/medical/financial claims:** Consult a professional. AI can point you in a direction; it cannot substitute for specific expert advice.
- **Logic and reasoning:** Read it critically. A confident-sounding argument can be wrong.

The safest approach: use AI to help you learn and draft, but hold the final responsibility for accuracy yourself.

---

## AI and Cognitive Offloading

There's a real risk: using AI too heavily for tasks that build important skills. If you always ask AI to write your emails, you may not develop your writing. If you always ask AI to solve coding problems, you may not learn to program.

The analogy to calculators is apt but imperfect. Calculators handle arithmetic that no one genuinely needs to do by hand anymore. But writing, reasoning, and judgment are different — they develop through practice, and offloading them entirely stunts development.

The practical question: are you using AI to accomplish a task faster, or to avoid developing a skill that will serve you? The former is smart productivity. The latter is a trap.
