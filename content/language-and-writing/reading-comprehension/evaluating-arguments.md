Reading critically means reading evaluatively — not just understanding what an author argues, but assessing whether the argument is sound. This requires identifying the claim, examining the evidence, and checking the logic that connects them.

---

## 1. The Components of an Argument

Before evaluating an argument, identify its parts:

- **Claim**: The position the author is taking — what they want you to believe
- **Evidence**: The data, examples, and facts offered to support the claim
- **Reasoning (warrant)**: The logic connecting evidence to claim — why this evidence counts as proof of this claim
- **Assumptions**: Unstated beliefs the argument depends on
- **Counterarguments**: Opposing views the author addresses (or avoids)

Many arguments present evidence clearly but leave the reasoning implicit. Part of critical reading is making that reasoning explicit so you can evaluate it.

---

## 2. Is the Claim Clear and Arguable?

**Weak claims** are too vague to evaluate (*"we should do better on climate"*), too broad to prove (*"technology has always changed society"*), or state something that isn't debatable (*"many people are affected by poverty"*).

**Strong claims** are specific, arguable, and falsifiable.

Ask:
- What exactly is the author claiming?
- Is this a claim of fact, value, or policy?
- Is it clear enough to be proven or disproven?
- Could a reasonable person disagree?

---

## 3. Is the Evidence Credible and Relevant?

**Credibility**: Where does the evidence come from? Who produced it?
- Peer-reviewed research, government data, and expert testimony are generally more credible than anecdotes, partisan advocacy, or unattributed claims.
- Consider the source's potential conflicts of interest.

**Relevance**: Does this evidence actually support this specific claim?
- Evidence that the economy grew in the 1990s doesn't prove that a specific policy caused the growth.
- Evidence about one population doesn't necessarily apply to another.

**Sufficiency**: Is there enough evidence to justify the claim?
- A single study, case, or example is rarely sufficient for a broad generalization.
- Look for whether the author draws a conclusion larger than the evidence supports.

**Currency**: Is the evidence recent enough to still apply?

---

## 4. Is the Logic Sound?

Logic is what connects evidence to claim. Common errors in reasoning:

### Hasty Generalization
Drawing a broad conclusion from too little evidence.
*"My neighbor's electric car broke down twice — EVs are unreliable."*

### False Cause (Post Hoc)
Assuming causation from correlation or sequence.
*"Crime rates fell after the new mayor took office, so the mayor reduced crime."* (Many other factors may explain the change.)

### Slippery Slope
Assuming one action will inevitably lead to extreme consequences without showing why.
*"If we allow this minor regulation, we'll end up with complete government control."*

### Ad Hominem
Attacking the person making the argument rather than the argument itself.
*"You can't trust his climate research — he drives an SUV."*

### Straw Man
Misrepresenting the opposing view to make it easier to attack.
*"Those who oppose this tax reform want to punish success."*

### Appeal to Authority
Treating an authority's opinion as definitive when the authority isn't expert in the specific domain.

### False Dichotomy
Presenting only two options when more exist.
*"You're either with us or against us."*

---

## 5. What Is the Author Leaving Out?

Critical reading asks not just what is there, but what isn't.

- Does the author acknowledge counterarguments? Ignore them? Misrepresent them?
- Are there obvious objections that go unaddressed?
- Is the evidence selective — presenting only data that supports the conclusion while omitting contradicting evidence?
- Whose perspective is absent from the account?

**Cherry-picking** — selecting evidence that supports a conclusion while ignoring contradictory evidence — is one of the most common failures in persuasive writing.

---

## 6. What Are the Unstated Assumptions?

Every argument rests on assumptions — beliefs the author holds that they haven't proven (or sometimes even acknowledged). If an assumption is false, the argument that depends on it fails.

*Argument: "We should teach financial literacy in schools so people make better financial decisions."*
*Unstated assumption: Financial literacy education actually improves financial outcomes.*

If that assumption is false (and there's evidence it often is), the argument fails even if the goal is laudable.

---

## 7. Overall Assessment

After examining claim, evidence, logic, omissions, and assumptions, ask:
- Does the evidence adequately support the claim?
- Is the reasoning free of major logical errors?
- Has the author dealt honestly with complexity and disagreement?
- What remains unproven?

A conclusion can be correct even if the argument for it is weak. Conversely, a well-structured argument can reach a wrong conclusion if it relies on false evidence. Evaluate the argument on its merits, separate from whether you happen to agree with the conclusion.

---

## Summary

Evaluating arguments means examining the claim (is it clear and arguable?), the evidence (is it credible, relevant, and sufficient?), and the logic (does the reasoning connect evidence to claim without fallacy?). Critical reading also attends to what's omitted — counterarguments, contradictory evidence, absent perspectives — and to unstated assumptions the argument depends on. Strong arguments survive this scrutiny; weak ones reveal their gaps.
