What you do before you start reading shapes how much you understand and retain. Pre-reading strategies activate relevant background knowledge, establish purpose, and create the mental framework that makes comprehension more efficient.

---

## 1. Why Pre-Reading Matters

Comprehension research consistently shows that readers who preview material before reading understand and retain more than those who dive in without preparation. This isn't because previewing provides the answers — it's because it activates schemas.

A **schema** is your existing mental framework for a topic — everything you already know and believe about it. When your schema is activated before reading, new information connects to something, rather than arriving in a vacuum. Connected information is remembered; isolated information is lost.

Pre-reading also sets up **expectations** — a sense of what you're about to encounter. When expectations are met, reading confirms and deepens understanding. When expectations are violated (the text says something different from what you expected), that surprise is itself a form of engagement that aids retention.

---

## 2. Preview the Text Structure

Before reading, survey the material:

**For articles and essays:**
- Read the title — what does it promise?
- Read the subheadings — what topics does the piece cover?
- Read the first paragraph — how is the piece framed?
- Read the last paragraph — where does the piece arrive?
- Note any bold terms, pull quotes, or figures

**For books:**
- Read the table of contents — how is the material organized?
- Read the preface or introduction — what is the author's purpose?
- Skim chapter summaries if available

This takes 3–5 minutes and significantly improves the quality of the reading that follows.

---

## 3. Identify Your Purpose

Reading with a purpose makes you a more active reader. Ask: *Why am I reading this?*

- To get a general overview (skim more)
- To find specific information (search reading)
- To understand a complex argument in depth (slow, careful reading)
- To evaluate the source's credibility and argument
- To take notes for a project

Your purpose determines how you read, how much detail you need, and what you pay attention to.

---

## 4. Activate Background Knowledge

Ask yourself: *What do I already know about this topic?*

Spend 1–2 minutes brainstorming before beginning:
- What do I know about this subject?
- What have I read before in this area?
- What questions or uncertainties do I have?

Even writing down five words related to the topic primes your brain to notice relevant connections while reading.

---

## 5. Generate Pre-Reading Questions

Turn titles and headings into questions you want the reading to answer.

*Heading: "The Causes of the French Revolution"*
*Question: What were the main causes? Were they primarily economic, political, or social?*

Generating questions before reading creates a purpose for reading each section — you're looking for answers. This is the "Question" step of the SQ3R method.

Good pre-reading questions come from:
- Headings and subheadings turned into questions
- Terms or concepts in the title you want to understand better
- Genuine curiosity: *What do I actually want to know here?*

---

## 6. Consider the Source

Before reading, briefly note:
- **Who wrote this?** What is their background and expertise?
- **Where was it published?** A peer-reviewed journal, a newspaper, a blog?
- **When was it written?** Is it recent enough for your purposes?
- **Why might they have written this?** To inform, persuade, sell, entertain?

These questions don't need full answers before you begin — they're awareness-setting. You'll answer them more fully during and after reading.

---

## 7. For Difficult Texts

If you're about to read something challenging (technical, unfamiliar, dense), extra preparation helps:

- **Read easier background material first**: Find a simpler explanation of the core concepts before tackling the difficult version
- **Build vocabulary**: Look up key terms you encounter in the preview
- **Find context**: Who is this author? When was this written? What debate is it part of?

Going into a difficult text cold is the least efficient approach. Even ten minutes of background research can make a dense reading comprehensible.

---

## Summary

Pre-reading strategies — previewing structure, identifying purpose, activating background knowledge, generating questions, and considering the source — significantly improve comprehension and retention. They work by activating relevant schemas and creating mental frameworks that new information can attach to. The time invested in pre-reading is more than recovered in reading efficiency and understanding. Most skilled readers do some version of these steps automatically; deliberate practice makes them habit.
