Every piece of writing exists for a reason. Understanding why an author wrote something — what they wanted to accomplish — is essential for reading critically rather than passively. When you know the author's purpose, you can better evaluate whether they achieved it and whether their methods are trustworthy.

---

## 1. The Three Main Purposes

Authors generally write to do one or more of the following:

### To Inform (Expository)

The primary goal is to share accurate information, explain how something works, or describe a topic objectively. The author positions themselves as a source of knowledge, and the measure of success is whether the reader comes away better informed.

*Examples: Textbooks, encyclopedia entries, news reports, how-to guides, scientific papers*

**Characteristics**: Third-person perspective, factual tone, sources cited, definitions provided, minimal emotional appeal.

### To Persuade (Argumentative)

The primary goal is to change what readers believe or do. The author takes a position and marshals evidence and reasoning to move the reader toward that position.

*Examples: Op-eds, political speeches, advocacy writing, debate essays, some literary criticism*

**Characteristics**: Clear thesis, supporting evidence, counterarguments addressed, appeals to logic and sometimes emotion, conclusion that reinforces the position.

### To Entertain (Narrative/Literary)

The primary goal is to engage, move, or give pleasure to the reader — through story, humor, beauty of language, or emotional resonance.

*Examples: Fiction, poetry, memoir, personal essays, humor writing*

**Characteristics**: Character and plot, sensory and emotional detail, focus on experience rather than argument, voice and style as primary vehicles.

---

## 2. Purposes Overlap

Real writing rarely has only one purpose. A narrative essay about a personal experience may primarily entertain but also implicitly argue for a particular view of the world. A persuasive essay may use storytelling to make its argument more compelling. A scientific paper primarily informs but also implicitly argues for the credibility of its methods.

Ask not just "what is the primary purpose?" but "what secondary purposes are operating here?"

---

## 3. Purpose Shapes Choices

Understanding purpose explains why authors make the choices they do.

**Word choice**: Persuasive writing often uses charged, emotional language. Informational writing prefers neutral, precise terms. A persuasive writer who says *"the reckless deregulation of an industry"* is making a different choice than an informational writer who says *"changes to industry regulations."*

**Selection of detail**: Every author selects what to include and what to exclude. An author whose purpose is to persuade will include evidence that supports their position. An author whose purpose is to inform aims for balance. The selection reveals purpose.

**Structure**: Informational texts often organize by categories, sequences, or cause-and-effect. Persuasive texts build toward conclusions. Narrative texts create tension and resolution.

**Tone**: A text written to comfort sounds different from one written to alarm — even if they cover the same topic.

---

## 4. Identifying Purpose: Questions to Ask

- Why did this author write this?
- Who is the intended audience?
- What does the author want the reader to think, feel, or do after reading?
- Is the author primarily sharing facts, making an argument, or telling a story?
- What effect does the author seem to be going for?
- Does the author state their purpose explicitly, or must you infer it?

---

## 5. Evaluating Whether Purpose Is Achieved

Once you've identified the purpose, evaluate whether the author achieved it:

**For informative writing**: Is the information accurate? Complete? Organized for clarity? Are sources credible?

**For persuasive writing**: Is the argument logical? Is the evidence sufficient? Are counterarguments addressed honestly? Does the author appeal to emotion in ways that are appropriate, or in ways that manipulate?

**For entertaining writing**: Does it engage? Are the characters or experiences vivid? Does the language create an effect?

---

## 6. Hidden Purposes

Some authors don't announce their purpose — and it may differ from what appears on the surface. A news article may be primarily informative but shaped by an editorial perspective that influences which sources are quoted and which facts are foregrounded. A "report" may be advocacy dressed in neutral language.

Critical readers ask: *Whose interests does this serve? What is left out? Is the stated purpose the whole story?*

---

## Summary

Authors write to inform, persuade, or entertain — often with overlapping purposes. Understanding purpose explains word choice, selection of detail, structure, and tone. Identifying purpose requires asking why the author wrote this, who the intended audience is, and what effect the author is trying to produce. Critical reading goes further: evaluating whether the purpose was achieved and whether unstated purposes are shaping the text.
