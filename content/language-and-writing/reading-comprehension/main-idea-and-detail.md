Every piece of writing has a main idea — the central point the author is making — and supporting details that develop, explain, or prove that idea. Distinguishing between the two is fundamental to reading comprehension and critical analysis.

---

## 1. What Is the Main Idea?

The **main idea** is the most important point of a text, the claim the author most wants the reader to take away. It is what the whole piece is about — not just what one section covers.

The main idea:
- Applies to the entire text (or entire paragraph), not just part of it
- Is the author's *point*, not just the *topic*
- Can usually be expressed as a complete sentence

**Topic vs. Main Idea**

These are not the same thing:
- *Topic*: What the text is about (a noun or noun phrase) — *climate change*
- *Main idea*: What the text says about the topic — *Coastal cities must begin preparing for sea level rise now, even under optimistic projections.*

The topic names a subject. The main idea makes a claim about it.

---

## 2. Finding the Main Idea

**In paragraphs**: The main idea is often expressed in the **topic sentence** — usually the first sentence, sometimes the last. If no single sentence states it directly, synthesize the paragraph's content into a single sentence yourself.

**In longer texts**: The main idea is often stated in:
- The introduction (especially the last paragraph of the intro)
- The thesis statement (in argumentative and academic writing)
- The conclusion (which often restates and expands the main claim)
- The title and subheadings (which preview the structure)

Not all texts have an explicit main idea — especially creative writing, literary essays, and narrative journalism. In these cases, you infer the main idea from the sum of the parts.

---

## 3. Major vs. Minor Details

**Supporting details** are the information the author uses to develop the main idea. Not all details are equally important.

**Major details** directly support the main idea:
- Key reasons, evidence, or examples that prove the central claim
- Information you couldn't remove without weakening the argument

**Minor details** support major details rather than the main idea directly:
- Specific statistics that flesh out a major point
- Anecdotes that illustrate but aren't the core argument
- Examples within examples

When taking notes or summarizing, focus on major details. Minor details add texture but aren't essential to understanding the argument's structure.

---

## 4. Distinguishing Main Idea from Detail

A common confusion: treating a specific claim as the main idea when it's actually a supporting detail.

Test: Ask whether the point supports the author's larger argument or whether it *is* the larger argument. A specific statistic or example almost always supports — it is a detail. The conclusion the author draws from that statistic may be the main idea.

Example:
*Text: "The average American spends 4.5 hours per day on their smartphone. Teenagers average 7 hours. These figures represent a dramatic shift in how time is allocated, one that has measurable effects on attention, sleep, and face-to-face interaction."*

- **Detail**: Americans spend 4.5 hours/day on smartphones; teens spend 7 hours
- **Main idea**: This shift in time allocation measurably affects attention, sleep, and social interaction

---

## 5. Implied Main Ideas

Sometimes the main idea isn't stated — you have to infer it. This is common in:
- Descriptive writing
- Narrative and creative essays
- Journalism that presents evidence and lets readers conclude

To identify an implied main idea:
1. List the major details
2. Ask: What do these details have in common?
3. Ask: What conclusion do they point toward?
4. Express that conclusion as a statement

*Example: A paragraph describes declining school enrollment, rising dropout rates, teacher shortages, and budget cuts. No single sentence states the main idea explicitly. But the implied main idea is: The public education system in this region is in serious trouble.*

---

## 6. Main Idea in Different Text Types

- **Argumentative texts**: Main idea = the thesis (a debatable claim)
- **Expository texts**: Main idea = the central topic statement (what will be explained)
- **Narrative texts**: Main idea = the theme or insight the story conveys
- **Descriptive texts**: Main idea = the dominant impression or controlling idea

The form changes; the principle doesn't: there is a central point, and everything else serves it.

---

## Summary

The main idea is what the text as a whole is trying to communicate — a complete claim about a topic, not just the topic itself. Supporting details develop the main idea; major details support it directly, minor details support the major details. Identifying the main idea requires distinguishing the author's central point from the specific evidence used to support it. When no main idea is stated explicitly, inference from the pattern of details reveals it.
