Reading actively means engaging with a text rather than moving your eyes over it passively. Most people read to finish — to get through the pages. Active readers read to understand and question. The difference in comprehension between these two approaches is substantial.

---

## 1. What Active Reading Is

Active reading is a set of deliberate strategies that keep you mentally engaged with the material:
- Predicting what's coming
- Questioning what's being claimed
- Connecting new information to what you already know
- Monitoring your own understanding
- Recording key ideas as you go

It's not about reading more slowly — it's about reading more intentionally. Some active reading strategies save time by preventing you from having to re-read material you processed passively the first time.

---

## 2. Before You Read: Preview

Before reading a text, spend a few minutes previewing it:
- Read the title, headings, and subheadings
- Look at any bolded terms, images, or charts
- Read the first and last paragraph
- Read the first sentence of each section

Previewing activates background knowledge, sets up expectations, and helps you read with a sense of structure. You know where you're going before you start.

For longer texts, previewing the table of contents and any summary or abstract serves the same function.

---

## 3. Annotating as You Read

**Annotation** — writing in the margins or recording notes — is one of the most powerful active reading strategies. It forces you to process the text in your own words, which dramatically improves retention.

What to annotate:
- **Mark the main idea** of each section (in your own words)
- **Question** anything you don't understand or find suspect: *?? What's the evidence for this?*
- **Connect** to other ideas: *This contradicts what the previous chapter said.*
- **Respond**: *Disagree — the author ignores the counterfactual.*
- **Define** unfamiliar terms
- **Highlight sparingly** — if everything is highlighted, nothing is

If you're reading on a device or using a library copy, use sticky notes, a reading journal, or an annotation app.

---

## 4. Questioning the Text

Reading with questions changes how you engage with material. Useful questions:

- **What is the author's main point?**
- **What evidence is given?**
- **What assumptions does the argument depend on?**
- **What is left out?**
- **Does this claim follow from the evidence?**
- **Who wrote this and why? What's their stake?**
- **What would a critic say?**

You don't need to answer every question immediately. Sometimes the best active reading is generating good questions that the text (or further research) will eventually answer.

---

## 5. The SQ3R Method

**SQ3R** is a structured active reading technique used widely in academic settings:

1. **Survey**: Preview the text (titles, headings, intro, summary)
2. **Question**: Turn each heading into a question — *"Early Civilizations" → "What characterized early civilizations?"*
3. **Read**: Read to answer your questions
4. **Recite**: After each section, close the text and answer your questions from memory
5. **Review**: At the end, review the whole text and your notes

The recite step — active recall without looking — is supported by strong evidence for improving long-term retention compared to re-reading.

---

## 6. Monitoring Comprehension

Active readers notice when they're confused and do something about it:

- **Re-read** the confusing passage (sometimes slow down is all that's needed)
- **Look up** unfamiliar vocabulary or references
- **Read ahead** — sometimes confusion resolves when you see where the argument is going
- **Summarize** what you've read so far to locate the gap in understanding

The passive reader assumes confusion is inevitable and keeps moving. The active reader treats confusion as information: *I don't understand this yet* — and then figures out why.

---

## 7. After You Read: Consolidate

Reading without reflection often means forgetting most of it within 24 hours.

After reading:
- **Summarize** the main argument in your own words (one paragraph is enough)
- **Write down the most important 3–5 things you learned**
- **Connect** the reading to other things you know
- **Identify lingering questions**

Even five minutes of post-reading reflection significantly improves retention compared to moving directly to the next thing.

---

## Summary

Active reading is deliberate, questioning engagement with a text — as opposed to passive eye movement. It includes previewing before reading, annotating during, questioning the text's claims and assumptions, using structured methods like SQ3R, monitoring your own comprehension, and consolidating understanding afterward. The goal is not just getting through the pages but genuinely processing what you read, which requires treating reading as something you do rather than something that happens to you.
