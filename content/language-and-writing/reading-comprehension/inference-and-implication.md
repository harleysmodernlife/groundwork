Not everything a text means is explicitly stated. Skilled readers understand what is implied — what the author suggests without saying directly — and can draw inferences by connecting stated information to background knowledge and logic. This ability separates literal comprehension from deep reading.

---

## 1. What Is Inference?

An **inference** is a conclusion you reach by combining what the text states with what you already know or with logic.

Think of it like detective work: the text provides clues, and the reader pieces them together. The inference isn't stated in the text, but it's supported by it.

*Stated: The car had a flat tire. The spare was missing. It was raining.*
*Inferred: The driver was in trouble.*

The text never says the driver was in trouble, but that conclusion follows from the evidence. That's an inference.

---

## 2. What Is Implication?

**Implication** is what the author suggests without stating directly. It's the author's side; inference is the reader's side.

When an author writes *"The meeting ended fifteen minutes early,"* they may be implying that the meeting went poorly, or efficiently — the implication depends on context and tone. The reader infers the meaning from the implied signal.

Skilled authors communicate much through implication — through word choice, selection of detail, juxtaposition, and what they leave out. Skilled readers notice these choices and draw meaning from them.

---

## 3. How to Draw Inferences

**Step 1: Read the literal meaning first.**
What does the text explicitly say?

**Step 2: Notice what's unusual, suggestive, or strategically placed.**
Why did the author include this detail? Why here? What effect does this word choice have?

**Step 3: Combine the text with relevant background knowledge.**
What do you know about this context, culture, situation, or convention that helps interpret what's happening?

**Step 4: Draw a conclusion that the evidence supports.**
A valid inference is supported by the text — not a wild guess or imposition of ideas the text doesn't invite.

---

## 4. Types of Inferences

**Character and motivation**: In narrative writing, inferences about why characters act as they do are drawn from their behavior, speech, and circumstances — not from explicit explanation.

*"She smiled at his response, then turned back to her work."*
*→ Inferred: She found his answer satisfying — or dismissible enough to move past quickly.*

**Cause and effect**: The text may describe effects and imply causes, or describe causes and imply effects.

*"The harvest was poor that year. By spring, the stores in the village were depleted."*
*→ Inferred: The poor harvest caused food shortages.*

**Author's attitude**: Tone and word choice imply how the author feels about the subject, even when they don't explicitly state it.

*"The company's 'voluntary' compliance record speaks for itself."*
*→ Inferred: The author is skeptical of the company's commitment to compliance.*

**Future events (prediction)**: Based on patterns and information given, readers infer what will happen next.

**Unstated comparisons**: When an author describes one thing in detail while mentioning another only briefly, they're often implying a contrast.

---

## 5. Implication Through Omission

What an author leaves out can be as meaningful as what they include. An account that describes a political decision without mentioning its costs implies the costs don't matter. A character description that focuses only on appearance may imply something about the narrator's priorities or biases.

Active readers ask: *What's missing here? What has the author chosen not to say — and why?*

---

## 6. The Difference Between Inference and Opinion

A valid inference is grounded in textual evidence — it can be supported with specific reference to what the text says. An opinion is a judgment that may or may not connect to the text.

When asked "What can you infer?" the answer should be accompanied by evidence: *"The author implies X because Y [specific textual detail]."*

An inference can be wrong — but it's wrong in a way that can be shown using the text. An opinion cannot be proven or disproven by the text.

---

## 7. Practice

To build inference skill:
- After reading each paragraph, ask: *What is being implied here that isn't stated?*
- Look for loaded word choices and ask: *Why this word?*
- Notice the details authors choose to include and which they omit
- When you form an inference, identify the specific evidence that supports it

---

## Summary

Inference is the process of drawing conclusions from textual evidence combined with background knowledge — arriving at meanings that aren't explicitly stated but are logically supported. Implication is what the author suggests through word choice, selection of detail, and omission. Valid inferences are grounded in the text; opinions may not be. Building the habit of asking "What is implied here? What is this detail doing?" transforms passive reading into genuine analysis.
