Transitions are the connective tissue of writing. They show how ideas relate — whether one follows from another, contrasts with it, provides an example, or marks a shift in time or topic. Without transitions, prose feels like a list; with them, it reads as connected argument.

---

## 1. What Transitions Do

A transition does one or more of three things:
- **Signals the relationship** between the previous idea and the coming one
- **Refers back** to what came before (creating continuity)
- **Points forward** to what's coming next

The key insight is that transitions are not filler — they carry meaning. The word *however* tells readers to expect a contrast; *therefore* tells them to expect a conclusion; *for example* tells them to expect an illustration. Choosing the wrong transition — or omitting one — creates confusion or obscures the logic of the argument.

---

## 2. Transition Words by Function

### Addition
*also, furthermore, moreover, in addition, additionally, and, besides, equally important*

*The data confirmed the initial hypothesis. **Moreover**, the secondary findings suggested a broader application.*

### Contrast
*however, nevertheless, nonetheless, yet, but, on the other hand, in contrast, although, despite, while, conversely, even so*

*The experiment produced consistent results. **However**, the sample size was too small for statistical significance.*

### Cause and Effect
*therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, because, since, so, hence, for this reason*

*The deadline was moved up; **therefore**, the team had to accelerate production.*

### Example and Illustration
*for example, for instance, specifically, to illustrate, in particular, such as, namely*

*Several factors contributed to the delay — **for example**, supply chain disruptions affected delivery schedules.*

### Sequence and Time
*first, second, then, next, finally, subsequently, previously, before, after, meanwhile, at the same time, later*

*First, gather all materials. **Next**, read through the instructions before beginning.*

### Summary and Conclusion
*in summary, in conclusion, to summarize, overall, in short, in brief, thus, ultimately*

### Concession
*admittedly, granted, of course, certainly, while it is true that, even though*

*Admittedly, the study had limitations. **Nevertheless**, its core findings stand.*

### Comparison
*similarly, likewise, in the same way, by the same token, compared to*

## 3. Transitions Between Sentences

The simplest transitions appear at the start of a sentence. But transitions don't always require transition words — sometimes a sentence creates its own bridge by picking up a word or concept from the previous sentence.

*The bacteria spread through contaminated water. **This water** served as the primary vector throughout the outbreak.*

The repetition of *water* and *this* links the sentences naturally, without a transitional word. This technique — often called a **cohesive device** — creates smooth connections without relying entirely on a fixed vocabulary of transition terms.

---

## 4. Transitions Between Paragraphs

Moving from one paragraph to the next is harder than moving between sentences because the ideas are more distinct. Strong paragraph transitions:

1. **Echo the end of the previous paragraph**
2. **Signal the relationship to what comes next**

*The committee's decision was driven primarily by financial concerns. [end of paragraph] **While cost was certainly a factor**, the timing of the announcement also shaped public response — a dimension the committee appears to have underestimated. [start of next paragraph]*

The transition phrase (*While cost was certainly a factor*) acknowledges the previous paragraph's focus, then pivots to introduce the new one.

---

## 5. Transitions Between Sections

In longer pieces, entire sections need transition. This often takes the form of a brief bridge paragraph — a sentence or two that closes the previous section and opens the next.

*Having established how the policy was designed, we can now examine how it was implemented — and where it diverged from its original intent.*

This sentence closes the "design" section and previews the "implementation" section.

---

## 6. Common Transition Problems

**Overusing the same transition**: Relying too heavily on *however* or *additionally* makes writing feel formulaic. Vary your choices.

**Using transitions without meaningful connection**: Placing *therefore* before something that isn't actually a conclusion, or *however* before something that isn't a contrast, confuses readers about the logic.

**Missing transitions at key moments**: Abrupt jumps between ideas — especially between paragraphs — leave readers to infer connections that should be made explicit.

**Treating transitions as decoration**: A transition isn't a courtesy gesture. If you need to add one and can't identify the relationship it should signal, that's a sign the argument itself needs clarification.

---

## Summary

Transitions signal how ideas relate — addition, contrast, cause and effect, example, sequence, and more. They appear at the sentence, paragraph, and section level. Strong transitions aren't just transition words: they also echo previous ideas and point forward, creating genuine continuity. Choosing the right transition requires understanding the logical relationship between ideas, not just reaching for a connector word.
