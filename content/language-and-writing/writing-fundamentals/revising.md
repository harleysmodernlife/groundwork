Revision is where writing actually happens. Most writers, when they read published work they admire, imagine it arrived more or less fully formed. It didn't. What distinguishes experienced writers from beginners is not that they write better first drafts — it's that they revise more aggressively and know what to look for.

---

## 1. Revision vs. Editing vs. Proofreading

These three terms are often used interchangeably, but they refer to different levels of work:

- **Revision**: Large-scale structural work — argument, organization, clarity of ideas, whether the piece does what it's supposed to do
- **Editing**: Sentence-level work — clarity, concision, word choice, flow, paragraph structure
- **Proofreading**: Surface errors — grammar, spelling, punctuation, typos

**Always revise before you edit. Always edit before you proofread.** It's inefficient (and demoralizing) to perfect sentences in a section you're about to cut.

---

## 2. Getting Distance First

Revision requires seeing your work as a reader would — but after writing, your mind fills in gaps and misses problems because you know what you intended. Distance helps.

**Let the draft sit.** Even a few hours helps. A day or more is better. When you return, you'll read what you actually wrote rather than what you meant to write.

**Read it aloud.** Your ear catches awkward phrasing, missing words, and unclear passages that your eye skips.

**Print it out.** Changing formats reveals new problems. Many writers can't revise effectively on the same screen they drafted on.

---

## 3. Big-Picture Revision: What to Look For

Start with the largest questions before worrying about smaller ones.

### Argument and Purpose
- Is there a clear central argument or purpose?
- Does each section contribute to it?
- Is there anything that doesn't belong?

### Structure and Organization
- Does the piece have a logical order? Would a reader follow it?
- Are ideas introduced before they're needed?
- Are there abrupt jumps or missing transitions between sections?

### Audience and Clarity
- Is the level of assumed knowledge appropriate?
- Are key terms defined?
- Would someone unfamiliar with the topic understand the argument?

### Beginning and End
- Does the introduction engage the reader and set up the argument?
- Does the conclusion do more than repeat the introduction?
- Does the ending earn its ending — does it leave the reader with something?

---

## 4. Reverse Outlining

One of the most effective revision tools is the **reverse outline**: after drafting, outline what you actually wrote rather than what you planned to write.

For each paragraph, write one sentence summarizing its main point. Then look at the resulting outline:
- Does each paragraph have one clear point?
- Are the points in the right order?
- Are any sections redundant?
- Are there gaps — ideas that should be there but aren't?

A reverse outline makes structural problems visible in a way that's impossible when reading the full draft.

---

## 5. Paragraph-Level Revision

Once the structure is sound, look at each paragraph:
- Does every sentence belong to this paragraph's idea?
- Is the topic sentence clear?
- Is there enough evidence and explanation, or does the paragraph feel thin?
- Does the paragraph connect to the one before and after it?

Cut sentences that don't serve the paragraph's point, even good sentences. "Kill your darlings" — William Faulkner's phrase — refers to deleting writing you love but that doesn't earn its place.

---

## 6. Common Structural Problems

**Burying the lead**: The most important idea appears too late. Move it earlier.

**Repetition**: The same point made in multiple places without development. Consolidate or cut.

**Missing transitions**: Ideas jump without connection. Add bridges between sections.

**Undersupported claims**: An argument asserted but not proven. Add evidence, examples, or explanation.

**Throat-clearing introduction**: A long, generic wind-up before the piece gets to the point. Often, the real beginning is in paragraph three.

---

## 7. Revision as Discovery

Revision is not just correction — it's continuation of thinking. As you revise, you'll often discover:
- What you actually think about the topic (which may differ from what you started out thinking)
- A better central argument that was buried in the draft
- A structure that serves the content more effectively

The best writing often comes from writers who revise extensively. Revision is where the piece becomes what it was trying to be.

---

## Summary

Revision is large-scale reworking of argument, structure, and clarity — distinct from editing (sentence-level) and proofreading (surface errors). Effective revision requires distance, reading aloud, and systematic diagnosis using tools like reverse outlining. Big-picture questions (argument, structure, audience) come first; sentence-level work comes only after the structure is sound. Revision is where most of the real writing happens.
