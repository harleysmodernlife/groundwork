A first draft is not a final product — it's a discovery. The purpose of drafting is to get ideas onto the page in a form that can be worked with. Writers who understand this write better first drafts, not because they write perfectly the first time, but because they write without the crippling weight of expecting perfection.

---

## 1. The Mindset for Drafting

Anne Lamott's concept of the "shitty first draft" captures the essential truth: almost all good writing begins as bad writing. The first draft's job is to exist. Every sentence, even a bad one, gives you something to revise. A blank page gives you nothing.

Common drafting traps:
- **Perfectionism**: Revising every sentence before moving on. This kills momentum and often produces over-polished but structurally weak writing.
- **Stopping to research**: Leaving gaps (like [cite source here]) is better than breaking flow to look something up mid-draft.
- **Rereading too much**: Re-reading the last two paragraphs before continuing slows drafting and tempts revision. Read forward, not backward.

The drafting phase is not the editing phase. Separate them.

---

## 2. Starting the Draft

The hardest part of drafting is often beginning. Strategies:

**Don't start at the beginning.** Start with whatever part you feel most confident about — the section you've thought through, the example that's clearest in your mind. The introduction can be written last.

**Use your outline as a skeleton.** Each outline point becomes a paragraph or section. You don't have to follow it rigidly, but it prevents you from staring at a blank screen without direction.

**Write your way into the draft.** Some writers warm up by writing about what they're trying to say before writing it: "In this paragraph, I want to show that..." This meta-writing can be deleted later, but it sometimes produces the exact language you need.

---

## 3. Maintaining Momentum

Once you start, keeping momentum matters more than quality. Techniques:

**Write in timed sessions.** Set a timer for 25–45 minutes and commit to writing for that full period without stopping to edit. This is the core of the Pomodoro technique applied to writing.

**Leave stopping points mid-sentence.** If you stop for a break at the end of a completed thought, restarting is hard. Stop in the middle of something you know how to finish — the next session will feel less daunting.

**Use placeholders.** If you can't find the right word, write [WORD] or [CHECK THIS]. If you need a statistic, write [STAT]. Don't stop the draft for these gaps.

**Write ugly.** Awkward sentences, clichés, and repetition are all allowed in a first draft. The rule is quantity, not quality.

---

## 4. Following and Departing from Your Plan

Outlines are plans, not contracts. As you draft, you'll often discover that:
- An argument you planned doesn't hold up
- A minor point deserves more space than planned
- A new idea emerges that improves the piece

Follow these discoveries when they genuinely improve the work. Revise your outline accordingly. The goal is a good piece of writing, not faithful execution of the original plan.

---

## 5. Dealing with Difficult Sections

When you get stuck on a particular section:

- Skip it and come back. Write [RETURN HERE] and keep going.
- Write the argument as if explaining it to a friend — lower the register, lose the academic formality, get the idea out.
- Ask: *What am I actually trying to say here?* Answer that question in plain English. Often the answer becomes the draft.

---

## 6. First Draft ≠ Finished Product

Most published writing looks radically different from its first draft. This is true for experienced writers too — sometimes especially so. Knowing the piece will change reduces the pressure to make the draft perfect.

When the first draft is complete:
- Let it sit. Distance is a revision tool — you'll see problems you couldn't see while inside the writing.
- Then read the whole draft before revising any part. Global structure problems take priority over sentence-level problems.

---

## Summary

Drafting is the generative phase of writing — not the polishing phase. The goal is a complete, workable draft, not a perfect one. Effective drafters separate drafting from editing, maintain momentum through timed sessions and placeholders, and follow discoveries that improve the work even when they depart from the plan. A first draft's job is to exist; revision's job is to make it good.
