After revision — the large-scale work of argument and structure — comes editing and proofreading. These are different tasks, done at different levels, and doing them in order matters. Editing improves sentences and paragraphs; proofreading catches surface errors. Both require slowing down to see what's actually on the page.

---

## 1. Editing: The Sentence Level

**Editing** focuses on how ideas are expressed — not what ideas are expressed (that's revision) or whether the spelling is correct (that's proofreading). The goal is clarity, concision, and flow.

### Cut for Concision

The most common editing problem is wordiness. These patterns recur:

**Redundant pairs**: Words that repeat the same meaning.
- *past history → history*
- *future plans → plans*
- *end result → result*
- *each and every → each*

**Filler phrases**: Words that add length without meaning.
- *It is important to note that → [just say the thing]*
- *In order to → to*
- *Due to the fact that → because*
- *At this point in time → now*

**Weak sentence openers**: Starting every sentence with "There is" or "It is" delays the real subject.
- *There are many factors that affect the outcome. → Many factors affect the outcome.*

**Passive voice** (when overused): The agent disappears.
- *Mistakes were made. → The manager made mistakes.*

Cutting is usually improving. When in doubt, cut.

### Vary Sentence Length and Structure

Prose with uniform sentence length becomes monotonous. Short sentences create emphasis. Longer sentences allow nuance and qualification, building momentum through accumulation. Read your writing aloud and listen for rhythm — if it sounds flat or choppy, restructure.

### Word Choice

Strong writing prefers:
- **Concrete to abstract**: *dog* over *animal*, *ran* over *moved*
- **Specific to general**: *quarterly revenue dropped 12%* over *performance declined*
- **Simple to ornate**: Use a simple word when it does the job
- **Active verbs**: Verbs carry meaning — use strong, specific ones

Avoid jargon your reader won't know. Avoid clichés. Avoid hedging every claim into meaninglessness (*somewhat*, *rather*, *in some ways*) unless genuine uncertainty is what you mean.

---

## 2. Paragraph Editing

Check each paragraph for:
- **Clear topic sentence**: Does the first sentence tell you what the paragraph is about?
- **Logical flow**: Does each sentence follow naturally from the one before?
- **Appropriate transitions**: Are connections between ideas marked?
- **Proportionality**: Is this paragraph too long (trying to cover multiple ideas) or too short (underdeveloped)?

---

## 3. Proofreading: Surface Errors

**Proofreading** is the final pass before submission or publication. It catches:
- Spelling errors (especially correctly spelled wrong words: *their/there/they're*)
- Punctuation mistakes
- Grammatical errors (agreement, tense consistency)
- Formatting inconsistencies
- Missing or repeated words
- Incorrect citations or references

### How to Proofread Effectively

**Read backward**: Start with the last sentence and work toward the beginning. This breaks the narrative flow and forces you to see individual sentences rather than meaning.

**Use spell-check — but don't trust it**: Spell-check catches misspellings but not wrong words (*form* for *from*, *their* for *there*). Read the text yourself.

**Change the format**: Print it out, or change the font. A different visual presentation reveals errors the familiar format hides.

**Read aloud**: You'll catch missing words, awkward phrasing, and punctuation problems your eye skips.

**Focus on known weak spots**: If you know you consistently confuse *its/it's* or forget commas after introductory phrases, do a targeted pass for those specific issues.

---

## 4. The Order of Operations

1. **Revise** (argument, structure, clarity of ideas) — most time-intensive
2. **Edit** (sentences, word choice, concision, flow)
3. **Proofread** (spelling, punctuation, grammar, formatting)

Do not proofread first and then revise — that's wasted effort. Proofreading a paragraph you're about to cut is pointless.

---

## 5. Editing Other People's Work

When editing someone else's writing:
- Ask what kind of help they want: structural feedback, sentence editing, or just a proofread
- Preserve the writer's voice — don't rewrite to sound like yourself
- Explain your changes rather than just making them (especially for bigger edits)
- Point out patterns rather than fixing every instance: "You tend to use passive voice frequently — here are three examples"

---

## Summary

Editing improves sentences and paragraphs through concision, clarity, strong word choice, and varied structure. Proofreading catches surface errors — spelling, punctuation, grammar — and should be done last. Effective proofreading uses strategies like reading backward, reading aloud, and doing targeted passes for known weaknesses. The sequence — revise, then edit, then proofread — ensures you're not perfecting writing you're about to change.
