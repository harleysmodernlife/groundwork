Before you write, you have to think. Brainstorming is the process of generating and exploring ideas before committing to a direction. Done well, it prevents the most common writing problem: staring at a blank page with nothing to say.

---

## 1. Why Brainstorm?

First drafts fail when writers try to generate ideas and evaluate them at the same time. Brainstorming separates these two phases deliberately — first you produce, then you judge. This is crucial because the inner critic that makes your prose better during revision makes your idea generation worse during brainstorming.

Good brainstorming:
- Surfaces ideas you didn't know you had
- Helps you discover what you actually think about a topic
- Reveals gaps in your knowledge (what you need to research)
- Gives you more material than you need, so you can select the best

---

## 2. Freewriting

**Freewriting** means writing continuously for a set time (typically 5–15 minutes) without stopping, editing, or lifting your pen. The rule: keep writing no matter what. If you can't think of anything, write "I can't think of anything" until something else comes.

Freewriting bypasses the internal editor by keeping the hand moving faster than the critic can object. It often produces unexpected insights and connections.

*Focused freewriting* applies this technique to a specific prompt or question rather than an open topic.

How to do it:
1. Set a timer (start with 10 minutes)
2. Write your starting question or prompt at the top
3. Write without stopping until the timer goes off
4. Highlight anything interesting, surprising, or worth pursuing

---

## 3. Listing and Clustering

**Listing** is the simplest brainstorm: write down everything you know, think, or want to say about the topic as quickly as possible. Don't sort or evaluate yet — just list.

**Clustering** (or mind mapping) starts with a central idea in the middle of a page, then branches outward with related ideas, sub-ideas, examples, and connections. Visual thinkers often find this more productive than linear lists — the spatial arrangement can reveal relationships and structures that lists obscure.

Both work best when you commit to volume. Push past the obvious first ideas into less expected territory — those are often the most interesting.

---

## 4. Asking Questions

Journalists use the **5 Ws**: Who? What? When? Where? Why? Add *How?* and you have a comprehensive questioning frame for almost any topic.

Other productive questions for academic writing:
- What's my argument?
- Who disagrees, and why?
- What evidence supports this?
- What would someone need to believe for this to be false?
- Why does this matter?

Questions force specificity. *Why?* and *So what?* are especially powerful — they push from observation to significance.

---

## 5. The "Cubing" Technique

Cubing examines a topic from six angles in quick succession (about 3 minutes each):

1. **Describe it**: What does it look like? What are its characteristics?
2. **Compare it**: What is it similar to? How is it different from related things?
3. **Associate it**: What does it make you think of? What connections come to mind?
4. **Analyze it**: What are its parts? How does it work?
5. **Apply it**: What can be done with it? How is it used?
6. **Argue for/against it**: Make the case for it, then against it.

Cubing is especially useful when you feel stuck on how to approach a topic you know factually but haven't found a fresh angle on.

---

## 6. Talking It Out

Conversation is one of the most underrated brainstorming tools. Explaining your topic to someone else — or even to yourself out loud — often produces clarity that silent writing doesn't.

Some writers dictate into their phone while walking. Others use "rubber duck debugging" adapted for writing: explain your argument to an inanimate object (or an imaginary reader) and notice where you stumble, where you can't explain something, and where you find unexpected energy.

---

## 7. Research as Brainstorming

For topics you know less about, reading and research serve a brainstorming function — they prime your thinking, reveal questions you hadn't considered, and often trigger your own ideas through disagreement or unexpected angles.

Read widely but don't stop to absorb everything deeply at this stage. Skim, note what stands out, ask questions as you go.

---

## 8. From Brainstorm to Plan

Brainstorming is not the same as outlining. Once you've generated material, you shift modes:
- Identify the ideas with the most potential
- Group related ideas together
- Look for a central claim or thread that could organize the piece
- Note what's missing — what you need to research or think through further

The transition from brainstorm to outline is where you start making decisions. Brainstorming gives you options; outlining makes choices.

---

## Summary

Brainstorming is idea generation without judgment — it separates the creative phase from the critical phase. Techniques include freewriting, listing, clustering, questioning, cubing, and talking through ideas. The goal is volume and variety: more raw material than you'll use, from which you can select and organize the best. Effective brainstorming prevents blank-page paralysis and often reveals angles and arguments that linear thinking misses.
