Email is the dominant form of professional communication, and most professionals send dozens daily. Yet many people have never been taught to write it well. A well-written email communicates clearly, respects the recipient's time, and moves things forward. A poorly written one creates confusion, wasted time, and a worse professional impression than you intend.

---

## 1. The Anatomy of a Professional Email

Every professional email has the same components:

- **Subject line**: Specific, informative, action-oriented when appropriate
- **Salutation**: Appropriate greeting for the relationship and context
- **Opening**: State the purpose in the first sentence or two
- **Body**: The necessary information, request, or explanation — in order of importance
- **Closing**: Clear next steps, if any; appropriate sign-off
- **Signature**: Name, title, and contact information

---

## 2. Subject Lines

The subject line determines whether the email gets opened and sets up the reader's expectations. It should be:

- **Specific**: Not "Question" or "Update" — but "Question about Q3 budget deadline" or "Project X status update — action needed by Friday"
- **Accurate**: It should actually reflect what the email contains
- **Brief**: Under 60 characters if possible (so it doesn't get cut off on mobile)

**Action flags** help recipients prioritize:
- "[Action Required]" or "[Please review by [date]]"
- "[FYI]" or "[No reply needed]" for informational emails
- "[Time Sensitive]" when genuinely urgent (don't cry wolf)

---

## 3. Salutations and Closings

**Salutation** depends on formality:

- Formal/first contact: *Dear [Name],* or *Dear [Title] [Last Name],*
- Standard professional: *Hi [First Name],* or *Hello [Name],*
- Established relationship: *Hi [Name],* (a comma, not an exclamation point — that reads as over-eager)

If you don't know the person's name: *Dear Hiring Manager,* or *Hello,*

Avoid: *To Whom It May Concern* (outdated), *Hey* (too casual for most professional contexts), *Greetings* (stiff)

**Closings:**
- Standard: *Best,* / *Best regards,* / *Regards,* / *Thank you,*
- More formal: *Sincerely,*
- Warm (appropriate with established contacts): *Thanks,* / *Take care,*

---

## 4. The Opening Line

Don't make the reader wade through pleasantries to get to the point. State the purpose immediately.

*Weak*: "I hope this email finds you well. I wanted to reach out because I had a thought regarding the Henderson account."

*Strong*: "I have a scheduling conflict for our Thursday call — can we move to Friday afternoon?"

If context is genuinely needed first (a cold email, a sensitive topic), provide it briefly, then get to the point.

---

## 5. The Body

**One email, one main point.** If you have two separate topics, consider two separate emails.

**Front-load the most important information.** Put the request, decision, or key information early — not at the end.

**Use structure for multiple items.** If your email covers several points or steps, use:
- Numbered lists for sequences or ranked items
- Bullet points for unordered items
- White space to separate topics

Walls of text in email are difficult to read. Short paragraphs and visual separation help.

**Be explicit about what you need.** Don't leave the recipient guessing:
- "I need your approval by end of day Thursday."
- "Please review the attached document and send me your comments."
- "No response needed — this is for your records."

---

## 6. Tone

Professional email should be clear, direct, and appropriately warm — not cold or robotic, but not overly casual either.

**Match the context**: More formal for first contact or sensitive topics; less formal with established colleagues.

**Avoid hedging**: "I was just wondering if maybe you might possibly be able to..." weakens your message and your professional impression.

**Avoid passive aggression**: "Per my last email..." and "As I mentioned previously..." signal frustration in ways that are rarely constructive.

**Don't email angry.** Write the email, save it as a draft, read it in the morning.

---

## 7. Practical Tips

**Reply time**: In most professional settings, reply within 24 hours — even if only to say you need more time to respond fully.

**CC and BCC**: CC only people who need to know. BCC is appropriate for large announcements or protecting recipient privacy; use it transparently.

**Reply All**: Use only when everyone on the thread genuinely needs your response.

**Attachments**: Mention them in the body of the email. Name files descriptively (*ProjectX_Budget_v3.xlsx*, not *Document1.xlsx*).

**Mobile readability**: Assume the recipient may read on a phone. Short paragraphs, white space, and front-loaded information are especially important.

**Proofread**: One pass before sending catches most errors. Spellcheck is not enough — it won't catch the right word in the wrong place.

---

## Summary

Professional email requires a specific subject line, a purpose-first opening, focused and organized body text, and a clear closing with any necessary next steps. Tone should match the relationship and context — direct without being abrupt, professional without being stiff. Front-load important information, make requests explicit, and leave the recipient knowing what to do next. Even small investments in email quality produce significant gains in professional credibility and efficiency.
