Clarity is the primary virtue of practical writing. In professional and everyday contexts — emails, reports, memos, instructions — the reader's time is limited and their patience finite. Clear writing communicates the right information quickly, without ambiguity or unnecessary difficulty.

---

## 1. Know What You're Trying to Say

The most common cause of unclear writing is unclear thinking. Writers who haven't fully worked out what they want to communicate produce vague, circular, or contradictory prose.

Before writing, ask:
- **What is the single most important thing I need the reader to know or do?**
- **What does the reader need to know to understand or act on this?**
- **What can I leave out?**

If you can't state your main point in one sentence, you don't know it clearly enough yet. Clarify the thought before writing the sentence.

---

## 2. Use Concrete, Specific Language

Abstract language obscures meaning. Concrete, specific language reveals it.

*Abstract*: "We experienced a significant revenue shortfall in the recent period."
*Concrete*: "Revenue dropped 18% in Q3, from $2.3M to $1.9M."

*Abstract*: "Please complete the required documentation at your earliest convenience."
*Concrete*: "Please submit the W-9 form by Friday, June 6."

Ask: *Can I say this more specifically?* Almost always, the answer is yes.

---

## 3. Prefer Simple Words

When a simple word does the job, use it. The goal is communication, not demonstration of vocabulary.

| Instead of... | Say... |
|---|---|
| utilize | use |
| facilitate | help |
| commence | start/begin |
| terminate | end |
| endeavor | try |
| in the event that | if |
| at this point in time | now |
| prior to | before |
| subsequent to | after |
| in order to | to |

This doesn't mean avoiding complex vocabulary entirely — sometimes a precise technical term is the clearest word. It means not dressing up simple ideas in complicated language to appear sophisticated.

---

## 4. Cut Wordiness

Wordiness forces readers to do extra work to extract the meaning. Cut words that add length without adding information.

**Redundant pairs:**
- *past history → history*
- *completely eliminate → eliminate*
- *future plans → plans*
- *free gift → gift*

**Filler phrases:**
- *It is important to note that → [just say it]*
- *The fact that → [restructure]*
- *What I mean is → [just say it]*
- *In terms of → [restructure]*

**Weak constructions:**
- *There are many people who support → Many people support*
- *It was decided that → [Who decided? Say it directly]*

---

## 5. Use Active Voice

Active voice names the actor and the action directly. Passive voice distances them — often obscuring who is responsible.

*Passive*: "Errors were found in the report." (By whom?)
*Active*: "The audit team found errors in the report."

*Passive*: "The decision was made to delay the project."
*Active*: "Leadership decided to delay the project."

Passive voice isn't always wrong — it's useful when the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant, or in scientific writing where passive is conventional. But in most practical writing, active voice is clearer.

---

## 6. Keep Sentences and Paragraphs Focused

**Sentence rule of thumb**: If a sentence requires a second reading to understand, it's too complex. Break it up.

**One idea per sentence** for complex ideas.

**One focus per paragraph**. A paragraph that shifts topics mid-way is actually two paragraphs.

---

## 7. Front-Load the Most Important Information

Readers of professional writing often scan before they read. The most important information should appear first — not buried at the end.

In emails: the subject line and first sentence should state the purpose.
In reports: the executive summary or first paragraph should state the conclusion.
In instructions: the outcome should be clear before the steps begin.

This structure (main point → supporting detail) is called **BLUF** (bottom line up front) in military and government writing. It's effective in almost all professional contexts.

---

## 8. Use Parallel Structure

When listing items, connecting related ideas, or writing headings, use parallel grammatical structure. Parallel structure is easier to read because it creates predictable patterns.

*Not parallel*: "The project requires planning, to research it, and execution."
*Parallel*: "The project requires planning, researching, and executing."

*Not parallel*: "She is talented, hardworking, and she has a lot of integrity."
*Parallel*: "She is talented, hardworking, and principled."

---

## Summary

Writing with clarity requires knowing what you want to say, using concrete and specific language, preferring simple words over complex ones, cutting wordiness, defaulting to active voice, focusing each sentence and paragraph on one idea, and placing the most important information first. These aren't rules to follow mechanically — they're principles for serving the reader. Clear writing respects the reader's time and communicates without unnecessary friction.
