A resume and cover letter are the first things a potential employer reads about you. They serve different purposes: the resume documents your qualifications; the cover letter makes the case for why you're the right fit for this specific role. Both must be well-written, relevant, and targeted.

---

## 1. The Resume

### Purpose and Mindset

A resume is not a biography or a complete work history — it's a strategic document designed to demonstrate that you have the qualifications for a specific job. Every decision about what to include and how to present it should be made with that job's requirements in mind.

### Core Sections

**Contact Information** — Name, professional email, phone, city/state, LinkedIn URL (optional), portfolio or personal site (if relevant to the role).

**Summary or Objective** (optional) — A 2–3 sentence overview of who you are professionally and what you bring to this role. Best used when you have significant experience to summarize or when making a career transition.

**Work Experience** — The core of most resumes. For each position:
- Job title, company, location, dates (month/year format)
- 3–6 bullet points describing what you did and accomplished

**Education** — Degree, institution, graduation year. GPA if above 3.5 and you're a recent graduate. Relevant coursework only if very targeted.

**Skills** — Technical skills, software, languages, certifications. List only what you can actually speak to in an interview.

**Optional sections**: Volunteer work, projects, publications, awards, certifications

---

### Writing Experience Bullets

This is where most resumes succeed or fail. Strong bullets:
- **Start with a strong action verb**: *Led, built, designed, reduced, increased, negotiated, implemented, coordinated*
- **Quantify results when possible**: Numbers make claims specific and credible
- **Show impact, not just activity**: Not "Managed social media accounts" but "Managed social media accounts across three platforms; grew total following 40% in six months"

*Weak*: "Responsible for customer service."
*Strong*: "Handled 50+ customer inquiries daily via phone and email; maintained 4.8/5 satisfaction rating over 18 months."

**Use the job description**: Mirror the language of the job posting where your experience genuinely aligns. Applicant tracking systems (ATS) often screen resumes for keywords before a human ever sees them.

---

### Format and Length

- **One page** for early-career candidates; two pages acceptable with 10+ years of relevant experience
- **Consistent formatting**: Same font, same heading levels, aligned margins, consistent date formats
- **Readable font** at 10–12pt: Calibri, Arial, Garamond, or similar
- **White space**: Dense text is harder to scan; leave margins and space between sections
- **No photos, graphics, or tables** on resumes going through ATS (they break parsing)

---

## 2. The Cover Letter

### Purpose

A cover letter is not a summary of your resume. It's a case for your candidacy — specifically why you want this job and why you're the right person for it. If your cover letter says nothing that your resume doesn't, it's not doing its job.

### Structure

**Opening paragraph**: State the position you're applying for and why you're interested. Be specific — name something about the company or role that genuinely matters to you. Generic openings (*"I am very excited to apply for this opportunity"*) signal that this is a form letter.

**Middle paragraphs (1–2)**: Connect your experience to the job's specific requirements. Pick 2–3 of the most relevant things you've done and explain how they prepare you for this role. Use specific examples, not vague claims.

*Not*: "I am a strong communicator with leadership experience."
*Better*: "In my previous role, I led a cross-functional team of eight through a product launch under a compressed timeline — an experience I believe directly prepares me for the project management demands of this position."

**Closing paragraph**: Express clear interest in next steps. Thank the reader for their time. If appropriate, state that you'll follow up. Keep it brief.

---

### Tone and Length

- One page maximum; three to four paragraphs is standard
- Professional but not robotic — genuine enthusiasm reads better than formal distance
- First-person but not self-obsessed — show you've researched the company and thought about what they need, not just what you want
- Proofread carefully: cover letter errors are noticed more than resume errors because the letter is prose

---

### Common Mistakes

**Generic**: Every word could be copied to a different application. Customize.
**Summary of resume**: The cover letter should add information or context the resume can't.
**Too long**: One page, three to four focused paragraphs.
**Starting with "I"**: Opens the letter on a flat note. Try: *"When I read the job description for..."* or *"After five years in..."*
**Typos and errors**: Indicate carelessness at the first impression.

---

## Summary

A resume is a targeted, strategic document demonstrating relevant qualifications through strong action verbs, quantified accomplishments, and ATS-friendly formatting. A cover letter adds what the resume can't: specific interest in this role, explanation of how past experience applies, and personality. Both must be tailored — a generic application is the norm, which means a specific one stands out. Strong bullet points and a compelling, focused cover letter do more than impressive credentials presented generically.
