Reports and memos are the workhorses of professional writing. They communicate information, analyze situations, and support decisions in organizational settings. Both have conventions that experienced readers expect — conventions that exist because they make information easier to find and process.

---

## 1. Memos vs. Reports

**Memos** (memoranda) are internal documents. They communicate information, make requests, or announce decisions within an organization. Typically short (one to two pages), they address a specific, focused topic.

**Reports** are more comprehensive documents that investigate a problem, analyze data, or document findings. They may be internal or external, and range from a few pages to hundreds depending on complexity.

The conventions below apply to both, though reports require more elaborate structure.

---

## 2. Memo Format

A standard memo has a fixed header:

```
TO:      [Recipient name and title]
FROM:    [Your name and title]
DATE:    [Full date]
SUBJECT: [Specific, clear subject line]
```

The subject line is critical — it should be specific enough that the memo could be filed and found by that line alone. Not *"Update"* but *"Q2 Production Summary — Action Items for June"*.

**Memo body structure:**
- **Purpose statement**: First paragraph states why you're writing
- **Background** (if needed): Brief context necessary for understanding
- **Body**: The information, request, or analysis
- **Action items**: Clearly stated next steps, with names and deadlines
- **Closing**: Brief and to the point — no need for a formal sign-off

Keep memos short. If a memo requires more than two pages, consider whether it has become a report.

---

## 3. Report Structure

Most professional reports follow this structure:

### 1. Title Page
Report title, author, date, organization, and (for external reports) recipient.

### 2. Executive Summary
A self-contained overview of the entire report: the problem, the methods, the findings, and the recommendations. This is often the only section read by senior decision-makers. Write it last, but place it first.

*One to two pages maximum. No new information here — only synthesis.*

### 3. Table of Contents
For reports over five pages; enables navigation.

### 4. Introduction
- What problem or question does the report address?
- Why does it matter?
- What is the scope — what does the report cover and not cover?
- How is the report organized?

### 5. Background / Literature Review
Relevant context, history, or prior research that frames the current analysis.

### 6. Methodology
How was the information gathered? What data sources were used? What analysis was performed? Readers should be able to evaluate whether the methods are appropriate.

### 7. Findings
What did the investigation reveal? Present data, organized logically — by theme, by timeline, or by ranking (most to least significant). Use headings, tables, and visuals to organize complex information.

*Findings are neutral — they report what is, not what should be done about it.*

### 8. Analysis
What do the findings mean? Interpret the data, identify patterns, explain implications. This is where your judgment and expertise appear.

### 9. Conclusions
What can be concluded from the analysis? These flow directly from the findings and analysis — no new information.

### 10. Recommendations
What should be done? Recommendations are actionable, specific, and connected to the findings.

*"We recommend increasing the training budget by 15% to address the skill gaps identified in Section 5."*
Not: *"We recommend addressing training issues."*

### 11. Appendices
Supporting material (raw data, interview transcripts, technical details) that would clutter the main body.

---

## 4. Writing Principles for Reports and Memos

**Front-load information**: The executive summary and introduction should give readers the conclusion before they read the details. Busy readers often read only the beginning.

**Use headings generously**: Readers scan before they read. Descriptive headings (*"Revenue Declined 22% in Q3"* not just *"Revenue"*) help readers navigate and understand structure.

**Active voice**: Clearly assigns responsibility. *"The team missed the deadline"* rather than *"The deadline was not met."*

**Precise numbers**: *"Revenue fell by 18%"* rather than *"revenue declined significantly."*

**Consistent formatting**: Use the same heading levels, numbering systems, and visual conventions throughout.

---

## 5. Visuals and Data

Tables, charts, and graphs communicate data more efficiently than prose for many readers. When using visuals:
- Label every figure clearly (title, axis labels, units, data source)
- Reference the visual in the text (*see Figure 3*)
- Don't make the reader guess what a visual shows — explain the key takeaway

Choose the right visual for the data type:
- **Bar charts**: Comparing discrete categories
- **Line charts**: Showing trends over time
- **Pie charts**: Showing parts of a whole (use sparingly; bar charts often communicate the same information better)
- **Tables**: Detailed data with multiple variables

---

## Summary

Memos are short internal communications with a fixed header format; reports are comprehensive documents with structured sections including executive summary, findings, analysis, and recommendations. Both front-load information, use headings for navigation, and write in clear and active voice. The executive summary is the most important section of a report — it should stand alone as a complete, actionable overview. Recommendations must be specific and connected directly to findings.
