Writing for the web is different from writing for print. Readers online scan before they read, use mobile devices with small screens, and have abundant alternatives a click away. These conditions demand a different set of priorities: clarity, brevity, and structure that supports scanning.

---

## 1. How People Read Online

Eye-tracking research by the Nielsen Norman Group and others has consistently found that web readers:

- **Scan first**: Most readers scan headings, bullet points, and bolded text before deciding whether to read more carefully
- **Read in an F or Z pattern**: Attention concentrates at the top left and drops off dramatically moving down and right
- **Don't read to the end**: Most users don't reach the bottom of a typical web page
- **Read less per page on mobile**: Smaller screens amplify these tendencies

The implication: **important information must appear early**. Bury the lead on a web page and most readers will miss it.

---

## 2. Front-Load Everything

The **inverted pyramid** structure from journalism works well on the web:

1. Most important information first
2. Supporting detail and context in the middle
3. Background and nice-to-know information at the end

Apply this at every level:
- **Page/article**: Lead with the conclusion
- **Section**: Lead with the section's point in the heading
- **Paragraph**: Lead with the main idea; expand below
- **Sentence**: Subject and verb early; qualifications and conditions after

---

## 3. Use Headings and Subheadings

Headings serve as a roadmap. They allow scanners to understand the structure quickly and find relevant sections without reading everything.

**Effective headings are:**
- Descriptive (tell readers what the section contains)
- Parallel (consistent grammatical structure)
- Brief (usually under 8 words)

*Weak heading*: "Background Information"
*Strong heading*: "Why Traditional Diets Fail Within Six Months"

Use heading hierarchy consistently: H1 for title, H2 for main sections, H3 for subsections.

---

## 4. Short Paragraphs

On screen, visual density is harder to process than on paper. **Keep paragraphs to 2–4 sentences** in most web writing. A single-sentence paragraph for emphasis is entirely appropriate online.

This serves both readability and scanning — shorter paragraphs provide natural rest points and make it easier to find where you stopped reading.

---

## 5. Bullet Points and Numbered Lists

Lists are easier to scan than prose. Use them for:
- Items that don't require explanation beyond a phrase
- Steps in a sequence (numbered list)
- Features, requirements, or criteria
- Any set of 3 or more parallel items

Don't use bullets as a crutch for ideas that need explanation — some content requires prose. But when content naturally itemizes, a list communicates it more efficiently than a paragraph.

---

## 6. Bold for Scannability

Use **bold** to highlight key terms, critical information, or the core idea of a paragraph. Readers who scan will catch the bolded words; those terms should reward them with the paragraph's most important point.

Don't over-bold — if everything is emphasized, nothing is.

---

## 7. Plain Language

Web writing should be simpler than academic or formal writing:
- Shorter words over longer words
- Active voice over passive
- Contractions acceptable (they sound like a real person)
- Second person (*you, your*) over third person — direct address builds connection

Aim for a reading level roughly equivalent to a smart general audience, not a specialist or academic audience — unless your specific readership is specialists.

---

## 8. Links as Navigation and Credibility

Hyperlinks do double duty on the web:
- **Navigation**: Links help readers find related or supporting material
- **Credibility**: Linking to sources demonstrates transparency

**Link text should describe the destination**, not just say "click here":

*Weak*: "For more information, click here."
*Better*: "The study found similar results across demographics."

Screen readers and search engines both use link text to understand what the destination contains.

---

## 9. Page Length

How long should a web page be? Long enough to cover the topic, no longer. The research is actually mixed on length:

- **Short content** (under 600 words): Works for news, announcements, simple tasks
- **Long content** (1,500–2,500 words): Performs well in search and is appropriate for in-depth topics — but only if the reader stays engaged
- **Mid-length** content often gets the worst of both: long enough to require effort but not comprehensive enough to justify it

Whatever the length, the structure must support scanning. A 3,000-word page with clear headings and good visual hierarchy reads more easily than a 600-word wall of text.

---

## 10. SEO Basics

**Search engine optimization** (SEO) means writing in a way that search engines can find and understand. The basics:

- Use the words your audience actually searches for in headings and early in the text
- Write descriptive page titles and meta descriptions
- Use header tags (H1, H2, H3) consistently
- Link internally and externally to credible sources
- Write clearly and thoroughly — search engines reward content that users find useful

Good writing and SEO increasingly overlap — both reward clarity, specificity, and usefulness to the reader.

---

## Summary

Web writing prioritizes scannability through inverted pyramid structure, descriptive headings, short paragraphs, bullet points, and strategic bolding. Important information must appear early because most readers never reach the end. Plain language, active voice, and direct second-person address make web content accessible. Links should have descriptive text, and content length should match the topic's depth. Writing for the web means respecting that attention is earned, not assumed.
