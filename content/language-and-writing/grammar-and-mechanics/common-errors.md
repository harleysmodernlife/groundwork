Some grammar and usage errors appear so frequently — even in professional writing — that they've become familiar fixtures. This lesson covers the errors that matter most: those that affect clarity, those that are heavily penalized in academic and professional contexts, and those that persist because the correct form isn't intuitive.

---

## 1. Who vs. Whom

*Who* is a subject pronoun; *whom* is an object pronoun. The rule parallels *he/him*: if you'd use *he*, use *who*; if you'd use *him*, use *whom*.

*Who called?* → *He called.* → Who ✓
*To whom should I address the letter?* → *To him.* → Whom ✓
*She is the candidate **whom** I most admire.* → *I admire **him**.* → Whom ✓

This substitution test works reliably. When in doubt, rephrase with *he/him* to identify which pronoun fits.

---

## 2. Fewer vs. Less

- **Fewer**: Countable things — *fewer books, fewer people, fewer errors*
- **Less**: Uncountable (mass) nouns — *less water, less time, less effort*

*There were **fewer** complaints this quarter.* ✓ (complaints are countable)
*We have **less** time than we expected.* ✓ (time is not countable)

*10 items or fewer* is technically correct (the grocery store sign that reads "10 items or less" is technically wrong, though widely used).

The one exception: quantities treated as single amounts typically use *less*: *less than five miles, less than three hours.*

## 3. Lay vs. Lie

This pair causes persistent confusion because the past tense of *lie* (*lay*) is the same as the present tense of *lay*.

| | Present | Past | Past Participle |
|---|---|---|---|
| **lay** (to place something) | lay | laid | laid |
| **lie** (to recline) | lie | lay | lain |

*Lay* takes a direct object — you always lay *something*:
*Please **lay** the book on the table.* (now) / *She **laid** the book down.* (past)

*Lie* does not take a direct object:
*I'm going to **lie** down.* (now) / *He **lay** on the couch all afternoon.* (past) / *She has **lain** in bed all day.* (past participle)

The error: *I'm going to **lay** down.* ✗ (lay requires an object — down is not a direct object here)

## 4. Effect vs. Affect

- **Effect** (noun): The result or outcome — *the **effect** of the medication*
- **Affect** (verb): To influence or have an impact on — *stress **affects** sleep*

*The policy had a significant **effect** on enrollment.* ✓ (noun)
*Cold weather **affects** plant growth.* ✓ (verb)

Exceptions (less common):
- *Affect* can be a noun in psychology: *flat **affect*** (emotional expression)
- *Effect* can be a verb meaning "to bring about": *to **effect** change* (not "to affect change")

The quick test: If you can replace it with *result*, use *effect* (noun). If you can replace it with *influences*, use *affect* (verb).

---

## 5. Then vs. Than

- **Then**: Related to time or sequence — *first this, **then** that*
- **Than**: Used in comparisons — *more **than**, rather **than***

*She finished **then** left.* (sequence) ✓
*She is more experienced **than** he is.* (comparison) ✓

These are different words with different functions. The confusion is usually a typo or phonetic habit, not a misunderstanding — but it reads as careless.

---

## 6. I vs. Me (in compound subjects/objects)

Remove the other person to test which pronoun is correct:

*She gave the report to **John and me**.* → *She gave it to **me**.* ✓
*She gave the report to **John and I**.* → *She gave it to **I**.* ✗

*Whom should we invite — **him** or **her**?*
***He and I** went to the conference.* → *He went... I went.* ✓

Hypercorrection ("John and I" used everywhere) is as wrong as the opposite error.

---

## 7. Dangling and Misplaced Modifiers

A **misplaced modifier** is a word or phrase positioned so it modifies the wrong element:

*She almost drove **her children** to school every day.* (almost modifies drove — she came close to driving but didn't?)
*She drove her children to school **almost** every day.* ✓

A **dangling modifier** has no logical subject to modify:

***Running to catch the bus**, my hat blew off.* (the hat was not running)
Fix: ***Running to catch the bus**, I lost my hat.*

The phrase at the start of a sentence must modify the sentence's subject. If it doesn't, the modifier is dangling.

---

## 8. Passive Voice (Overused)

Passive voice isn't wrong, but it's often unclear and wordy. Active voice usually communicates more directly.

Passive: *Mistakes were made.* (by whom?)
Active: *The committee made mistakes.* ✓

Passive voice is appropriate when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or deliberately unmentioned. In scientific writing, passive is standard (*The samples were heated*). In most other contexts, prefer active voice.

---

## Summary

Common errors cluster around word confusion (*effect/affect, fewer/less, who/whom, lay/lie, than/then*), pronoun case (*I/me* in compounds), modifier placement (dangling and misplaced modifiers), and overused passive voice. Most can be caught in revision by testing word substitutions, removing compound elements, or checking whether opening phrases logically modify the sentence subject.
