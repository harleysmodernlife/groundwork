Capitalization signals what kind of word something is — a proper name, the start of a sentence, an official title. The rules are consistent enough to be learnable, but they have edge cases that require judgment.

---

## 1. Always Capitalize

### The First Word of a Sentence

*The experiment yielded unexpected results.*

This applies even after a colon when what follows is a complete sentence:

*The results were clear: **The hypothesis was wrong.***

Some style guides (especially Chicago) allow lowercase after a colon even for complete sentences; check the specific guide you're following.

### The Pronoun *I*

*I* is always capitalized, in any position in a sentence.

*She and **I** disagreed. When **I** arrived, it had already started.*

### Proper Nouns

A **proper noun** is the specific name of a person, place, organization, day, month, or other particular thing. Proper nouns are always capitalized.

- People: *Maria Garcia, Albert Einstein, Oprah*
- Places: *Paris, Mount Everest, the Amazon River*
- Organizations: *the United Nations, Apple Inc., Stanford University*
- Days and months: *Monday, January* (not seasons: *spring, fall*)
- Holidays: *Thanksgiving, Ramadan, Christmas*
- Historical events and periods: *the Civil War, the Renaissance, World War II*
- Languages and nationalities: *Spanish, French, Nigerian, Mandarin*

---

## 2. Titles: Before vs. After a Name

Titles are capitalized **when they directly precede a name** as part of that name:

*President Lincoln, Dr. Patel, Senator Harris, Professor Ahmed*

They are **not** capitalized when they follow a name or appear without one:

*Abraham Lincoln, the president*
*She spoke with a doctor.*
*the senator from Ohio*

This distinction is consistent: the title *before* the name is part of the name itself; anywhere else, it's just a common noun.

### Titles in Direct Address

When a title substitutes for a person's name in direct address, capitalize it:

*Yes, **Doctor**, I understand.*
*Thank you, **Senator**.*

---

## 3. Course Names and Academic Subjects

Academic subjects and disciplines are **not** capitalized unless they are proper nouns (languages, nationalities) or part of a specific course title:

*She studies history, biology, and economics.* (general subjects — lowercase)
*She is enrolled in **History 201: The American West**.* (specific course title — capitalize)
*He takes French and Spanish.* (languages — always capitalized)

---

## 4. Directions and Regions

Compass directions are **not** capitalized when describing direction:

*Turn north at the light. Drive three miles east.*

They **are** capitalized when referring to a specific geographic region or cultural area:

*She grew up in the **South**. The **Northeast** has harsh winters. **Western** Europe adopted the policy.*

When in doubt: if "North" or "East" could be replaced by a region's name (*the South* = *the Southern United States*), capitalize it.

---

## 5. Family Relationship Words

Capitalize family titles used as names or in direct address; lowercase when preceded by a possessive:

*I asked **Mom** for advice.* (used as a name — capitalize)
*I asked **my mom** for advice.* (preceded by possessive — lowercase)
*Happy birthday, **Grandpa**.* (direct address — capitalize)

---

## 6. Titles of Works

For titles of books, films, articles, songs, and other creative works, the first and last words are always capitalized, as are all major words. Lowercase: articles (*a, an, the*), short prepositions (*of, in, on, at, by*), and short conjunctions (*and, but, or*) — unless they appear first or last.

*To Kill a Mockingbird*
*The Sound and the Fury*
*Pride and Prejudice*
*"A Day in the Life"*

Different style guides (APA, MLA, Chicago) have slightly different rules about which words to capitalize; when following a specific guide, defer to it.

---

## 7. After a Colon

Whether to capitalize the first word after a colon depends on style guide and whether a complete sentence follows:

- **Chicago**: Capitalize if what follows is a complete sentence
- **APA and MLA**: Generally capitalize
- **AP**: Capitalize only if the sentence following is a direct quote or could stand alone

If unsure, capitalizing after a colon when a full sentence follows is a safe default.

---

## Summary

Capitalize the first word of sentences, the pronoun *I*, and all proper nouns — specific names of people, places, organizations, events, and languages. Capitalize titles directly before names but not after or alone. Academic subjects are lowercase unless they are languages or specific course titles. Compass directions capitalize when referring to a region but not when indicating direction. These rules apply consistently; most "judgment calls" resolve by asking whether a word functions as a proper name or a common description.
