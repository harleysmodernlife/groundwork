One of the most fundamental rules of English grammar is that a verb must agree with its subject in number: singular subjects take singular verbs, and plural subjects take plural verbs. This sounds simple, but it breaks down in predictable, specific situations that trip up even careful writers.

---

## 1. The Basic Rule

*The cat **sleeps** on the couch.* (singular subject, singular verb)
*The cats **sleep** on the couch.* (plural subject, plural verb)

Most errors occur not from ignorance of the rule but from losing track of what the actual subject is.

---

## 2. Tricky Situations

### Words Between Subject and Verb

When a prepositional phrase or clause separates the subject from the verb, writers often mistakenly agree the verb with the nearest noun instead of the actual subject.

*The quality of the reports **is** inconsistent.* ✓
*The quality of the reports **are** inconsistent.* ✗

The subject is *quality* (singular), not *reports*. The phrase *of the reports* modifies the subject but is not the subject.

### Compound Subjects

Subjects joined by *and* are plural:
*The CEO **and** the CFO **are** attending.* ✓

Subjects joined by *or* or *nor* agree with the subject **closest to the verb**:
*Neither the director nor the writers **were** satisfied.* ✓ (writers = plural)
*Neither the writers nor the director **was** satisfied.* ✓ (director = singular)

### Indefinite Pronouns

Some indefinite pronouns are always singular:
*anyone, anyone, everyone, everyone, nobody, nothing, someone, something, each, either, neither, one*

*Everyone **is** welcome.* ✓
*Each of the students **submits** a paper.* ✓

Some are always plural:
*both, few, many, others, several*

*Several **have** already left.* ✓

Some are singular or plural depending on context:
*all, any, most, none, some*

*Most of the cake **is** gone.* (cake = singular, uncountable)
*Most of the students **have** finished.* (students = plural)

### Collective Nouns

Words like *team, jury, committee, audience, family* can be singular or plural depending on whether the group acts as a unit or as individuals:

*The jury **has** reached a verdict.* (acting as one unit — singular)
*The jury **were** unable to agree.* (acting as individuals — plural; more common in British English)

American English generally treats collective nouns as singular.

### Subjects That Look Plural But Aren't

Some nouns end in *-s* but are singular:
*Economics, mathematics, physics, news, measles*

*The news **is** alarming.* ✓
*Mathematics **requires** precision.* ✓

Some nouns have the same singular and plural form: *sheep, deer, species, aircraft*

*One deer **was** in the yard. Three deer **were** in the yard.*

### Inverted Sentences

When the verb precedes the subject, agreement errors increase. Identify the subject before choosing the verb.

*There **are** three options available.* (subject: *options*)
*There **is** one option available.* (subject: *option*)

*On the table **sits** the answer.* (subject: *answer*)

### Titles, Names, and Quantities

Titles of works, organization names, and amounts are singular even when they look plural:

*"The Beatles" **is** a legendary band.* (the name as a unit)
*Twenty dollars **is** a fair price.* (amount as a unit)
*Five miles **was** too far to walk.* (distance as a unit)

### Relative Clauses: *Who*, *Which*, *That*

The verb in a relative clause agrees with the antecedent of the relative pronoun:

*She is one of those people who **always arrive** early.* (*who* refers to *people* — plural)
*She is the only one of those people who **always arrives** early.* (*who* refers to *one* — singular)

This distinction is subtle and frequently mishandled, even in professional writing. The key is identifying what the relative pronoun refers to.

---

## 3. A Diagnostic Strategy

When uncertain, isolate the subject by stripping away all modifying phrases and clauses, then test agreement with the bare subject:

*The collection of rare manuscripts _____ stored in climate-controlled vaults.*

Strip: *The collection _____ stored* → *The collection is stored* → fill in: *is*

---

## Summary

Subject-verb agreement requires matching singular verbs to singular subjects and plural verbs to plural subjects. The most common errors occur when phrases separate subject from verb, when indefinite pronouns or collective nouns are involved, or when sentence word order is inverted. The fix is always the same: identify the actual subject, determine its number, and match the verb accordingly.
