The apostrophe has two jobs: showing possession and marking where letters have been omitted in contractions. These are straightforward rules — yet apostrophes generate more errors than almost any other punctuation mark, largely because of one persistent confusion: *its* vs. *it's*.

---

## 1. Contractions

An apostrophe marks the place where one or more letters have been left out when two words are combined.

| Full form | Contraction |
|---|---|
| it is / it has | it's |
| you are | you're |
| they are | they're |
| we are | we're |
| I am | I'm |
| do not | don't |
| cannot | can't |
| will not | won't |
| would not | wouldn't |
| should have | should've |
| he would / he had | he'd |

The apostrophe replaces the missing letters. *Don't* = *do not* (the apostrophe replaces the *o*). *Should've* = *should have* (not *should of* — a common spoken misunderstanding).

---

## 2. Possessive Nouns

Apostrophes indicate that something belongs to someone or something.

### Singular Nouns

Add *'s* to singular nouns regardless of what letter they end in:

*the dog**'s** collar, the student**'s** paper, James**'s** book*

Some style guides allow *James'* for names ending in *s* (especially classical names like *Socrates*, *Jesus*), but *James's* is always acceptable.

### Plural Nouns Ending in *-s*

Add only the apostrophe (no additional *s*):

*the students**'** papers, the companies**'** policies, the Joneses**'** house*

The test: If the word is already plural (ending in *s*), just add the apostrophe after it.

### Plural Nouns Not Ending in *-s*

Add *'s* (treat them like singular nouns):

*the children**'s** museum, the men**'s** room, the sheep**'s** wool*

### Joint vs. Individual Possession

*Ali and Sam**'s** project* — one shared project (apostrophe on last name only)
*Ali**'s** and Sam**'s** projects* — separate projects (apostrophe on each name)

---

## 3. The *Its* vs. *It's* Problem

This is the single most common apostrophe error.

- **It's** = *it is* or *it has* (contraction)
  - *It**'s** raining.* = *It is raining.*
  - *It**'s** been a long day.* = *It has been a long day.*

- **Its** = belonging to it (possessive pronoun — no apostrophe)
  - *The cat licked **its** paw.*
  - *The company released **its** annual report.*

The rule: **Possessive pronouns never use apostrophes.** This applies to all possessive pronouns: *his, hers, its, ours, yours, theirs.* None of them take an apostrophe.

Quick test: If you can replace the word with *it is* or *it has* and the sentence still makes sense, use *it's*. Otherwise, use *its*.

---

## 4. Possessive Pronouns (No Apostrophe)

This category causes consistent confusion:

*yours* (not your's)
*hers* (not her's)
*theirs* (not their's)
*ours* (not our's)
*its* (not it's, unless meaning "it is")
*whose* (not who's, unless meaning "who is")

*Who's* = *who is* or *who has*: *Who**'s** there?* = *Who is there?*
*Whose* = belonging to whom: ***Whose** book is this?*

---

## 5. Plurals: What Apostrophes Don't Do

Apostrophes do **not** form ordinary plurals. This error — the "grocer's apostrophe" — appears constantly on signs:

*Apple's for sale.* ✗ → *Apples for sale.* ✓
*The 1990's were turbulent.* ✗ → *The 1990s were turbulent.* ✓

Exceptions (where apostrophes do appear in plurals):
- Lowercase letters: *Mind your p**'s** and q**'s**.* (to prevent misreading *ps* as a word)
- Some style guides allow it for abbreviations: *PhD**'s*** (though *PhDs* is also standard)

When in doubt: if you just mean "more than one," don't use an apostrophe.

---

## Summary

Apostrophes mark contractions (omitted letters) and possessive nouns. Singular possessives add *'s*; plural possessives ending in *-s* add only *'*; plurals not ending in *-s* add *'s*. Possessive pronouns (*its, theirs, yours, whose*) never take apostrophes. The most common error is confusing *it's* (contraction: it is/has) with *its* (possessive pronoun). Apostrophes never form ordinary plurals.
