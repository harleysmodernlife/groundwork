Sentence fragments and run-on sentences are two of the most common grammatical errors in writing. Fragments say too little — they're incomplete. Run-ons say too much at once — they're uncontrolled. Both break the rules of sentence completeness, but they do so in opposite directions.

---

## 1. Sentence Fragments

A **sentence fragment** is a group of words that is punctuated as a sentence but lacks one or more of the requirements for a complete sentence. A complete sentence needs:

1. A subject
2. A predicate (verb)
3. A complete thought

### Types of Fragments

**Missing subject:**
*Ran through the park every morning.* — Who ran? No subject.
Fix: *She ran through the park every morning.*

**Missing verb:**
*The tall woman in the blue coat.* — What did she do? No verb.
Fix: *The tall woman in the blue coat left early.*

**Dependent clause fragment:**
*Because the deadline had passed.* — This has a subject and verb, but it's a dependent clause introduced by *because*. It doesn't express a complete thought on its own.
Fix: *Because the deadline had passed, he filed for an extension.*

**Participial phrase fragment:**
*Exhausted from the long journey.* — No subject or main verb.
Fix: *Exhausted from the long journey, she fell asleep immediately.*

**Infinitive phrase fragment:**
*To finish the project on time.* — No subject or main verb.
Fix: *To finish the project on time, they worked through the weekend.*

### Intentional Fragments

Fragments are sometimes used deliberately for rhetorical effect in creative and informal writing:

*Was she ready? Not at all.*
*One last thought. Think carefully.*

This is acceptable when the fragment is clearly intentional and the writing context permits informality. Academic and professional writing generally requires complete sentences.

---

## 2. Run-On Sentences

A **run-on sentence** occurs when two or more independent clauses are joined incorrectly — without proper punctuation or conjunctions.

### Fused Sentence

Two independent clauses with no punctuation between them:

*She finished her work she went home.* ✗

### Comma Splice

Two independent clauses joined with only a comma:

*She finished her work, she went home.* ✗

A comma alone cannot join two independent clauses.

---

## 3. Fixing Run-Ons: Four Methods

Given two independent clauses that run on: *She finished her work she went home.*

**Method 1 — Period:** Make them two sentences.
*She finished her work. She went home.*

Best when the ideas are distinct or you want emphasis.

**Method 2 — Semicolon:** Join with a semicolon when the ideas are closely related.
*She finished her work; she went home.*

**Method 3 — Coordinating conjunction with comma:** Join with FANBOYS (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*).
*She finished her work, and she went home.*

**Method 4 — Subordinating conjunction:** Make one clause dependent.
*After she finished her work, she went home.*

Each fix has a slightly different effect on meaning and rhythm. Choose based on the relationship between the ideas.

---

## 4. Conjunctive Adverbs

Words like *however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, furthermore, consequently* are **conjunctive adverbs**. They signal relationships between sentences but **cannot join two independent clauses on their own** — they require a semicolon before them and a comma after.

*She finished her work**; however,** she was still not satisfied.* ✓
*She finished her work**, however,** she was still not satisfied.* ✗ (comma splice)
*She finished her work**. However,** she was still not satisfied.* ✓ (two sentences)

---

## 5. How to Check Your Own Writing

To catch fragments and run-ons when editing:

1. **For fragments**: Isolate each sentence. Does it have a subject? A verb? Does it express a complete thought on its own? If not, it's a fragment.

2. **For run-ons**: Find every place where you have a comma. Could what comes before *and* after the comma each stand as its own sentence? If yes, you may have a comma splice.

Reading sentences aloud also helps — fragments often feel cut off, and run-ons feel breathless.

---

## Summary

Fragments are incomplete sentences missing a subject, verb, or complete thought. Run-ons occur when independent clauses are improperly joined — either with no punctuation (fused) or only a comma (comma splice). Run-ons can be fixed by adding a period, semicolon, coordinating conjunction, or subordinating conjunction. Conjunctive adverbs (*however, therefore*) require a semicolon before them when joining independent clauses. Understanding these errors makes it possible to identify and fix them reliably during revision.
