A sentence is more than a string of words — it's a structured unit with predictable components and relationships. Understanding sentence structure lets you write with variety and precision, diagnose problems, and control how ideas connect.

---

## 1. The Core: Subject and Predicate

Every complete sentence has two essential parts:

- **Subject**: Who or what the sentence is about
- **Predicate**: What the subject does, is, or has (always contains a verb)

*The dog barked.* — Subject: *the dog* | Predicate: *barked*
*The scientist published her findings.* — Subject: *the scientist* | Predicate: *published her findings*

The **simple subject** is just the noun; the **complete subject** includes modifiers. Same for the predicate.

---

## 2. Objects and Complements

The predicate can include additional elements:

- **Direct object**: Receives the action directly — *She threw the ball.*
- **Indirect object**: Receives the direct object — *She threw **him** the ball.*
- **Subject complement**: Follows a linking verb and describes the subject — *He seems **tired**. She is **a doctor**.*
- **Object complement**: Follows the direct object and describes it — *They elected her **president**.*

---

## 3. Four Sentence Types by Structure

Sentences are classified by how many clauses they contain and how those clauses relate.

### Simple Sentence

Contains one **independent clause** — a clause that can stand alone.

*The train arrived on time.*

Can have compound subjects or predicates and still be simple: *The train and the bus arrived on time.*

### Compound Sentence

Contains two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS), a semicolon, or a conjunctive adverb.

*The train arrived on time, **but** the passengers were still late.*
*The train arrived on time**; however**, the passengers were still late.*

Both halves could stand alone. The connection shows a relationship between them.

### Complex Sentence

Contains one independent clause and at least one **dependent clause** — a clause that cannot stand alone.

***Because the storm delayed the train**, passengers missed their connections.*

The dependent clause (*because the storm delayed the train*) adds information but needs the independent clause to be complete. **Subordinating conjunctions** (*because, although, when, if, since, unless*) introduce dependent clauses.

### Compound-Complex Sentence

Contains at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.

***Although she was exhausted**, she finished her report, **and** she submitted it before the deadline.*

---

## 4. Phrases vs. Clauses

A **clause** contains a subject and a verb. A **phrase** does not.

- Phrase: *running through the park* (no subject)
- Clause: *she ran through the park* (subject + verb)

Types of phrases:
- **Prepositional**: *on the shelf, after the meeting*
- **Participial**: *exhausted from the hike, singing loudly*
- **Infinitive**: *to win the race, to understand the problem*
- **Absolute**: *Her work finished, she left the office.*

---

## 5. Sentence Variety

Varying sentence structure improves readability and rhythm. Writing composed entirely of short simple sentences feels choppy. Writing composed entirely of long complex sentences feels dense.

Effective writers alternate:
- Short sentences for emphasis
- Longer sentences for elaboration and nuance
- Varied sentence openers (not always subject-first)

Compare:

*The experiment failed. The data was corrupted. We had to start over.*

vs.

*When the experiment failed and the data was corrupted, we had no choice but to start over.*

Neither is universally better — the right choice depends on what you want to emphasize and the rhythm of surrounding sentences.

---

## 6. Sentence Functions

Beyond structure, sentences are classified by purpose:

- **Declarative**: Makes a statement — *The meeting is at noon.*
- **Interrogative**: Asks a question — *When is the meeting?*
- **Imperative**: Gives a command (subject *you* is implied) — *Call me when you arrive.*
- **Exclamatory**: Expresses strong emotion — *That was incredible!*

---

## Summary

Every sentence has a subject and predicate. Sentences are classified as simple, compound, complex, or compound-complex based on the number and type of clauses they contain. Phrases add information without containing a subject-verb pair. Mastering sentence structure allows writers to express ideas with precision and control rhythm through deliberate variety.
