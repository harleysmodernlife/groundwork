Character is the heart of fiction. Plot events matter because they happen to someone we care about. Setting becomes meaningful because characters inhabit it. Theme becomes felt rather than abstract when we experience it through a person's choices. Learning to create vivid, complex characters is one of the most important skills in all of creative writing.

---

## 1. What Makes a Character Feel Real

A character feels real when they have:

- **Interiority**: An inner life — desires, fears, contradictions, assumptions about the world
- **Specificity**: Particular habits, preferences, speech patterns, mannerisms
- **Consistency**: Behavior that makes sense given who they are, even when surprising
- **Agency**: They make choices; things don't just happen to them
- **Change (in most stories)**: They are not the same person at the end as at the beginning

The most common failure in characterization is flatness — giving a character a defining trait or role without the contradictions, desires, and history that make people complex. Real people are inconsistent, self-deceiving, and surprising. Convincing characters should be too.

---

## 2. Desire and Motivation

Every significant character needs to **want something**. Kurt Vonnegut's first rule of writing: "Use the time of a total stranger in such a way that he or she will not feel the time was wasted." His third: "Every character should want something, even if it's only a glass of water."

Desire drives plot. Without it, things happen to characters rather than through them.

Motivation operates on two levels:
- **Surface desire**: What the character consciously wants — money, love, revenge, escape
- **Deeper need**: What they actually need, often what they're avoiding — acceptance, self-knowledge, forgiveness

The gap between desire and need creates dramatic tension. A character who wants money but needs belonging will make choices that serve neither well — which is story.

---

## 3. The Character Arc

Most main characters in fiction undergo a **character arc** — they change in some fundamental way over the course of the story. The change is produced by the events of the plot: what the character experiences, chooses, and loses.

**Positive arc**: The character moves toward truth, health, or growth.
**Negative arc**: The character deteriorates — refuses to change, makes wrong choices, is destroyed.
**Flat arc**: The character doesn't change, but the world around them does — useful for certain hero archetypes.

The arc should be connected to the theme: what the story is ultimately about. A character who learns to accept help might anchor a story about community. A character who fails to let go might drive a tragedy about obsession.

---

## 4. The Character Wound

Many writers use the concept of the **wound** — a formative experience (often in the past, often unresolved) that shapes how a character sees the world and what they're afraid of. The wound creates a **lie the character believes** — a false belief that limits them or drives them toward destructive patterns.

The story becomes the journey toward confronting the wound, either healing it or being destroyed by it.

*Example: A character who was abandoned as a child may believe they are fundamentally unlovable. They pursue connection desperately but sabotage relationships before they can be hurt — which fulfills the prophecy and confirms the lie.*

The wound doesn't need to be explicit backstory. It can be implied through behavior, fear, and pattern.

---

## 5. Showing Character Through Behavior

**Show, don't tell** applies most powerfully to character. Telling: *She was a compassionate person.* Showing: *She stayed late to help the intern reformat the report, even though it wasn't her problem and she had a deadline of her own.*

Behavior under pressure reveals character most clearly. What does your character do when:
- They don't get what they want?
- They can help someone at some cost to themselves?
- They're afraid?
- No one is watching?

The answers to these questions are character.

---

## 6. Minor Characters

Supporting characters need enough specificity to feel real — but they don't need the full arc of the protagonist. Even a character who appears in one scene benefits from one specific detail that makes them a person, not a placeholder.

Minor characters can also serve structural functions: **foils** (contrasting characters who highlight the protagonist's qualities), **mentors** (guides), **antagonists** (who create conflict), or **confidants** (who allow exposition through conversation).

---

## 7. Character and Voice

Each character should have a distinct **voice** — the way they speak, think, and see the world. This includes:
- Vocabulary (formal or informal? Technical? Regional?)
- What they notice in a scene
- How they explain things
- What they're sensitive to and what they're oblivious to

In stories told from multiple perspectives, distinguishable voices are essential. In stories with a single narrator, the narrator's voice is also the reader's window — it should be as specific as the character it belongs to.

---

## Summary

Compelling characters have interiority, specificity, consistent motivation, and (usually) the capacity to change. Every character needs something they want; the gap between that desire and what they actually need drives the story. Character arcs are connected to theme — what the character learns or fails to learn reflects what the story is about. Character is revealed through behavior, especially under pressure. Specificity — the particular detail that makes a character feel like a particular person — is what separates memorable characters from types.
