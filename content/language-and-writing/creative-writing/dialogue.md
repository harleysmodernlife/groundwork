Dialogue is one of the most powerful tools in fiction. It creates immediacy — putting the reader inside the scene, listening directly. It reveals character, advances plot, and can compress or create tension more efficiently than almost any other technique. It is also one of the most commonly mishandled elements of craft.

---

## 1. What Dialogue Does

Good dialogue accomplishes multiple things simultaneously:

- **Reveals character**: How people speak — their vocabulary, rhythms, what they say and don't say — tells us who they are
- **Advances plot**: Important information is delivered, decisions are made, relationships shift
- **Creates conflict**: Most strong dialogue has tension — competing desires, hidden agendas, misunderstanding
- **Varies pace**: Dialogue moves faster than narration; scenes without dialogue can feel dense

The most efficient scenes use dialogue to do all these things at once.

---

## 2. Dialogue Sounds Like People, Not Writing

The single most important principle: **dialogue should sound like spoken language, not written prose**.

Real speech is:
- Incomplete (people don't finish sentences)
- Indirect (people rarely say exactly what they mean)
- Interrupted (by other people, by thoughts, by the environment)
- Idiosyncratic (everyone has particular phrases, rhythms, gaps)

*Too formal*: "I want to communicate to you my displeasure with the recent sequence of events, which I believe could have been handled more competently."

*More natural*: "I'm — I just don't understand how this keeps happening."

The second version is less complete but more human.

---

## 3. Subtext: What's Not Said

The most powerful dialogue is often about what characters are *not* saying. People in conflict, people in love, people in grief — they rarely say exactly what they mean. They deflect, change the subject, ask questions instead of answering, and speak around the core issue.

**Subtext** is the emotional reality beneath the surface of what's spoken. The reader should feel it even when it's not stated.

*Surface dialogue*:
— "The coffee's getting cold."
— "You didn't have to wait."
— "I know."

*Implied meaning*: Something is unresolved between them. One waited for a reason; the other acknowledges the waiting without acknowledging why.

Good dialogue leaves space for the reader to feel what the characters aren't saying.

---

## 4. Each Character Should Sound Different

Characters should be distinguishable by their dialogue alone — without dialogue tags. Their vocabulary, sentence length, use of slang or formality, what they choose to talk about and what they avoid — all of these should reflect their individual personality.

Read dialogue aloud and ask: could any character have said this? If yes, it's not differentiated enough.

A working-class father speaks differently from his graduate-student daughter — not just vocabulary, but sentence structure, what they consider worth saying, what they assume the other knows.

---

## 5. Dialogue Tags

A **dialogue tag** attributes a line to a speaker. Rules:

**Use *said* most of the time.** It's invisible — readers don't notice it. Avoid *exclaimed, queried, opined, countered* unless there's a specific reason. These draw attention to themselves.

**Don't use adverbs with *said* as a substitute for better dialogue.** *"I hate you," she said angrily* — if the dialogue is doing its job, the adverb is redundant. Rewrite the dialogue to carry the emotion.

*"I hate you."* — no tag needed if it's clear who's speaking.

**Action beats replace tags and add physicality**:
*She put down her glass. "I hate you."*

Action beats (a character's physical action in the same paragraph as their dialogue) are usually more vivid than *said* tags and ground the scene in physical reality.

---

## 6. Punctuation

American convention:
- Dialogue goes inside quotation marks
- Comma before closing quote if a tag follows: *"I'll be there," she said.*
- Period inside closing quote if no tag: *"I'll be there."*
- A new paragraph for each new speaker
- If one character speaks more than one paragraph, open quotes at the start of each paragraph but close quotes only at the final one

---

## 7. How Much Is Too Much?

Dialogue-heavy scenes move fast but can feel unanchored if they lack physical grounding or internal thought. Narration-heavy scenes can feel static if they have no dialogue or action.

The right balance depends on the scene's purpose:
- Confrontation scenes often have more dialogue
- Reflective or descriptive scenes often have less
- Long dialogue exchanges need physical beats to keep readers grounded

Avoid **dialogue as information dump** — characters explaining things to each other that they already know, solely so the reader can learn them. This is called "As you know, Bob" exposition. Find ways to convey necessary information that characters actually wouldn't already know.

---

## Summary

Good dialogue sounds like spoken language — incomplete, indirect, idiosyncratic. It reveals character, advances plot, and creates tension simultaneously. The most powerful dialogue operates through subtext: what characters don't say matters as much as what they do. Each character should be distinguishable by voice alone. Use *said* as the default dialogue tag; action beats often serve better. Dialogue that explains things characters would already know is a red flag — exposition should be integrated more naturally.
