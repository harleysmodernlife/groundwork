Poetry is the art of using language at maximum compression. A poem does with twenty words what a paragraph might labor through and still miss. Learning to read and write poetry develops skills that transfer to all writing: attention to word choice, rhythm, image, and the way language can carry meaning beyond its literal content.

---

## 1. What Poetry Is (and Isn't)

Poetry is often defined negatively — it's not prose, not fiction. More useful: poetry is writing in which **form and content are inseparable**, where how something is said is part of what is being said.

The line break, the sound of a word, the rhythm of a phrase — these aren't decoration in a poem. They are part of the meaning.

Poetry doesn't have to rhyme. Most contemporary poetry doesn't. The defining features of poetry are:
- **Line breaks used deliberately** (not wrapping at the page margin)
- **Heightened attention to language**: every word chosen with care
- **Compression**: more meaning per word than prose
- **Often, a central image or moment that carries emotional weight**

---

## 2. The Image

The image is poetry's primary unit. A **poetic image** is a concrete sensory detail that resonates beyond its literal meaning — it carries emotional or conceptual charge.

*"The fog comes on little cat feet."* — Carl Sandburg

The fog is personified through cat-behavior. We feel something about the fog's quiet, unexpected arrival that a meteorological description couldn't convey.

Strong images are:
- **Specific and concrete**: "a red wheelbarrow" not "an object in the yard"
- **Resonant**: They mean more than they appear to mean
- **Fresh**: Not clichéd; arrived at by genuine observation, not convention

## 3. Sound: Rhyme, Rhythm, and Other Devices

Poetry is heard, even when read silently. Sound is one of its primary materials.

### Rhyme

**End rhyme**: Words at the end of lines share sounds (*moon/June*)
**Internal rhyme**: Rhyme within a line (*"The fair breeze blew, the white foam flew"*)
**Slant rhyme** (near rhyme): Words that sound similar but don't perfectly rhyme (*love/move, broken/spoken*)

Strict rhyme can feel forced; slant rhyme allows the satisfaction of sound pattern without the artificiality of perfect rhyme.

### Meter

**Meter** is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. **Iambic pentameter** — five pairs of unstressed/stressed syllables per line (da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM) — is the dominant meter in English poetry, the natural rhythm of much speech.

*"Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?"* — Shakespeare

**Free verse** abandons regular meter, relying instead on the rhythms of speech, repetition, and line breaks for its music.

### Other Sound Devices

- **Alliteration**: Repeated consonant sounds at the beginning of words (*"wild and windy winters"*)
- **Assonance**: Repeated vowel sounds (*"the rain in Spain"*)
- **Consonance**: Repeated consonant sounds anywhere in words (*"pitter-patter"*)
- **Onomatopoeia**: Words whose sound imitates their meaning (*buzz, crash, murmur*)

---

## 4. Figurative Language

Poetry uses figurative language more intensively than prose.

**Simile**: *Her voice was like water moving over stones.*
**Metaphor**: *His voice was water over stones.* (direct equation, no "like" or "as")
**Extended metaphor**: A metaphor sustained throughout a poem
**Personification**: Giving human qualities to non-human things
**Synesthesia**: Crossing sensory descriptions (*the sound of blue, the taste of grief*)
**Apostrophe**: Addressing an absent person or abstract quality directly (*"Death, be not proud"*)

## 5. Form

### Sonnet

14 lines in iambic pentameter. Two major types:
- **Shakespearean (English)**: Three quatrains (4-line stanzas) and a couplet (2 lines), rhyming abab cdcd efef gg
- **Petrarchan (Italian)**: Octave (8 lines) and sestet (6 lines), with a "turn" (volta) between them

The volta — a shift in argument, perspective, or emotion — is the sonnet's defining structural feature.

### Haiku

Three-line Japanese form: 5 syllables / 7 syllables / 5 syllables. Traditionally captures a moment in nature with juxtaposition between two images. The form asks for simplicity and precision.

*An old silent pond...  
A frog jumps into the pond.  
Splash! Silence again.*

### Villanelle

Nineteen lines with two repeating rhymes and two refrains. Highly structured, most famous in *Do Not Go Gentle into That Good Night* by Dylan Thomas.

### Free Verse

No required meter or rhyme scheme. The line break becomes the primary formal tool — deciding where to break a line shapes rhythm, emphasis, and meaning.

---

## 6. The Line Break

In free verse, the line break is the poet's most powerful decision. Where you break determines:
- **Rhythm**: A short line reads faster and with more emphasis; a longer line flows more
- **Emphasis**: The last word of a line receives emphasis; so does the first word of the next
- **Meaning**: Breaking before a key word creates anticipation; breaking after creates finality

*"I have wasted my life." — James Wright (end of "Lying in a Hammock")*

The placement of "life" at the end creates finality. Moving the break would change everything.

---

## Summary

Poetry compresses language to maximum density, using images, sound, and form to carry meaning that prose can't. The image is poetry's core unit — concrete, specific, resonant beyond its literal meaning. Sound devices (rhyme, meter, alliteration, assonance) give poetry its music. Major forms include the sonnet, haiku, villanelle, and free verse. In free verse, the line break becomes the primary formal tool, shaping rhythm, emphasis, and meaning. Reading poetry attentively — hearing the sound, seeing the images, noticing the form — develops the close reading skills that make all writing better.
