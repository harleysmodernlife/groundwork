Citations serve two purposes: giving credit to the sources you used, and enabling readers to find those sources themselves. Different academic disciplines use different citation styles, but the underlying logic is the same across all of them.

---

## 1. Why Cite?

**Academic integrity**: Using someone else's ideas or words without attribution is plagiarism, regardless of intent. Citation is how you acknowledge intellectual debt.

**Reader navigation**: A citation tells readers exactly where a claim comes from, so they can verify it, read more, or build on it themselves.

**Credibility**: Citing credible sources strengthens your argument by showing it's grounded in evidence, not just assertion.

---

## 2. What Requires Citation

Everything you did not generate yourself requires attribution:
- Direct quotations
- Paraphrases (someone else's idea in your own words)
- Summaries of another's argument
- Facts, statistics, and data that aren't common knowledge
- Figures, tables, and images from external sources

**Common knowledge** (widely known, undisputed facts like "World War II ended in 1945") generally doesn't need citation. When in doubt, cite.

---

## 3. MLA Style (Modern Language Association)

Used primarily in **humanities**: literature, languages, cultural studies, philosophy.

### In-Text Citation

MLA uses **parenthetical citations** — author's last name and page number in parentheses after the quoted or paraphrased material, with no comma.

*(Smith 47)*
*(García Márquez 112–115)*

If the author's name appears in the signal phrase, include only the page number:
*Smith argues that "resistance is futile" (47).*

For sources without page numbers (websites, etc.), use the author's name alone or a shortened title:
*(Smith)* or *("Article Title")*

### Works Cited

Every source cited in the text appears in a **Works Cited** list at the end, alphabetized by the first element of the citation (usually the author's last name).

Basic format for a book:
*Last, First. *Title of Book*. Publisher, Year.*

Article in a journal:
*Last, First. "Article Title." *Journal Name*, vol. #, no. #, Year, pp. ###–###.*

Webpage:
*Last, First. "Page Title." *Website Name*, Date Published, URL.*

---

## 4. APA Style (American Psychological Association)

Used primarily in **social sciences, education, psychology, business**.

### In-Text Citation

APA uses **author-date citations**: the author's last name and the year of publication, with a comma between them.

*(Smith, 2019)*
*(Smith, 2019, p. 47)* — include page for direct quotes

If the author appears in the sentence: *Smith (2019) found that...*

### References List

APA's reference list (titled "References") is alphabetized and hanging-indented.

Book:
*Smith, J. A. (2019). *Title of book*. Publisher.*

Journal article:
*Smith, J. A. (2019). Article title. *Journal Name*, *volume*(issue), page–page. DOI*

Webpage:
*Smith, J. A. (2019, Month Day). Title of page. Website Name. URL*

Key differences from MLA:
- Only the first word of the title and proper nouns are capitalized (in references)
- Year appears immediately after author name
- Includes DOIs for journal articles when available

---

## 5. Chicago Style

Used in **history, some social sciences, arts**. Two systems:

**Notes-Bibliography**: Uses footnotes or endnotes + a bibliography. Common in humanities.

**Author-Date**: Uses in-text parenthetical citations + a reference list. Common in social sciences.

For footnotes (Notes-Bibliography), the note appears at the bottom of the page:
*1. John Smith, *Title of Book* (Publisher, Year), 47.*

Bibliography entry:
*Smith, John. *Title of Book*. Publisher, Year.*

---

## 6. Managing Citations

Keeping track of citations manually is error-prone. **Citation managers** automate the process:

- **Zotero** (free, browser extension captures sources automatically, generates citations in any style)
- **Mendeley** (free, similar to Zotero)
- **EndNote** (paid, common in sciences)
- **Google Docs citation tool** (built-in, basic but functional)

Whatever system you use, record full citation information — including page numbers, access dates for online sources, and DOIs — while you're researching, not after.

---

## 7. Formatting Principles Common to All Styles

- Hanging indent: All lines after the first line of each reference are indented
- Alphabetical order by the first element (usually author's last name)
- Italics for book and journal titles
- Quotation marks for article titles (in most styles)
- Double-space (in most academic submissions)

---

## Summary

Citations credit sources, enable readers to find them, and demonstrate intellectual honesty. Everything that isn't common knowledge or your original idea requires attribution — including paraphrases. MLA uses author-page in-text citations with a Works Cited page; APA uses author-year with a References page; Chicago offers notes-bibliography or author-date formats. Citation managers (Zotero, Mendeley) significantly reduce the burden of formatting and tracking. Whatever style your instructor or publication requires, learn its format and apply it consistently.
