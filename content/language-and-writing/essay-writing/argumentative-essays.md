An argumentative essay does one thing: makes a claim and defends it with evidence and reasoning. Unlike a report (which presents information) or a narrative (which tells a story), an argument takes a position on a debatable issue and works to persuade a thoughtful, skeptical reader that the position is correct.

---

## 1. The Core Elements

Every argumentative essay has three essential components:

**Claim (Thesis)**: The position you're defending. It must be arguable — a reasonable person could disagree — and specific enough to be proven within the essay.

**Evidence**: The data, examples, expert testimony, and facts that support the claim. Evidence must be credible, relevant, and sufficient.

**Reasoning**: The logical connections between evidence and claim. Evidence doesn't speak for itself — you must explain what it shows and why it matters.

---

## 2. The Thesis

The thesis is the central claim. It appears typically at the end of the introduction and governs every paragraph in the body.

**Weak thesis** (too vague, too broad, or merely factual):
*Social media has changed how young people communicate.*

**Strong thesis** (specific, arguable, significant):
*Social media platforms amplify anxiety in teenagers not primarily through social comparison, but through the disruption of sleep — a mechanism that policymakers have largely ignored.*

A strong thesis names not just a position but the reasoning behind it. It tells readers what to expect and makes a bet the essay will pay off.

---

## 3. Building the Argument: Toulmin's Model

Stephen Toulmin's framework provides a useful structure for building arguments:

- **Claim**: The position (your thesis)
- **Grounds**: The evidence supporting the claim
- **Warrant**: The reasoning connecting grounds to the claim (often implicit, but should be made explicit)
- **Backing**: Support for the warrant when it's not obvious
- **Qualifier**: The scope and limitations of the claim (*in most cases*, *under these conditions*)
- **Rebuttal**: Acknowledgment of exceptions or counterarguments

Most good arguments follow this logic, even if they don't use this terminology.

---

## 4. Handling Counterarguments

A common mistake: ignoring disagreement. Stronger essays acknowledge the best objections to their position — and respond to them.

**Why this matters**: If you ignore counterarguments, skeptical readers find them on their own — and you lose credibility. Addressing objections directly signals intellectual honesty and strengthens your position.

**Concede-and-refute**: Acknowledge what's true about the counterargument, then explain why it doesn't undermine your claim.

*It is true that screen time restrictions alone have little effect on teen anxiety. **However**, the research on sleep disruption suggests that even modest reductions in late-night phone use produce significant mental health improvements — which means timing, not just total screen time, is the key variable.*

---

## 5. Evidence: Types and Standards

Different types of evidence serve different functions:

- **Statistical data**: Provides quantitative support (*"72% of teenagers report using their phones within an hour of bedtime"*)
- **Expert testimony**: Citing specialists adds credibility
- **Examples**: Specific cases illustrate abstract claims
- **Studies and research**: Systematic evidence is typically more persuasive than anecdote

Standards for evidence:
- **Credibility**: Who produced it? What's the source?
- **Relevance**: Does it actually support this specific claim?
- **Sufficiency**: Is one study or example enough, or do you need more?
- **Currency**: Is it recent enough to still apply?

---

## 6. Essay Structure

**Introduction**: Hook, background, thesis

**Body paragraphs**: Each focused on one aspect of the argument. Typical structure:
- Topic sentence (the paragraph's claim)
- Evidence
- Explanation of how the evidence supports the thesis
- Transition

**Counterargument paragraph(s)**: Present and refute the strongest objection(s)

**Conclusion**: Synthesize, point toward broader implications, don't merely repeat the introduction

The counterargument section can appear after the evidence or interwoven throughout, depending on the essay's structure.

---

## 7. Tone

Argumentative writing should be:
- **Confident**: State claims directly; avoid excessive hedging
- **Measured**: Don't overstate; use qualifiers where appropriate
- **Respectful**: Treat opposing views charitably; argue against the strongest version, not a caricature

Avoid emotional appeals alone — they may move readers but don't constitute reasoned argument. Avoid attacks on persons (ad hominem) rather than ideas.

---

## Summary

An argumentative essay takes a specific, debatable position and defends it with evidence and reasoning. The thesis must be arguable and specific. Body paragraphs develop the argument point by point; a counterargument section addresses and responds to objections. Evidence must be credible, relevant, and sufficient, and each piece of evidence must be connected to the claim through explicit reasoning. Strong argumentative essays treat disagreement honestly and write to persuade thoughtful skeptics, not just reinforce existing believers.
