Evidence is the material that backs up your claims. But knowing how to find evidence isn't enough — you also have to know how to use it. Poorly integrated evidence weakens arguments even when the evidence itself is strong. Effective use of evidence requires selecting appropriately, introducing it clearly, quoting or paraphrasing correctly, and analyzing it explicitly.

---

## 1. Why Evidence Alone Isn't Enough

A common mistake: presenting evidence and assuming it speaks for itself.

*Teen smartphone use has increased dramatically. According to a 2023 Pew survey, 95% of teenagers have a smartphone. Therefore, social media harms teenagers.*

The leap from "teenagers have smartphones" to "social media harms teenagers" is not supported — the evidence doesn't establish the conclusion without additional reasoning. Evidence and claim must be connected explicitly.

The formula: **Claim + Evidence + Explanation**

## 2. Three Ways to Use Sources: Quote, Paraphrase, Summarize

### Direct Quotation

Use the source's exact words, enclosed in quotation marks. Use quotation when:
- The exact wording matters (a legal definition, a striking phrase, a key term)
- The authority of the source itself (not just the idea) adds credibility
- Changing the wording would lose something important

Avoid long quotations when a paraphrase would communicate the idea more efficiently.

### Paraphrase

Restate the source's idea in your own words. A paraphrase covers roughly the same scope as the original passage, but in different wording. Use paraphrase:
- When the idea matters more than the exact wording
- To translate dense or technical source language into clearer form
- For most routine incorporation of source material

**Paraphrase ≠ find-and-replace synonyms**. A true paraphrase reflects your understanding of the idea, not a rearranged version of the original sentence. If you can't write the paraphrase without looking at the source, you haven't processed the idea.

### Summary

A summary condenses a longer source (an article, a chapter, a book) into its main points. Use summary when:
- You need to convey what a source argues overall, not a specific passage
- Background context can be efficiently conveyed rather than quoted

## 3. Introducing Evidence (Signal Phrases)

Don't drop evidence into your writing without introduction. Use **signal phrases** to tell the reader who is speaking and why it matters.

*According to..., [author] argues that..., [author] found that..., [author] notes..., researchers at [institution] concluded..., a 2022 study in [journal] demonstrated...*

Signal phrases:
- Identify the source
- Contextualize it (author's credentials, publication, relevance)
- Indicate the nature of the claim (argues, found, notes, warns, questions)

**Varying signal verbs adds precision**: An author who *claims* something is making an unverified assertion; one who *demonstrates* is providing evidence; one who *argues* is making a case. Choose verbs that accurately characterize the source's relationship to the claim.

---

## 4. Analyzing Evidence (The "So What?")

After presenting evidence, you must explain it. Don't let the evidence end the paragraph — analyze it.

Questions to ask after presenting evidence:
- What does this show?
- Why does this matter for my argument?
- What does this prove that wasn't proven before?
- Does this fully support the claim, or just part of it?
- What are the limitations of this evidence?

Example (evidence without analysis):
*A 2022 study found that students who slept fewer than 7 hours had higher rates of depression.*

With analysis:
*A 2022 study found that students who slept fewer than 7 hours had significantly higher rates of depression. This suggests that interventions targeting sleep — rather than screen time per se — may offer a more direct route to improving adolescent mental health, since sleep disruption is the mechanism through which heavy phone use appears to cause harm.*

The analysis connects the evidence to the specific argument being made, not just to the general topic.

## 5. Avoiding Plagiarism

**Plagiarism** is presenting someone else's ideas or words as your own — intentionally or not. It includes:
- Copying text without quotation marks and citation
- Paraphrasing too closely (keeping the original sentence structure with synonyms substituted)
- Presenting another person's ideas without attribution, even in your own words

**How to avoid it:**
- Cite every source you use — quotes, paraphrases, and summaries all require citation
- Take notes in your own words while researching; record the source
- When paraphrasing, close the source, write from memory, then verify accuracy

Unintentional plagiarism (careless note-taking, inadequate paraphrase) carries the same academic consequences as deliberate plagiarism. The standard is the same.

---

## 6. Balancing Sources and Your Own Voice

A research essay is not a patchwork of quotations — it's your argument, supported by sources. You should be the dominant voice. Sources are evidence for your claims, not substitutes for making them.

Signs of over-reliance on sources:
- Multiple consecutive sentences are all paraphrases or quotes with thin connecting analysis
- Every paragraph introduces a new source without developing any of them
- Your own argument disappears under the weight of others' ideas

Every paragraph should have more of your analysis than your sources' content.

---

## Summary

Using evidence effectively means selecting quotes, paraphrases, and summaries appropriately, introducing them with signal phrases, and — most critically — analyzing them to connect evidence to your specific claim. Evidence doesn't speak for itself; the writer must explain what it shows and why it matters. Proper citation prevents plagiarism, which applies to ideas and paraphrases, not just copied text. Your own analytical voice should remain dominant; sources serve your argument, not the reverse.
