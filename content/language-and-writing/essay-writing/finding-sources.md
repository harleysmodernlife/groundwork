Finding good sources is a skill. The internet makes information easy to access but difficult to evaluate — there are more sources than ever, and the gap in quality between excellent and terrible is vast. Learning to find credible, relevant sources efficiently is one of the most practical skills academic writing requires.

---

## 1. Types of Sources

### Primary Sources

**Primary sources** are original, firsthand materials: raw data, original research, historical documents, speeches, diaries, interviews, creative works, legal documents.

*Examples: A study published in a scientific journal, a transcript of a congressional hearing, a speech, a photograph, a census record*

Primary sources give you direct access to the thing itself — not someone else's interpretation of it.

### Secondary Sources

**Secondary sources** analyze, interpret, or summarize primary sources.

*Examples: A review article, a textbook, a history book, a news article reporting on a study, a critical essay*

Secondary sources help you understand context, competing interpretations, and the significance of primary material.

### Tertiary Sources

**Tertiary sources** compile and summarize secondary sources.

*Examples: Wikipedia, encyclopedias, textbooks, databases*

Useful for background knowledge and finding primary/secondary sources to follow up on — but not generally citable as authoritative sources in academic work.

---

## 2. Where to Find Sources

### Academic Databases

For academic work, databases are far more reliable than general web searches:

- **Google Scholar**: Free; indexes academic papers, books, and court opinions; shows citation counts (a rough indicator of influence)
- **JSTOR**: Journal articles across humanities and social sciences (some free, some requiring institutional access)
- **PubMed**: Biomedical and life sciences literature (free)
- **EBSCO, ProQuest**: Broad academic databases, typically available through libraries

If you have access to a university or public library, their database subscriptions are invaluable.

### Library Catalogs

Library catalogs (including WorldCat, which aggregates holdings from libraries worldwide) find books and journals. Books, especially recent ones from academic presses, are often more thoroughly argued and vetted than articles.

### Government and Institutional Sources

For data and policy:
- **census.gov**: U.S. demographic data
- **cdc.gov, nih.gov**: Health data
- **bls.gov**: Labor and economic data
- **data.gov**: Federal datasets across many topics
- **United Nations, World Bank, WHO**: International data

### Reference Lists

One of the most efficient search strategies: find one good source, then mine its reference list for additional sources. Good scholars cite the most important prior work; following these trails leads to the core literature quickly.

## 3. Evaluating Sources: CRAAP and Beyond

The **CRAAP test** is a widely used framework for evaluating sources:

- **Currency**: When was it published? Is it recent enough for this topic?
- **Relevance**: Does it actually address your specific question?
- **Authority**: Who wrote it? What are their credentials? What's the publication?
- **Accuracy**: Is the information supported by evidence? Are claims documented?
- **Purpose**: Why was it written? To inform? Advocate? Sell?

Additional questions:
- Is it peer-reviewed? (For scientific claims, this matters significantly)
- Does it have a transparent methodology?
- Are there obvious errors, exaggerations, or signs of bias?
- Is the source cited by other credible scholars?

---

## 4. What Peer Review Means

A **peer-reviewed** (or **refereed**) article has been evaluated by experts in the field before publication. Reviewers assess whether the methods are sound, the conclusions justified, and the work original. Peer review doesn't guarantee correctness — reviewers can miss errors, and the system has known weaknesses — but it applies a filter that self-published or commercially motivated content lacks.

When filtering in Google Scholar or databases, you can often select "peer-reviewed only." In health, science, and social science writing, prioritizing peer-reviewed sources is usually expected.

---

## 5. Avoiding Low-Quality Sources

Common sources to be cautious about:

- **Wikipedia**: Useful for background and finding primary sources, but not citable as an authority in academic work. It is explicitly user-edited and may be wrong.
- **Unreferenced web content**: Blogs, opinion sites, and news aggregators with no byline or credentials
- **Advocacy and lobbying sites**: Credible for understanding a position but not neutral sources of data
- **AI-generated text**: Should not be cited as a source for factual claims — AI systems generate plausible text that may be fabricated
- **Non-expert commentary**: A politician or celebrity's opinion on a scientific question is not comparable to a scientist's

---

## 6. Keeping Track of Sources

As you research, track your sources in a way that makes citation easy later:

- **Citation managers**: Zotero (free), Mendeley, and EndNote automatically generate citations in multiple formats
- **Manual tracking**: Record author, title, publication, volume, issue, page numbers, URL, and access date as you go — not after the fact

Keep notes on what each source argues and which claims it supports. This saves significant time during drafting.

---

## Summary

Finding good sources means knowing where to look and how to evaluate what you find. Primary sources are original materials; secondary sources analyze them; tertiary sources are compilations. Academic databases (Google Scholar, JSTOR, PubMed) are more reliable than general web searches for scholarly work. Evaluate sources using criteria of currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, and purpose. Peer-reviewed sources meet a higher bar for scientific and academic claims. Keep organized records as you research to simplify citation later.
