## Trademarks: Protecting Your Name

A trademark is a source identifier. It doesn't protect an idea or an invention; it protects the "brand" so that customers know exactly who they are buying from. If you have a great product but no trademark, someone else can build a better brand around your idea and effectively steal your market.

---

## Trademark vs. Copyright vs. Patent

Small business owners frequently confuse these three. Using the wrong one is a waste of time and money.

| Type | What it Protects | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Trademark** | Brand names, logos, slogans, and sounds. | The Nike "Swoosh" or the name "Apple". |
| **Copyright** | Original creative works (text, art, music, code). | A textbook, a song, or the source code of an app. |
| **Patent** | Inventions and unique functional processes. | A new type of engine or a specific chemical formula. |

---

## Common Law vs. Registered Trademarks

You don't actually need a government filing to have a trademark, but there is a massive difference in protection.

### Common Law (TM)
As soon as you start selling a product under a specific name in a specific area, you have "Common Law" rights. You can use the ™ symbol immediately. 
- **Limit:** Your protection only extends to the geographic area where you actually do business. If you sell in Ohio, you can't stop someone in California from using the same name unless you can prove "national fame."

### Registered (®)
This is a federal registration with the USPTO (United States Patent and Trademark Office). You can only use the ® symbol after the government officially grants the registration.
- **Benefit:** You have nationwide protection. You can sue anyone in the US for using your name, regardless of where you are located. It also makes it much easier to stop counterfeiters on platforms like Amazon or eBay.

## How to Check for Availability

Before you spend money on logos and signage, you must verify the name isn't already taken. A "business license" from your city is NOT a trademark check; the city only cares if you're paying taxes, not if you're infringing on a brand.

1. **Google Search:** The most basic check. Look for businesses with the same name in your industry.
2. **TESS (Trademark Electronic Search System):** Search the USPTO database. Look for names that are "confusingly similar," not just identical. If you want "Blue Bolt Electric" and there is already a "Blue Bolt Power," you will likely lose a trademark battle.
3. **Domain Check:** See if the .com is available. If a squatter owns it for $5,000, you might want to reconsider the name before you commit.

## Common Mistakes

- **Descriptive Names:** You cannot trademark a name that simply describes the product. You can't trademark "Cold Beer" for a beer company. You *can* trademark "Budweiser" because it's a distinct brand name, not a description of the liquid.
- **Assuming the LLC Name is Enough:** Registering "XYZ Services LLC" with the Secretary of State does not give you trademark rights. It only means no other company in that state can have that exact legal name. It does not stop someone from using "XYZ Services" as their brand name.
- **Waiting Too Long:** If you build a huge brand and then find out you're infringing on someone else's trademark, they can force you to rebrand everything—website, signs, trucks, and uniforms—overnight. This is a business-killing expense.
