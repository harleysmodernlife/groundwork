## Common Contract Mistakes

Most business owners treat contracts as a formality—something you sign to "make it official." In reality, a contract is a set of instructions for how to handle a disaster. The most expensive mistakes happen not when the contract is missing, but when the contract is vague, outdated, or based on a "template" that doesn't fit the actual business relationship.

---

## 1. The "Template" Trap
Downloading a generic contract from the internet is a dangerous shortcut. A template is designed to protect a hypothetical company in a hypothetical state. 
- **The Failure:** You use a California-based employment template for a worker in Texas. The laws regarding "at-will" employment, overtime, and non-compete agreements vary wildly by state. 
- **The Result:** You include a non-compete clause that is legally unenforceable in your state, giving you a false sense of security while your best employee leaves to start a competing business next door.

## 2. Vague Deliverables (The Scope Creep)
When a contract says "Consultant will provide marketing support," it is not a contract; it is an invitation for the client to demand everything for free.
- **The Failure:** Failing to define the "Done" state. If the contract doesn't specify the exact number of deliverables, the frequency of updates, and the limit on revisions, the project will never end.
- **The Fix:** Use a detailed Statement of Work (SOW). Instead of "marketing support," use "four 500-word blog posts per month, delivered by the 1st of each month, with a maximum of two rounds of revisions per post."

## 3. Ignoring the "What If" Scenarios
Most people write contracts for the "Happy Path"—where everyone does their job and the money flows. A professional contract is written for the "Nightmare Path."
- **The Failure:** Forgetting to define the exit. What happens if the client stops paying? What happens if you get sick and can't finish the work? What happens if the client disappears for three months and then comes back demanding the project be finished in 48 hours?
- **The Fix:** Include a "Termination for Convenience" clause (allowing either party to leave with 30 days' notice) and a "Suspension of Work" clause (allowing you to stop all work if an invoice is more than 15 days overdue).

---

## 4. The Oral Modification Error
"We talked about it on the phone, so it's fine." This is how lawsuits start.
- **The Failure:** Making significant changes to a project's scope, price, or deadline via a phone call or a casual text message without updating the written agreement.
- **The Fix:** Every major change requires a "Change Order." This is a simple one-page document that says: "Per our conversation on [Date], we are adding [X] to the scope of work, which increases the total price by [Y] and extends the deadline to [Date]." Both parties sign it, and it becomes part of the legal contract.

## 5. Misunderstanding "Indemnification"
Indemnification is a legal promise to pay for the other party's losses if you cause a problem. Many small business owners sign this without realizing they are potentially betting their entire company on a single project.
- **The Failure:** Signing a contract with "Unlimited Indemnification." This means if you make a mistake that costs the client $1 million, you are legally responsible for the full $1 million, regardless of your insurance limit.
- **The Fix:** Always cap your liability. A standard cap is either the total value of the contract or the limit of your professional liability insurance policy. Never agree to be responsible for "consequential damages" (lost profits the client *might* have made if the project had succeeded).

---

## Summary Checklist for Every Contract

Before signing, ask yourself these four questions:
1. **Can I point to the exact moment this project is "finished"?** (If not, the scope is too vague).
2. **Do I know exactly how and when I get paid?** (If not, the payment terms are missing).
3. **Do I have a clear, legal way to fire this client tomorrow?** (If not, you lack a termination clause).
4. **Is my total financial risk limited to a specific number?** (If not, your liability is uncapped).
