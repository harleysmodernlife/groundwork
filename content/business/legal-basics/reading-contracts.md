## Reading a Contract Without a Law Degree

A contract is not a friendly agreement; it is a risk-allocation document. Its primary purpose is to define exactly who is responsible for what, and who pays when something goes wrong. You do not need a law degree to read a contract, but you do need to stop reading it as a narrative and start reading it as a set of rules.

---

## The Four Critical Pillars

When reviewing any business agreement, ignore the "whereas" clauses and the legal jargon at the start. Go straight to these four areas:

### 1. Scope of Work (SOW)
This is the most common point of failure. If the SOW is vague, you will suffer from "scope creep"—where the client expects more work than you intended to do for the price agreed upon.
- **Bad:** "Provide marketing services for the client."
- **Good:** "Create four 30-second social media videos per month, including two rounds of revisions per video."
- **The Rule:** If it isn't explicitly listed in the SOW, you aren't obligated to do it, and the client shouldn't expect it.

### 2. Payment Terms
Money is the only thing that keeps a business alive. Look for these specifics:
- **Trigger:** What exactly triggers the payment? (e.g., "Upon signing," "Upon completion of Milestone 1," "Net 30 days from invoice date").
- **Penalty:** Is there a late fee? If there isn't one, you have no leverage to get paid on time.
- **Method:** How is payment made? Ensure the contract specifies the method to avoid "the check is in the mail" excuses.

### 3. Termination and Exit
Every relationship ends. The contract must define how to leave without destroying the business.
- **Termination for Cause:** Can you fire the client immediately if they stop paying? Can they fire you immediately if you miss a deadline?
- **Termination for Convenience:** Can either party leave for any reason with 30 days' notice? This is standard and protects both sides.
- **Deliverables upon Exit:** Do you keep the work until you are paid in full? Does the client get the raw files or just the final product?

### 4. Liability and Indemnification
This is where the "big money" risk lives. Indemnification is a promise to pay for the other party's losses if you cause a problem.
- **The Cap:** Never sign a contract with unlimited liability. Your liability should be capped at the total amount of the contract or the limit of your insurance policy.
- **The Trigger:** Ensure you are only liable for things *you* actually controlled. You should not be responsible for a client's failure to provide accurate information.

## Red Flags to Spot

If you see these phrases, stop and renegotiate:

| Phrase | Why it's a Red Flag |
| :--- | :--- |
| "Including but not limited to" | This is a loophole that allows the other party to add tasks indefinitely. |
| "Reasonable efforts" | "Reasonable" is subjective and leads to arguments. Use "best efforts" or specific metrics. |
| "In perpetuity" | This means forever. Never give away your rights or obligations forever. |
| "Sole discretion" | This gives the other party total power to decide if you've met a goal or deserve payment. |

---

## The "What Happens If..." Test

Before signing, run these three scenarios through the contract text:
1. **The Ghost:** The client disappears and stops responding. Does the contract allow you to stop work and keep a deposit?
2. **The Nightmare:** You make a massive mistake that costs the client $10,000. Does the contract limit your liability, or are you personally on the hook for the whole amount?
3. **The Pivot:** The client decides they want the project to go in a completely different direction. Does the contract provide a mechanism for a "Change Order" and additional payment?

If you can't find the answer to these three questions in the text, the contract is incomplete.
