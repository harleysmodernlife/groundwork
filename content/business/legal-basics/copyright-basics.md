## Copyright Basics

Copyright is the legal protection for "original works of authorship." In the simplest terms: you own the copyright to anything you create the moment you fix it in a tangible medium (write it down, record it, or save it to a disk). You do not need to register with the government to *own* the copyright, but you do need to register it to *enforce* it in federal court.

---

## What Copyright Actually Protects

Copyright protects the **expression** of an idea, not the **idea itself**. This is a critical distinction for business owners.

- **The Idea (Not Protected):** The idea of a "blog about fixing old cars" cannot be copyrighted. Anyone can start a blog about fixing old cars.
- **The Expression (Protected):** The specific words, photos, and layout of *your* blog about fixing old cars are protected. If someone copies your exact articles word-for-word, they are infringing on your copyright.

### Common Business Assets Protected by Copyright:
- **Written Content:** Website copy, blog posts, e-books, training manuals, and email sequences.
- **Visual Media:** Photographs, videos, graphics, and logo designs.
- **Software:** The actual source code of an application or website.
- **Audio:** Podcasts, music, and voice recordings.

## The "Work for Hire" Trap

This is the most common legal mistake in the creative and tech industries. Many business owners assume that because they *paid* someone to create a logo or a website, they *own* the copyright to it. This is often false.

### The Default Rule
By default, the person who creates the work (the freelancer) owns the copyright. The client only receives a "license" to use the work for the purpose it was created.

### The Solution: "Work Made for Hire"
To ensure the business owns the asset, the contract must explicitly state that the work is a **"Work Made for Hire."** 
- **The Clause:** "The parties agree that all deliverables created under this agreement shall be considered a 'work made for hire' as defined by the US Copyright Act. The Client shall own all rights, title, and interest, including all copyrights, in and to the deliverables."
- **The Backup:** If the work doesn't legally qualify as a "work for hire," the contract should include an **Assignment of Rights** clause, where the freelancer formally transfers the copyright to the business upon final payment.

## Fair Use and Infringement

"Fair Use" is a legal defense used to justify using copyrighted material without permission. It is not a "right," and it is often misunderstood by business owners.

**Fair Use is generally allowed for:**
- **Criticism and Commentary:** Quoting a small part of a work to critique it.
- **News Reporting:** Using a clip of a video to report on a news event.
- **Teaching/Education:** Using a snippet of a text in a classroom setting.

**Fair Use is NOT allowed for:**
- **Commercial Gain:** Using a copyrighted song in a commercial for your business.
- **Substantiality:** Copying the most important part of a work, even if it's a small percentage of the total.
- **Market Harm:** Using a piece of work in a way that makes people less likely to buy the original.

## Practical Steps for Business Owners

1. **Use the Symbol:** Put "© [Year] [Your Company Name]" in the footer of your website and at the end of your documents. It doesn't give you extra legal power, but it tells the world you are aware of your rights and intend to protect them.
2. **Audit Your Assets:** Make a list of everything you paid a freelancer to create. Check your contracts. If they don't say "Work Made for Hire" or "Assignment of Rights," you may not actually own your own logo or website.
3. **Register High-Value Assets:** If you have a core piece of intellectual property (like a unique software tool or a best-selling book), register it with the US Copyright Office. This is the only way to claim "statutory damages" (a set amount of money per infringement) in court, which is far easier than proving exactly how much money you lost from the theft.
