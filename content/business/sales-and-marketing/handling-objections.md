## Handling Objections

An objection is not a rejection; it is a request for more information. When a customer says "It's too expensive" or "I need to think about it," they are rarely saying "no." They are saying, "I don't yet see enough value to justify the cost or the risk." Most business owners panic when they hit an objection and either lower their price immediately or give up. A professional views an objection as the moment the real sale begins.

---

## The Framework: L.A.I.R.

To handle an objection without sounding desperate or argumentative, use the **L.A.I.R.** method. This process moves the conversation from a confrontation to a collaboration.

### 1. Listen
When the customer raises an objection, stop talking. Do not interrupt. Do not start formulating your rebuttal while they are speaking. Listen to the entire statement and allow for a moment of silence afterward. This proves you are listening and prevents you from reacting emotionally.

### 2. Acknowledge
Validate their concern without necessarily agreeing with it. This lowers their defensive walls. 
- **Wrong:** "Actually, it's not that expensive when you consider the quality."
- **Right:** "I completely understand. It is a significant investment, and you want to make sure it's the right move for your budget."

### 3. Isolate
Ensure that the objection you are discussing is the *only* thing standing in the way of the deal. If you solve the price issue, but they still have a trust issue, they still won't buy. 
- **The Question:** "Aside from the price, is there anything else that would stop you from moving forward with this today?"
- **The Result:** If they say "No, just the price," you have isolated the problem. Now you only have one hurdle to clear.

### 4. Resolve
Now that the objection is isolated, provide the solution. Do not lower the price; instead, increase the perceived value or change the terms.

## Common Objections and How to Solve Them

### Objection 1: "It's too expensive / I can't afford it."
This is rarely about the actual dollar amount; it's about the **Value Gap**. The customer doesn't believe the result is worth the price.
- **The Solve:** Shift the conversation from *Cost* to *Cost of Inaction*.
- **The Script:** "I hear you. It is a higher price than some of the other options. But if we go with a cheaper fix and it fails in six months, you'll be paying for this entire job again, plus the cost of the downtime. My price includes a [Warranty/Guarantee] that ensures you only pay for this once. Does it make more sense to pay a bit more now to avoid paying twice later?"

### Objection 2: "I need to think about it / I need to talk to my spouse."
This is a stalling tactic used to avoid making a decision. It is often a mask for a different, hidden objection.
- **The Solve:** Use the Isolation technique to find the real problem.
- **The Script:** "I completely understand. It's a big decision. Usually, when people need to think about it, it's because I've missed something or haven't explained a part of the process clearly. Just so I know, is it the price, the timeline, or something about the technical approach that you're unsure of?"

### Objection 3: "I've had a bad experience with [Similar Service] before."
This is a trust objection. They aren't doubting you; they are projecting a past trauma onto you.
- **The Solve:** Acknowledge the pain and contrast your process with the failure.
- **The Script:** "I'm sorry that happened. That's exactly why I do [Specific Step in Process]. Most people in this industry skip that step, which is why [Common Failure] happens. I make that a mandatory part of my workflow so you don't have to deal with that again."

## The Golden Rule: Never Lower Your Price First

When a customer asks for a discount, the worst thing you can do is immediately drop the price. This tells the customer two things: 1) Your original price was a lie, and 2) You are desperate for the work.

**The Professional Alternative: The Trade-Off**
If you must lower the price, you must remove a piece of the value. This maintains the integrity of your pricing.
- **Wrong:** "Okay, I can do it for $1,200 instead of $1,500."
- **Right:** "I can get the price down to $1,200, but to do that, we'll have to remove the [Premium Feature/Extended Warranty/Faster Timeline]. Which of those are you comfortable giving up to hit that budget?"

By requiring a trade-off, you force the customer to acknowledge that your time and expertise have a fixed value.
