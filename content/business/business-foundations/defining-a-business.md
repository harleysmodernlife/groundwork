## What Is a Business?

A business is any organized activity where someone provides goods or services in exchange for something of value — usually money.

That's it. No minimum size. No required structure. No employees needed.

The person cutting grass for neighbors on weekends and charging for it? That's a business. The woman selling handmade candles on Etsy? Business. The mechanic working out of his garage? Business.

The thing that makes something a business isn't its size or its legal structure. It's the **consistent exchange of value for compensation**.

---

## The Key Difference: Business vs. Hobby

This distinction matters more than most people realize — especially to the IRS.

A **hobby** is something you do primarily for personal enjoyment. If it sometimes makes money, great. But it's not organized around making money.

A **business** is organized with the intent to generate profit consistently. You're not just doing it when you feel like it — you're building something that runs whether you feel like it or not.

The IRS uses several tests to determine which one you're running. The most important: **did you make a profit in at least 3 of the last 5 years?** If not, they may classify your "business" as a hobby — and hobby losses aren't deductible.

## Why the Structure Matters From Day One

Most people start doing something, start making money at it, and then figure out the "business stuff" later. This is the wrong order.

Here's what can go wrong if you wait:

**Personal liability.** If you're operating without a formal business structure, you're operating as a sole proprietor by default. That means if your business gets sued, your personal bank account, car, and house are on the line.

**Tax problems.** Without clear records from the start, tax time becomes a nightmare of reconstructed receipts and guesses.

**Missed deductions.** Business expenses are deductible. But only if you can prove they were business expenses — which requires keeping them separate from personal spending.

**Credibility.** Clients and vendors treat you differently when you have a formal business entity. It signals that you're serious.

## What Every Business Needs

Regardless of size, every real business has these things:

1. **A value proposition** — what problem you solve and why people should pay you to solve it
2. **A way to get paid** — a business bank account, payment methods, invoicing
3. **Records** — income, expenses, transactions. This doesn't have to be complicated. A spreadsheet is fine to start.
4. **An understanding of what you owe** — taxes exist. They don't go away because you didn't know about them.

You don't need a fancy office. You don't need employees. You don't need a logo.

You need to know what you're offering, who you're offering it to, and how to keep track of the money.

---

## The Bottom Line

Starting a business doesn't require permission. It requires making a decision, setting up the right structure, and being organized about it from the start.

The people who fail at business — and most do fail — usually don't fail because their idea was bad. They fail because they treated the business stuff as an afterthought.

Don't be that person.
