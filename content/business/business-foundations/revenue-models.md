## Revenue Models

A revenue model is how your business makes money. Not what you sell — how money flows from customers to you. The same product or service can be structured with completely different revenue models, and the choice has major implications for cash flow, pricing, customer relationships, and scalability. Understanding the options lets you make that choice intentionally rather than defaulting to whatever seems most obvious.

---

## The Main Revenue Model Types

### One-Time Transaction (Direct Sales)

You sell something; the customer pays once; the sale is complete. The simplest model and the oldest.

**Examples:** A retail store, a freelance web design project, a furniture maker, a wedding photographer, a home inspector.

**Advantages:**
- Simple to understand and communicate
- No ongoing obligations after delivery
- Revenue is recognized immediately

**Disadvantages:**
- Revenue doesn't compound — you're always hunting for the next sale
- No predictability; revenue fluctuates with new sales
- Customer relationships often end at the transaction

**When it's right:** Physical goods, one-time services, project-based work where the engagement has a natural end point.

### Recurring Revenue / Subscription

Customers pay on a regular schedule (monthly, annually) for ongoing access to a product or service.

**Examples:** Software subscriptions (Adobe, Spotify, Netflix), gym memberships, retainer arrangements with lawyers or accountants, lawn care service, SaaS tools.

**Advantages:**
- Predictable revenue you can plan around
- Customer lifetime value compounds over time
- Lower cost of customer retention vs. acquisition
- Business has a valuation premium (investors and acquirers love recurring revenue)

**Disadvantages:**
- Harder to sell (ongoing commitment vs. one-time purchase)
- Churn (customers canceling) must be actively managed
- Often lower revenue in early months while subscriber base builds

**When it's right:** Services delivered continuously, software with ongoing value, businesses where ongoing relationships are natural and valuable.

### Retainer

A specific type of recurring revenue common in service businesses. The client pays a fixed monthly fee for ongoing access to your services or a defined scope of work.

**Examples:** PR agency, bookkeeper, marketing consultant, attorney, IT support.

**Advantages:**
- Predictable income for you; predictable cost for the client
- Deepens the client relationship over time
- Can be more efficient than billing hourly (no timekeeping required)

**Considerations:** Define scope carefully. A retainer without a clear definition of what's included leads to scope creep — the client asks for more and more and you're obligated to deliver without additional compensation.

### Usage-Based / Metered

Customers pay for what they use, like a utility.

**Examples:** AWS cloud computing (billed per GB, per compute hour), phone plans (per minute, per GB of data before the era of unlimited), some SaaS tools (per API call, per email sent), payment processors (per transaction).

**Advantages:**
- Low barrier to start — customers only pay when they use
- Revenue scales directly with customer usage and growth
- Fair: customers who use more pay more

**Disadvantages:**
- Unpredictable revenue
- Complex to bill
- Customers may aggressively optimize to minimize usage

**When it's right:** Businesses where usage varies significantly across customers and over time; infrastructure or API businesses.

### Marketplace / Percentage of Transaction

You facilitate transactions between buyers and sellers and take a percentage.

**Examples:** Etsy, eBay, Airbnb, Uber, staffing agencies, real estate agents.

**Advantages:**
- Revenue scales with platform volume
- No inventory required
- Network effects: more buyers attract more sellers, attracting more buyers

**Disadvantages:**
- Requires critical mass to be useful (the "cold start problem")
- Thin margins if percentage is small; high friction if percentage is large
- Platform dependency creates risk for both buyers and sellers

**When it's right:** Businesses that aggregate supply and demand; platforms where connecting parties is the core value.

### Licensing

You create intellectual property (software, content, brand, patents, recipes) and charge others for the right to use it.

**Examples:** Software license fees, music royalties, franchise fees (the franchisor licenses the brand and systems), content licensing (stock photos, music sync licenses).

**Advantages:**
- Revenue without delivering labor
- Highly scalable — one piece of IP can generate revenue from many licensees simultaneously
- Often passive once established

**Disadvantages:**
- Requires significant upfront creation and protection costs
- Enforcement can be complex
- Requires partners to succeed

### Freemium

A base version is free; premium features cost money. The goal is to convert free users to paying customers.

**Examples:** Spotify (free with ads; Premium without), Slack (free for small teams; paid for more history and integrations), Dropbox.

**Advantages:**
- Free removes the barrier to trial — high volume of users
- Free users provide word of mouth marketing
- Conversion to paid is easier when users already rely on the product

**Disadvantages:**
- Requires large user base to make the economics work (typically 2-5% convert to paid)
- Free tier must be good enough to use but not so good that paying seems unnecessary
- High infrastructure cost to serve free users

**When it's right:** Products that get better with network effects, software with high scalability and low marginal cost per user.

### Advertising

You provide content or a platform that attracts an audience; advertisers pay to access that audience.

**Examples:** Social media platforms, news sites, podcasts, YouTube channels.

**Advantages:**
- Can be free to users, enabling large-scale adoption
- Scales with audience size

**Disadvantages:**
- Audience and advertiser interests can conflict
- Requires very large scale to generate meaningful revenue
- Dependent on advertiser budgets (which can disappear in recessions)
- Privacy and user experience trade-offs

**When it's right:** Media businesses with very large audiences.

## Revenue Model Combinations

Most businesses use more than one revenue model. A gym has membership fees (subscription), personal training sessions (one-time transactions), and retail merchandise (direct sales). A software company has subscription revenue plus professional services plus licensing.

Common combinations:
- **Subscription + professional services:** Core product is subscription; implementation, training, and consulting are one-time fees
- **Freemium + direct sales:** Self-serve free tier; enterprise customers purchased through a sales team
- **One-time + recurring:** A wedding photographer charges a project fee, then offers ongoing licensing for use of the photos

---

## How to Choose

Start with what's natural for the transaction:
- **Is delivery continuous or episodic?** Continuous delivery → subscription. Episodic (one project, one event) → one-time transaction.
- **Do customers benefit from ongoing relationship?** Yes → retainer, subscription. No → transaction.
- **Does usage vary significantly between customers?** Yes → usage-based. No → flat fee.
- **Are you connecting two sides of a market?** Yes → marketplace.

Then consider the business implications:
- **Cash flow:** One-time transactions are faster but less predictable. Subscriptions build slower but compound.
- **Customer acquisition cost:** How expensive is it to get a customer? If CAC is high, you need high lifetime value — which favors recurring revenue models.
- **Scalability:** Can you serve more customers without proportionally more labor? If yes, recurring or licensing revenue compounds more attractively.

---

## Pricing Within the Revenue Model

The revenue model defines how you charge; pricing defines how much. These are separate decisions.

A subscription can be priced at $10/month, $99/month, or $999/month — completely different businesses despite the same model. Pricing is addressed in separate lessons; the important point here is not to conflate model choice with price point.

## Testing Your Revenue Model

Before committing fully to a revenue model:

**Look at what direct competitors charge.** Not to copy them, but to understand what customers in your market are accustomed to paying and how.

**Ask potential customers how they prefer to pay.** Particularly relevant when shifting from industry norms — e.g., from project-based to retainer. Customer preference matters.

**Run a small test.** Can you structure one deal according to the model you're considering and see how it actually performs?

The revenue model is a hypothesis until it's proven. Prove it with small experiments before building your entire business around it.
