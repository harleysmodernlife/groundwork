## What a Business Plan Actually Is

A business plan is a document that describes what your business does, who it serves, how it makes money, and whether the numbers add up. That's all it is. The elaborate 40-page documents you've seen in textbooks are one format, and usually the wrong format for most situations. Understanding what a business plan is actually for will help you write one that's useful instead of one that fills drawers.

---

## Who a Business Plan Is For

A business plan has two audiences, and the document looks different depending on which one you're writing for.

**For yourself:** Thinking through your business on paper forces you to confront assumptions you haven't tested. Do you actually know who your customers are? Have you calculated whether the revenue can cover your costs? Do you have a realistic plan for getting customers? Writing it down exposes gaps that feel obvious once you see them and invisible when they're only in your head.

**For outside readers:** Lenders (banks, SBA loan programs) and some investors require a business plan before they'll consider funding. These documents are longer and more formal, with financial projections, market analysis, and management backgrounds.

If you're self-funding and not seeking outside capital, you're writing for yourself. That means the document can be shorter, more practical, and more honest.

---

## The Five Questions Every Business Plan Answers

Regardless of format, any useful business plan answers these five questions:

### 1. What problem do you solve and for whom?

Describe what your business does and who it's for in plain language. Not marketing language — plain language. If someone asked you at a hardware store what your business does, what would you say?

"I repair appliances for homeowners in the south side of Denver."

"I make custom leather wallets sold online."

"I provide bookkeeping services to small restaurants."

The more specific the description, the more useful it is as a planning tool. "I sell things" is not a business. "I sell handmade ceramic mugs at farmers markets and on Etsy, primarily to women 30-50 who care about artisan goods" is a business you can build a plan around.

### 2. Who will pay you, and why will they choose you?

Who specifically is your customer? What makes your offer better than the alternatives they already have? This is not "everyone" — it's a specific group of people with a specific need you're positioned to meet.

If you can't describe why someone would choose you over existing options, that's not a gap in your writing — it's a gap in your business.

### 3. How will customers find out you exist?

This is the most underestimated question. You might have a perfect product at a perfect price with perfect positioning — and none of it matters if customers can't find you. How are you going to get in front of the people who need what you sell?

Specific answers: word of mouth from existing network, Google search with a website, door-to-door in a geographic area, social media presence, partnership with a complementary business, trade shows, direct mail. Not "marketing" — actual channels.

### 4. What does the money look like?

How much does it cost to deliver your product or service? How much will you charge? How many units/clients/projects at that price give you a living? What are your fixed costs (rent, software, insurance)?

Revenue – direct costs – overhead = profit. Can you make that math work?

A simple one-page spreadsheet answering these questions is more useful than elaborate financial modeling.

### 5. What do you need to get started, and do you have it?

Money, equipment, licenses, skills, time. What's required that you don't already have? How will you get it?

## What a Business Plan Is Not

**Not a prediction.** You cannot accurately predict your revenue 3 years out. Anyone who claims to can is either lying or deluded. Financial projections are scenarios and assumptions, not forecasts. Label them accordingly.

**Not a commitment to every detail.** Businesses change. The plan you write in January will be meaningfully wrong by June. The value is in the thinking, not in the document's accuracy.

**Not a substitute for talking to customers.** A plan written entirely from your assumptions and no customer conversations is a plan built on guesses. Get out and talk to actual potential customers before you write, not after.

**Not a guarantee of success.** A polished business plan doesn't make a weak business stronger. Lenders and investors know this. What a plan shows is that you've thought seriously about the questions that matter.

## Simple Business Plan Format

For most small businesses and side ventures, a short document covering these sections is sufficient:

**The Business**
- Business name and legal structure (sole proprietor, LLC, etc.)
- What you sell / what service you provide
- Where you operate (online, local, regional)

**The Customer**
- Who specifically you're serving
- What problem you're solving for them
- Why they would choose you

**Getting Customers**
- How you'll reach them
- What your first 10 customers will come from
- Ongoing marketing approach

**The Financials**
- Startup costs (one-time)
- Monthly operating costs
- Price per unit/service
- Break-even calculation: how many sales per month to cover costs
- Revenue target: what you need to earn and whether it's achievable

**What You Need**
- Equipment or inventory
- Licenses or permits
- Skills or training
- Funding (if any)

This is enough for most businesses. Write it honestly. If a section reveals a problem, that's the plan working as intended.

## The Lean Canvas

For startups or businesses with significant uncertainty, the Lean Canvas is a one-page format that forces the most important decisions:

- **Problem:** Top 3 problems you're solving
- **Customer segments:** Who has the problem
- **Unique value proposition:** Why your solution is different/better
- **Solution:** Your solution (briefly)
- **Channels:** How you reach customers
- **Revenue streams:** How you make money
- **Cost structure:** What it costs to run the business
- **Key metrics:** What numbers tell you if you're succeeding
- **Unfair advantage:** What competitors can't easily copy

The whole thing fits on one page. This is useful specifically because it fits on one page — you can't hide behind elaborate prose, you have to commit to specific answers.

## When You Need the Long Version

Banks, SBA loan programs, and most outside investors require more detailed documentation:

- **Executive summary** (1-2 pages describing the whole business)
- **Company description** (legal structure, location, history)
- **Market analysis** (industry size, target market research, competitive analysis)
- **Organization and management** (who's running it and their relevant experience)
- **Products and services** (detailed description)
- **Marketing and sales strategy**
- **Financial projections** (income statement, cash flow statement, balance sheet, typically 3-year projections)
- **Funding requirements** (how much you need and what for)

The Small Business Administration (SBA) has templates and guidance for this format. SCORE (a nonprofit with free small business mentoring) offers free plan review and guidance.

---

## Starting

Write a draft in one sitting. Don't wait until you have all the answers — a business plan with identified gaps is useful; a blank page is not. Note the questions you can't answer. Those are research tasks.

The goal of the first draft is not completeness. The goal is to see what you know and what you don't.
