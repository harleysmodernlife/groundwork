## Defining Your Customer

"My customers are everyone" is the same as saying "I have no customers." When you try to serve everyone, you end up with messaging that resonates with no one, a product positioned for nothing, and a marketing spend that produces very little. The counterintuitive truth of business: getting specific about who you serve makes it easier to reach them, easier to sell to them, and easier to make something they actually want.

---

## Why Customer Definition Matters

Every business decision flows downstream from who your customer is:

- **Product decisions:** What features to build, what quality level to aim for, what price point to target
- **Marketing decisions:** Where to advertise, what to say, what images and tone to use
- **Channel decisions:** Where to sell (online, retail, direct), how customers discover you
- **Pricing decisions:** What your customers can and will pay
- **Support decisions:** How customers want to be helped when something goes wrong

If your customer definition is vague, all of these decisions are arbitrary. If it's specific, each decision has a right answer you can reason toward.

---

## The Spectrum from Broad to Specific

**Too broad:** "People who need accounting services"
**Better:** "Small business owners who need bookkeeping"
**Specific:** "Restaurants with 1-3 locations who need monthly bookkeeping, don't want to hire an in-house bookkeeper, and have been in business for at least 2 years"

The specific version immediately tells you where to find them (restaurant trade associations, food industry forums, business networking groups near restaurants), what to say ("we specialize in restaurant bookkeeping"), and what they care about (cost control, freeing up owner time, reliability).

Getting to this level of specificity feels risky because you're excluding people. It's not risky — it's the only approach that works. You can always expand later. Starting narrow and learning is far better than starting broad and learning nothing.

## The Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)

An Ideal Customer Profile is a detailed description of the specific type of customer who is the best possible match for what you sell. Not an average customer — the best possible one.

### For B2B (Selling to Businesses)

Define the company:
- **Industry:** What sector are they in?
- **Size:** Number of employees, annual revenue
- **Geography:** Local, regional, national, or online anywhere?
- **Stage:** Startup, growth, established, struggling?
- **Budget:** Can they afford what you sell?

Define the person you're selling to within that company:
- **Role and title:** Who actually makes the purchasing decision?
- **Goals:** What are they trying to achieve?
- **Problems:** What's frustrating them or costing them money?
- **Decision triggers:** What would cause them to look for a solution right now?

### For B2C (Selling to Individual Consumers)

Define the person:
- **Demographics:** Age range, income level, location, family situation
- **Values and interests:** What do they care about? What do they spend time on?
- **Behaviors:** Where do they shop? How do they find new products/services? Who do they trust for recommendations?
- **Problem:** What specific problem do they have that you solve?
- **Alternatives they're currently using:** What are they doing now instead of buying from you?

## The Jobs-to-Be-Done Framework

A useful lens for understanding customers: instead of asking "who is my customer?", ask "what job is my customer trying to get done?"

People "hire" products and services to accomplish specific jobs in their lives. The job is not the product — it's the outcome the customer wants.

A cordless drill is hired to make holes. But that's not the real job. The real job is "hang a picture on the wall." If a wall anchor that required no drill existed, it would compete with the drill — not because it's a drill, but because it accomplishes the same job.

Understanding the job-to-be-done reveals:
- What the customer actually cares about (the outcome, not the product)
- Who your real competitors are (anything that accomplishes the same job)
- What messaging will resonate (speak to the outcome, not the features)

**Examples:**
- People don't buy gym memberships to exercise. They hire the gym to feel healthy, look better, or reduce guilt about not exercising.
- People don't buy project management software to manage tasks. They hire it to reduce the stress of dropped balls and missed deadlines.
- People don't hire accountants to file taxes. They hire them to avoid owing money and to not have to worry about it.

Ask your potential customers why they'd be looking for something like what you offer. The answers tell you the job.

## The "1000 True Fans" Principle

The musician Kevin Kelly popularized the idea that you don't need millions of customers to build a sustainable business — you need 1,000 true fans. A true fan is someone who will buy everything you make, tell their friends, and choose you over alternatives without extensive selling.

A thousand people at $100 per year is $100,000 in revenue. That's a real business.

This principle reframes customer definition: instead of trying to identify the largest possible market, identify the specific people who would love what you do most — the people for whom your product or service is exactly right. Build for them first. They'll help you find more people like them.

## Testing Your Customer Definition

Customer assumptions are just assumptions until you talk to actual people. Before building products, writing marketing, or spending money on ads:

**Interview potential customers.** Ask them about the problem you're trying to solve. Not "would you buy this?" (people say yes to be polite) but "tell me about the last time this was a problem for you. How did you handle it? What do you wish existed?" Listen for patterns.

**Look at who your current best customers are.** If you already have some customers, identify your best ones — the ones who pay on time, don't complain excessively, refer others, and come back. What do they have in common? That's your ICP.

**Find online communities where your potential customers gather.** Reddit forums, Facebook groups, industry forums, LinkedIn groups. What questions are they asking? What problems are they complaining about? What solutions are they already using?

## One Page: Your Customer Definition

Write this out and refer to it when making product and marketing decisions:

**Primary customer:**
[A specific description — not a demographic bucket, a person]

**Their primary job-to-be-done:**
[The outcome they're trying to achieve]

**Their biggest frustration with existing options:**
[Why what's currently available doesn't work well enough]

**How they currently handle this:**
[Their current workaround or alternative]

**What would make them choose you over everything else:**
[Your specific advantage for this specific customer]

**Where you find them:**
[Channels, platforms, events, places this person is]

Keep this document short and honest. Update it when you learn you were wrong about something. Being wrong about your customer definition early is cheap; being wrong about it after you've built a product, hired staff, or signed a lease is expensive.
