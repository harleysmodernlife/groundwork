## Why Business Structure Matters

The structure you choose determines three critical things:

1. **Who is liable if something goes wrong**
2. **How you pay taxes**
3. **How complex your paperwork is**

Getting this wrong doesn't immediately destroy your business. But it can cost you a lot of money and a lot of stress down the road.

---

## The Main Options

### Sole Proprietorship

This is the default. If you start doing business and don't file anything, you're automatically a sole proprietor.

**The good:** Dead simple. No forms, no fees, minimal paperwork. All income flows straight to your personal tax return.

**The bad:** Zero protection. You and the business are legally the same person. If your business gets sued and loses, they come after your house, your car, your bank account. There is no wall between you and the business.

Most small freelancers and side-gigs start here. It's fine to start here. But if you're taking on clients, doing work that carries any risk of liability, or making serious money — move on.

---

### LLC (Limited Liability Company)

The LLC is the workhorse of small business structures. It's what most entrepreneurs should start with.

**What it does:** Creates a legal separation between you and the business. The company is its own entity. If the company gets sued, they can generally only come after the company's assets — not yours personally.

**The good:** Real liability protection. Flexible tax options. Not that expensive to set up ($50-$500 depending on state). Minimal ongoing requirements compared to a corporation.

**The bad:** You have to actually maintain the separation. This means:
- A separate business bank account (this is non-negotiable)
- Not paying personal expenses from the business account
- Keeping records
- Filing an annual report in most states (usually $50-$100/year)

If you run your business finances through your personal account, courts can "pierce the corporate veil" — meaning they ignore the LLC and come after you personally anyway.

**Who it's for:** Almost every solo entrepreneur or small business. This is the right call for most people reading this.

---

### S-Corporation

An S-Corp isn't really a different structure — it's a tax election you can make on top of an LLC or corporation.

**What it does:** At a certain income level (generally $40,000+ profit), paying yourself a reasonable salary and taking additional profit as a distribution can reduce your self-employment tax burden.

**Who it's for:** Businesses making consistent profit where the tax savings justify the additional complexity. Talk to an accountant before going here — it's not for everyone and the setup/maintenance is more involved.

---

### C-Corporation

This is what big companies are. Multiple classes of stock, shareholders, boards of directors. Complicated.

**Who it's for:** Companies planning to raise venture capital or go public. Not you, not right now. Don't bother.

---

### Partnership

Two or more people running a business together without forming a formal entity.

**The bad:** Both partners are personally liable. And partnerships end badly almost as often as marriages — usually messier because money is involved.

If you're going into business with someone, form an LLC and have an operating agreement that spells out exactly who owns what, who does what, and what happens if someone wants out.

---

## What You Should Do

If you're serious about your business:

**Step 1:** Form an LLC in your state. Go to your state's Secretary of State website. File the Articles of Organization. Pay the fee.

**Step 2:** Get an EIN (Employer Identification Number) from the IRS. It's free. Takes 10 minutes at irs.gov. This is your business's tax ID number — like a Social Security number for the company.

**Step 3:** Open a business bank account. Use your EIN and LLC formation documents. From this point forward, all business money goes through this account. No exceptions.

**Step 4:** Get a simple bookkeeping system set up. Even a spreadsheet. Track every dollar in and every dollar out.

That's the foundation. Everything else builds on this.

## The Mistake That Kills Most Small Businesses

It's not a bad product. It's not bad marketing. It's not even bad luck.

It's paperwork.

Specifically: not keeping the business separate from personal life, not tracking money, not filing taxes correctly.

The IRS does not care that you didn't know. Penalties and interest accumulate. Back taxes can destroy a business that was otherwise perfectly viable.

The rules aren't complicated once you know them. The cost of ignoring them is very high.

Know the rules.
