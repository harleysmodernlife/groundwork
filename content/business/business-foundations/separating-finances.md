## Separating Business and Personal Finances

The single most common financial mistake small business owners make is running business money through personal accounts. It feels harmless at the start — the business is small, you're the only one involved, what's the difference? The difference is enormous, and it gets worse the longer you wait to fix it.

---

## Why It Matters

### Taxes

If you run business transactions through a personal account, you cannot produce a clean set of business financials at tax time without going through your entire bank statement line by line and guessing which expenses were business and which were personal. This is a miserable, error-prone process that accountants charge extra for because it takes extra time. It also means you'll miss deductions — because if you can't confidently categorize something as business, you may not claim it.

With separate accounts, you run a report in your accounting software and your business financials are complete.

### Legal Protection

If your business is an LLC or corporation (rather than a sole proprietorship), the legal protection those structures provide — keeping your personal assets separate from business liabilities — depends on maintaining a genuine separation between business and personal finances. This is called "maintaining the corporate veil."

Commingling funds (mixing personal and business money) is one of the main ways courts "pierce the corporate veil" — holding business owners personally liable for business debts because they weren't actually operating a distinct legal entity. The LLC provides no protection if you're using the business account as a personal slush fund.

### Clear Picture of Your Business

You cannot tell whether your business is profitable, cash-flow positive, or financially healthy if business and personal expenses are mixed together. Separated finances let you look at the business independently and know what's actually happening.

### Credibility

When you apply for a business loan, show financial statements to a partner, or work with an accountant, separated financials indicate that you run the business seriously.

## The Minimum Separation Required

**Business checking account:** Open a dedicated checking account at a bank or credit union for the business. All business income goes in. All business expenses come out. Never the other way.

That's the baseline. It costs nothing (many banks offer free business checking) and takes one afternoon to set up.

**Business credit card (strongly recommended):** A business credit card keeps additional separation for business purchases. Using it consistently creates a clean record of business expenses and earns points or cash back on business spending. Almost every business expense you can put on a card should go on the card.

**Business savings account (useful for tax reserves):** Business owners who pay quarterly estimated taxes benefit from a separate savings account designated for tax reserves. As money comes in, a portion goes to the tax account and doesn't get spent.

## How to Pay Yourself

If the business money is in a business account, how do you access it for personal use?

**Sole proprietorships and single-member LLCs:** Transfer money from the business account to your personal account. This is called an "owner's draw." It's just a transfer between accounts, not a salary or paycheck. You're the owner; you can draw money out whenever the business can afford it.

**S-Corps and C-Corps:** More structured. If you work in the business, the IRS requires you to pay yourself a "reasonable salary" (as a W-2 employee) before taking additional distributions. The salary is subject to payroll taxes; distributions are not. This structure can have tax advantages at higher income levels but adds payroll administration complexity.

**How much to draw:** Take enough to cover your personal expenses. Leave enough in the business account to cover upcoming expenses and give you a buffer. As the business grows, draw more. Don't drain the business account based on your personal wants — the business needs reserves.

## What to Do with Commingled Finances You Already Have

If you've been running the business through a personal account, here's the path forward:

1. **Open a business account now.** Stop the problem from accumulating further.
2. **Route all future income to the new business account.** Update any payment processors, invoicing software, or client payment instructions.
3. **Route all future business expenses through the business account or business credit card.**
4. **For the past period of commingled finances:** This is a cleanup job. Either do it yourself (go through statements, categorize everything) or pay an accountant or bookkeeper to clean it up. It costs money but is finite — you only do it once.

## Setting Up Accounting Software

Once you have a dedicated business account, connect it to simple accounting software:

**QuickBooks Online:** The most common small business accounting tool. Has more features than most small businesses need, but accountants and bookkeepers universally know how to use it.

**Wave:** Free accounting software for small businesses. Adequate for most simple businesses — invoicing, expense tracking, basic reporting.

**FreshBooks:** Strong invoicing and time-tracking features. Good for service businesses that invoice clients.

**Xero:** Cloud-based, good bank reconciliation, popular outside the US.

Connect your bank account(s) and credit card(s) to the software. Transactions import automatically. You categorize them. The software produces reports. This takes 30-60 minutes per week for most small businesses.

## Business Entity and Accounts

Different business structures have different requirements:

**Sole proprietorship:** No legal separation required, but financial separation is still strongly recommended. Open a business checking account in your name with a DBA (Doing Business As) designation if you operate under a business name.

**LLC:** Should have a dedicated bank account in the company's name. Most banks require your EIN (Employer Identification Number from the IRS) and LLC formation documents to open. Get the EIN first (free from the IRS, takes 5 minutes online), then open the account.

**Corporation:** Same as LLC requirements.

The account title matters: it should say "[Business Name], LLC" not your personal name. Business accounts in the company's name with business documentation are what courts look for as evidence of a real, separate business.

---

## The EIN

An Employer Identification Number (EIN) is the business equivalent of a Social Security Number. It's issued by the IRS and used to identify the business for tax purposes.

**When you need one:**
- You have or will have employees
- Your business is an LLC, partnership, or corporation (even if no employees)
- You want to open a business bank account in the company's name
- You're required to file employment taxes

**When you might not need one:**
- Sole proprietor with no employees (you can use your SSN)
- Single-member LLC taxed as a sole proprietor (but a separate EIN is still a good idea for account separation)

**Getting one:** Apply online at IRS.gov (search "EIN application"). It's free and instant. Do not pay a third-party service for this.

## The Weekly Money Habit

The separation only works if you maintain it. A simple weekly routine:

1. Log into your accounting software. (10 minutes)
2. Categorize any uncategorized transactions.
3. Check your account balance.
4. Make sure any invoices due have been paid.

That's the whole routine for most small businesses. The separation makes this simple; the commingling makes it impossible.
