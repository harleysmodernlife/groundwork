## When to Hire (and When Not To)

Hiring is the most consequential decision most business owners make, and it's almost always made too fast or too slow — either before the business can support it, or so late that the owner is collapsing under the workload. Understanding when hiring actually makes sense, and what it actually costs, prevents both mistakes.

---

## What Hiring Actually Costs

The salary number on the job posting is not the cost of an employee. The full cost is substantially higher:

**Payroll taxes:** Employers pay their share of Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) taxes on every dollar of wages — the FICA taxes. You also pay federal and state unemployment taxes. Combined, employer-side payroll taxes add roughly 7-10% to wages.

**Benefits:** Health insurance, if you offer it, is typically $300-800+/month per employee depending on the plan and how much you cover. Paid time off is additional effective cost per hour. Retirement contributions (if offered) add more.

**Workers' compensation insurance:** Required in most states for employees. Cost varies by industry (dangerous work = higher rates) but typically 1-5% of payroll.

**Overhead:** More employees means more space, more equipment, more software licenses, more administrative work.

**Training and onboarding time:** New employees are not immediately productive. Expect 1-3 months before a new hire is fully contributing, during which you're paying full wages while managing and training them.

**Your time managing:** Managing an employee takes time — typically 20-30% of a full-time employee's hours in actual management work (feedback, answering questions, addressing problems, performance reviews).

A rule of thumb: the true cost of an employee is **1.2 to 1.4 times their base salary** in direct costs alone, before accounting for your management time.

---

## The Revenue Test

Before hiring, confirm the math works:

**What revenue will this employee generate or protect?**

If you're hiring a salesperson, what's the realistic revenue they'll bring in within 6-12 months? Does that exceed their fully-loaded cost? If you're hiring a production person, does their addition allow you to take on enough additional business to cover their cost?

**What happens if the revenue doesn't materialize?**

The business must be able to pay the employee whether or not the projected growth happens. Hiring for growth that hasn't happened yet is a risky bet. The more dependent the hire is on projected revenue, the more dangerous it is.

---

## Signs You're Ready to Hire

**You're turning down work.** You have more demand than you can serve and you're declining or losing customers because of capacity. This is the clearest signal. The revenue is there; you need the capacity to deliver it.

**You're doing work that someone cheaper should be doing.** As the business owner, your time is the most expensive in the business. If you're doing tasks that someone could do for $20/hour while you have $100/hour work waiting, you're spending $100 to do $20 work. Hire for the lower-value work and free yourself for the higher-value work.

**You've confirmed the financials work.** You've run the numbers: the business currently generates revenue above expenses, and the added revenue or freed owner capacity from a hire will more than cover the employee's fully-loaded cost.

**You have 3-6 months of their salary in reserve.** Not required, but strongly advisable. If the hire doesn't work out, or revenue dips, you need to be able to pay them while you figure it out.

---

## Signs You're Not Ready to Hire

**You're hoping revenue will grow to cover it.** Hiring into hoped-for growth puts you in a precarious position if the growth doesn't arrive on schedule. Wait until the revenue is there, or nearly there.

**You're exhausted and want relief.** This is completely understandable but the wrong reason to hire. Hiring out of desperation leads to rushed decisions, no bandwidth for proper onboarding, and a hire that doesn't fix the underlying problem (often that you haven't figured out what you actually need).

**You haven't tried contractors first.** A contractor tests the need without the full commitment. If you're unsure whether you have enough ongoing work to justify a full hire, start with a contractor (see the Contractor vs. Employee lesson).

**The financials don't work yet.** If the business isn't profitable enough to cover the additional cost, you need to fix the business's revenue and expenses before adding headcount.

## Your First Hire: Likely What to Hire For

Most small businesses need their first hire in one of these areas:

**Administrative/operations:** Scheduling, answering emails, basic bookkeeping, customer service. Often the first relief a busy owner needs. Look for someone organized, communication-capable, and reliable. This role often evolves significantly.

**Delivery/production:** Someone to help deliver the service or make the product — a second technician, a second teacher, a second chef. This hire directly enables revenue growth by expanding your capacity.

**Sales:** Only hire a salesperson if you have capacity to deliver what they'll sell. A salesperson who brings in business you can't deliver damages your reputation. Sales hires are also risky in early stages because they take time to ramp up.

**Specialized expertise:** A skill you lack that's creating a bottleneck. A graphic designer for a marketing-heavy business, a bookkeeper for a numbers-heavy one.

The choice should be driven by: what is the biggest bottleneck to the business growing right now?

## The Part-Time or Fractional Approach

You don't have to hire full-time. For many small businesses, a part-time employee is the right first step:

- 10-20 hours per week for administrative support
- A bookkeeper one day per month
- A delivery person for the three busy days of the week

Part-time employees are still full employees (payroll taxes, potentially workers' comp), but the cost is proportional to hours. This tests whether the hire is needed, trains you as a manager, and leaves room to grow.

A "fractional" hire is the same concept applied to higher-level roles: a fractional CFO works 10 hours per month on your finances. A fractional marketing director works 20 hours per month on your marketing. You get experienced help without the full-time commitment.

## The Hiring Timeline

Hiring always takes longer than expected. A realistic timeline from "I need to hire" to "new person is productive":

- **Job description and posting:** 1-2 weeks
- **Applications collected:** 2-4 weeks
- **Screening and interviews:** 2-4 weeks
- **Offer, acceptance, background check:** 1-2 weeks
- **Start date to current employer (notice period):** 2-4 weeks
- **Onboarding and ramp-up to full productivity:** 4-12 weeks

Total: 3-6 months from "decide to hire" to "fully productive new employee."

This means you need to start the process well before you're desperate. Starting when you're already buried means you're evaluating candidates while drowning and making worse decisions under pressure.

## What You're Responsible for as an Employer

**Legal obligations:**
- Verify the employee is legally authorized to work in the US (Form I-9)
- File a new hire report with your state
- Set up payroll withholding (federal income tax, FICA)
- Provide pay stubs showing withholding
- Pay employer-side taxes
- Maintain workers' compensation insurance (usually required)
- Comply with federal and state employment laws (FLSA minimum wage, overtime, anti-discrimination laws)

This is real administrative burden. Payroll software (Gusto, ADP, Paychex, QuickBooks Payroll) handles most of it automatically and is worth the cost.

---

## The Most Important Thing

Hire slowly. The cost of a bad hire — wages during an unproductive period, time spent managing performance problems, severance or unemployment if it ends poorly, the delay in finding the right person — is significant. Rushing to fill a seat almost always costs more than the slightly longer search for the right person.

Define exactly what you need before you look. The clearer your picture of who would succeed in this role, the better your ability to evaluate candidates. Hiring is not finding someone who seems nice and can probably figure it out — it's matching a specific person to a specific defined need.
