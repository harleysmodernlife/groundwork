## Finding Good People

Most businesses fail at hiring because they treat it like a lottery—they post a job ad and hope the "perfect" candidate happens to see it. This is called passive recruiting. The problem is that high-quality talent—the top 10% of workers—rarely spend their day browsing job boards; they are already working for someone else and are generally satisfied enough not to be actively searching. To acquire top-tier talent, a business owner must shift from recruiting to scouting. Scouting is the active process of identifying, vetting, and attracting people who are already proving their value in the real world.

---

## The Three Sourcing Channels

To build a high-performance team, you must use multiple channels to find candidates. Each channel serves a different purpose and attracts a different type of worker.

### 1. The Trusted Network (Referrals)
Referrals are the highest-quality source of talent because they come with a built-in vetting process. A current high-performer is unlikely to recommend an incompetent worker because it reflects poorly on their own judgment and disrupts their own workflow.

- **The Surgical Request:** Do not ask "Do you know anyone looking for work?" This is too vague and attracts people who are desperate for any job. Instead, ask for the best person the referrer has ever worked with. 
    - **Example:** "I am looking for a project manager who has managed budgets over $50k and can operate without daily supervision. Who is the best person you have ever worked for in that role?"
- **The Referral Bonus Structure:** To incentivize your best people to scout for you, offer a referral bonus. To prevent "friend-hiring" (hiring someone just because they are friends), split the bonus: 20% after the new hire completes their first 30 days, and 80% after they hit 90 days. This ensures the referrer only suggests people who are a genuine fit and intended to stay.

### 2. Active Scouting (The Hunter Approach)
Active scouting involves identifying people who are already delivering results for someone else and inviting them to join your business. This is how you acquire "A-Players."

- **Where to Scout:** Look at your competitors, but also look at adjacent industries. If you need an operations manager, look for people running high-efficiency logistics in a different sector. Look for people who are consistently praised in public forums, industry awards, or who maintain high-quality professional portfolios.
- **The Outreach Strategy:** Your first contact should not be a job offer; it should be a recognition of their specific skill. 
    - **Outreach Template:** "Hi [Name], I've been following your work on [Specific Project] and was particularly impressed by how you handled [Specific Detail]. We are currently scaling our [Department] and are looking for someone with your level of precision. I'm not sure if you're looking for a move, but I'd love to buy you a coffee and tell you about the problem we're trying to solve."
- **The Value Proposition:** Top talent is rarely moved by a small salary increase. They are moved by autonomy, the ability to solve a more interesting problem, or a better culture. Your pitch should focus on the gap between where they are now and where they could be with you. Focus on the *impact* they will have, not the *tasks* they will perform.

### 3. Public Postings (The Wide Net)
Use job boards (Indeed, LinkedIn, ZipRecruiter) primarily for entry-level roles or highly specialized technical skills where the pool is naturally small. The danger of public postings is the "application flood," which wastes hours of management time.

- **The Repellent Filter:** Design your job post to repel the wrong people. Include a specific, small instruction in the middle of the text. 
    - **Example:** "To be considered, please start your cover letter with the phrase 'Precision First' and list your favorite tool in the first paragraph."
- **The Logic:** If a candidate cannot follow a simple, explicit instruction in a job ad, they will lack the attention to detail required for the job. You can instantly delete any application that misses this instruction, saving you hours of vetting.

## Job Description Architecture

A job description is a marketing document designed to attract a specific type of person and repel everyone else. Most job descriptions are boring lists of duties that attract "job seekers" rather than "high performers."

### Outcome-Based vs. Task-Based
Instead of listing every single task the person will do, focus on the **Outcomes** and the **Constraints**.

- **Task-Based (Wrong):** "Must be able to answer phones, schedule appointments, and manage the calendar."
- **Outcome-Based (Right):** "Your primary goal is to ensure the owner's calendar is optimized for revenue-generating work, meaning no gaps in the schedule and zero double-bookings."

### The Three Core Sections of a High-Performance Job Ad
1. **The Mission:** Explain why this role exists and what the business wins when the person succeeds. "We are building the most reliable HVAC service in the county; we need an operator who can ensure every customer gets a call-back within 2 hours."
2. **The Non-Negotiables:** List the 3-5 hard skills or traits that are absolute requirements. If they don't have these, the rest doesn't matter. "Must have 3+ years of experience with X software; must be comfortable working in high-heat environments."
3. **The Performance Metric:** Tell them exactly how they will be judged. "Success in this role looks like maintaining a 4.8-star customer rating while increasing job completion rate by 10%."

## Vetting for Competence vs. Confidence

Interviews are a performance. Many people are excellent at the *act* of interviewing—they are charismatic, confident, and know the right buzzwords—but they are terrible at actually executing the work. You must move from "talking about work" to "demonstrating work."

### The Work Sample Test
Never hire based on a resume and a conversation alone. A resume is a marketing document. Every candidate must pass a small, paid trial project that mirrors the actual tasks they will perform.

- **Application Examples:**
    - **For an Administrative Assistant:** Give them three conflicting calendar appointments and a messy list of client notes, then ask them to organize the schedule and draft a professional confirmation email to the clients.
    - **For a Technician/Mechanic:** Give them a vehicle with a known, non-destructive fault and ask them to diagnose it and write a brief report on the necessary parts and estimated labor time.
    - **For a Salesperson:** Give them a real-world objection you've faced recently and ask them to record a 2-minute video or audio clip of how they would handle it.

- **The Grading Rubric:** Grade the work sample on these four metrics:
    - **Accuracy (Binary):** Did they solve the problem? If they failed the core task, the interview is over.
    - **Efficiency:** Did they take 2 hours for a 30-minute task? Did they over-engineer the solution?
    - **Communication:** Did they ask clarifying questions before starting, or did they make assumptions that led to errors?
    - **Attention to Detail:** Did they follow the formatting and submission instructions exactly?

### The Behavioral Interview (The Drill-Down)
Avoid hypothetical questions like "What would you do if a customer was angry?" People will give you the "correct" answer, not the true answer. Instead, ask for specific past examples: "Tell me about a time you had to deal with a customer who was genuinely angry. What happened, and how did you resolve it?"

- **The Three-Why Method:** Once they give an answer, drill down into the details to separate the doers from the talkers.
    1. "Why did you choose that approach?"
    2. "How exactly did you implement that?"
    3. "What was the specific result?"
- **The Tell:** A person who actually did the work can provide granular, boring details (the specific software they used, the exact words they said, the time of day). A person who is faking it will stay at the surface level and use generalities like "I just handled it professionally."

## Red Flags in the Hiring Process

Recognizing a bad hire early saves thousands of dollars in turnover costs and prevents toxic culture from taking root.

- **The Former-Boss Bash:** If a candidate spends their interview complaining about their previous employer, they are a liability. Professional people focus on a lack of alignment or a desire for growth. People who trash their last boss will eventually trash you.
- **Vague Metrics:** Beware of phrases like "I helped grow the company" or "I was responsible for a lot of sales." Demand numbers. "I grew monthly revenue from $12k to $20k over six months" is a fact. "I helped grow the company" is a story.
- **The Small-Stuff Negotiator:** If a candidate spends significant time fighting over a minor perk (like a specific brand of coffee or a $20 monthly stipend) before they have even proven their value, it is a preview of their behavior on the job.
- **The Passive Interviewee:** A high-performer is interviewing you as much as you are interviewing them. They should have specific questions about your business goals, your current bottlenecks, and how their role contributes to the bottom line. Someone with no questions is just looking for a paycheck, not a career.

## The Decision: Aptitude vs. Experience

When choosing between two candidates, you must decide whether you value current skill (Experience) or the rate of growth (Aptitude).

- **The Experience Trap:** Hiring the person with the most experience can be a mistake if they have reached their ceiling. They know "how it's always been done," which makes them resistant to new processes and slow to adapt to your specific business needs.
- **The Aptitude Win:** Hiring someone with slightly less experience but high aptitude (the ability to learn and apply new information quickly) is often the better long-term move. These employees are more adaptable and will eventually surpass the "experienced" hire because they are not fighting their own old habits.
- **The Decision Framework:**
    - **Prioritize Experience when:** The role requires immediate, high-stakes execution where a mistake could be catastrophic (e.g., a Lead Electrician, a Head Accountant). You cannot afford a learning curve when the risk is structural or legal.
    - **Prioritize Aptitude when:** The role is about growth, scaling a system, or adapting to a changing market (e.g., an Operations Manager, a Marketing Lead). You need someone who can evolve as the business evolves.

## The Validation: The 30-Day Probationary Trial

Signing the offer letter is not the end of the hiring process; it is the beginning of the final vetting stage. Every new hire should be on a 30-day probationary trial.

### Setting the 30-Day Milestone
Do not judge a new hire on "vibes." Set 3-5 specific, measurable goals they must hit by day 30. 
- **Example:** "By day 30, you must be able to complete a full customer intake and invoice without any errors, and you must have successfully documented one new internal process in the wiki."

### Cutting Losses Early
If a hire is failing, do not wait for the 90-day mark to let them go. The cost of keeping a bad hire for an extra 60 days is higher than the cost of restarting the search. If they miss their 30-day milestones and show no aptitude for improvement, terminate the relationship immediately. It is better to be short-staffed than to have a low-performer poisoning your culture.
