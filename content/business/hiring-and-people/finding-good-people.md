## Finding Good People

Most businesses fail at hiring because they treat it like a lottery—they post a job ad and hope the "perfect" candidate happens to see it. High-quality talent rarely spends their day browsing job boards; they are already working for someone else. To find the top 10% of workers, you have to stop recruiting and start scouting.

---

## The Three Sourcing Channels

Depending on the role, you should prioritize your search in a different order.

### 1. The Trusted Network (Highest Quality)
Referrals are the gold standard. A current high-performer is unlikely to recommend a lazy worker because it makes them look bad. 
- **The Move:** Don't just ask "Do you know anyone?" Be specific. "I'm looking for a project manager who can handle a $50k budget and doesn't need their hand held. Who is the best person you've ever worked for?"
- **The Incentive:** Offer a referral bonus that pays out *after* the new hire hits 90 days. This ensures the referrer only suggests people who actually stay and perform.

### 2. Active Scouting (The Hunter Approach)
Go where the talent is already proving their value. If you need a great salesperson, look at the top performers at your competitors or in adjacent industries.
- **The Approach:** Reach out with a specific compliment about their work. "I saw the way you handled the [Specific Project] and it's exactly the kind of precision we need." 
- **The Pitch:** Don't offer a "job"; offer a better environment, more autonomy, or a more challenging problem to solve. Top talent is driven by growth, not just a slightly higher salary.

### 3. Public Postings (The Wide Net)
Use job boards (Indeed, LinkedIn, ZipRecruiter) only for entry-level roles or highly specialized technical skills where the pool is small.
- **The Filter:** Your job post should be designed to *repel* the wrong people. Include a "test" in the application (e.g., "Put the word 'Screwdriver' in the subject line"). If they can't follow a simple instruction in the ad, they will never follow instructions on the job.

## Vetting for Competence vs. Confidence

Interviews are a performance. Many people are great at *interviewing* but terrible at *working*. You must move from "talking about work" to "doing work."

### The Work Sample Test
Never hire based on a resume and a conversation. Give them a small, paid trial project.
- **For a Writer:** Give them a 500-word topic and a 24-hour deadline.
- **For a Tech:** Give them a broken piece of code and ask them to find the bug.
- **For a Manager:** Give them a messy spreadsheet of data and ask them to find the three biggest inefficiencies.

### The Behavioral Interview
Stop asking hypothetical questions ("What would you do if...") because people lie to sound smart. Ask for specific past examples ("Tell me about a time when...").
- **The Drill-Down:** Once they give an answer, ask "Why?" and "How?" three times. A person who actually did the work can give you granular details. A person who is faking it will start to give vague, general answers.

## Red Flags in the Hiring Process

- **Badmouthing previous employers:** A professional focuses on the lack of alignment, not the personality conflict. If they trash their last boss in an interview, you'll be next.
- **Vague achievements:** "I helped grow the company" vs. "I increased monthly recurring revenue from $10k to $25k in six months." No specifics means no proof.
- **Over-negotiating small stuff:** Fighting over a $50 phone stipend before they've even started is a preview of how they'll behave on the job.
- **No questions for you:** A high-performer interviews you as much as you interview them. Someone with no questions about your business, your goals, or your challenges isn't thinking strategically — they just want a paycheck.

---

## The Final Decision: Hire for Slope, Not Y-Intercept

In a graph, the y-intercept is where you are now (current skill), and the slope is how fast you are learning. 
- **The Trap:** Hiring the person with the highest current skill but a flat slope. They are great today, but they will be obsolete in two years.
- **The Win:** Hiring the person with slightly lower current skill but a steep slope. They may take a few weeks to get up to speed, but they will eventually surpass everyone else in the company.
