## What Cash Flow Is (and Why It Kills Businesses)

Profit is an accounting concept. Cash flow is a physical reality. The most dangerous lie a business owner can believe is that "Profit = Cash." You can be the most profitable company in your industry and still go bankrupt on a Tuesday morning because you ran out of cash to pay your employees.

---

## Profit vs. Cash Flow: The Fundamental Difference

**Profit** is what is left over after all your expenses are subtracted from your revenue *on paper*. 
**Cash Flow** is the actual movement of money into and out of your bank account. 

### The Timing Gap Example
Imagine you land a $10,000 project. 
1. **Day 1:** You spend $2,000 on materials. (Cash Flow: -$2,000)
2. **Day 15:** You finish the job and send an invoice for $10,000. (Profit: +$8,000)
3. **Day 30:** Your rent and payroll are due ($3,000). (Cash Flow: -$3,000)

At Day 30, your books say you have a **Profit of $8,000**. But your bank account is **-$5,000**. You are profitable, but you are broke. This is the "Timing Gap," and it is how most small businesses die.

---

## The Three Types of Cash Flow

1. **Operating Cash Flow:** Money generated from your actual business activities. This should be your primary source of cash.
2. **Investing Cash Flow:** Money spent on or earned from assets (e.g., buying a new truck, selling a piece of machinery).
3. **Financing Cash Flow:** Money coming from loans, investors, or your own pocket (and the money going back to them as repayments).

---

## The Burn Rate

Burn rate is the amount of cash your business spends each month to stay alive, regardless of income. 
- **Gross Burn:** Your total monthly expenses (Rent + Payroll + Software + Utilities).
- **Net Burn:** Your total monthly expenses minus your monthly revenue.

**The Runway Calculation:**
$$\text{Runway (Months)} = \frac{\text{Total Cash in Bank}}{\text{Monthly Net Burn}}$$

If you have $20,000 in the bank and your net burn is $2,000/month, you have a 10-month runway. When the runway hits zero, the business is dead unless you find new revenue or new financing.

---

## How to Fix Cash Flow Problems

If you are profitable but cash-poor, you don't have a sales problem; you have a timing problem. Use these levers:

- **Tighten Payment Terms:** Move from "Net 30" (payment in 30 days) to "Due on Receipt" or "Net 7." 
- **Require Deposits:** Never start a project without a deposit. This uses the customer's cash to fund the COGS, eliminating the initial cash dip.
- **Implement Milestone Payments:** For long projects, bill at 25%, 50%, and 100%. This keeps cash flowing in throughout the project life.
- **Negotiate Supplier Terms:** Ask your suppliers for longer payment terms (Net 30 or Net 60). This allows you to get paid by the customer *before* you have to pay the supplier.
- **Maintain a Cash Buffer:** A healthy business should have 3-6 months of operating expenses in a liquid reserve. This buffer turns a "crisis" into a "bad month."

---

## The Danger Signs

- **Growing AR (Accounts Receivable):** Your customers are taking longer to pay. You are essentially giving them an interest-free loan while you struggle to pay your own bills.
- **Relying on a Line of Credit for Payroll:** If you need a loan to pay your employees, your business model is fundamentally broken or your timing gap is unsustainable.
- **Unable to Buy Materials for New Jobs:** The ultimate red flag. When you can't afford the COGS for a job that would make you a profit, you have hit a cash flow wall.
