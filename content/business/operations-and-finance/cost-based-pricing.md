## Cost-Based Pricing: The Floor of Your Business

Cost-based pricing is the most basic way to set prices. It involves calculating exactly how much it costs to produce a product or deliver a service and then adding a fixed percentage (a markup) on top to create a profit. While simple, it is a dangerous way to run a business because it ignores the market and the customer. Cost-based pricing tells you the absolute minimum you can charge without losing money—it does not tell you the maximum you *can* charge.

---

## The Calculation: The Markup Method

To use cost-based pricing, you must have a perfect handle on your Cost of Goods Sold (COGS). If you miss a single expense, your profit margin disappears.

### 1. Calculate Total Direct Costs
Sum every single expense that is tied directly to the unit of work.
- **Materials:** Every screw, board, or component.
- **Direct Labor:** The hourly rate of the person doing the work multiplied by the time it takes. (Do not forget to include "burdened labor"—taxes and insurance).
- **Consumables:** Sandpaper, glue, electricity for the machine, etc.

### 2. Apply the Markup
Decide on the profit percentage you want to earn. 
- **Example:** Your total cost to build a table is $200. You want a 50% markup.
- **The Math:** $200 + ($200 \times 0.50) = $300.

### 3. The Margin vs. Markup Trap
Many business owners confuse markup with margin. This is a common way to lose money.
- **Markup** is the percentage added to the cost. ($100 cost + 50% markup = $150 price).
- **Margin** is the percentage of the final price that is profit. ($150 price - $100 cost = $50 profit. $50 / $150 = 33% margin).

If you aim for a 33% margin but apply a 33% markup, you are undercharging your customers.

---

## When Cost-Based Pricing Works

This method is effective in specific scenarios:
- **Commodity Markets:** When you are selling something identical to everyone else (e.g., gravel, standard lumber) and the price is set by the market. You compete on efficiency, not value.
- **Subcontracting:** When you are doing a specific task for another business that has already sold the project. You provide a cost-plus-fee quote.
- **Internal Benchmarking:** Using it as a "sanity check" to ensure your value-based prices aren't accidentally falling below your cost of production.

---

## The Fatal Flaws of Cost-Based Pricing

1. **The "Efficiency Penalty":** If you get better and faster at your job, your costs go down. If you use cost-based pricing, you are forced to lower your prices, meaning you are effectively punished for becoming more skilled.
2. **Ignoring the Customer:** It assumes the customer cares about how much it cost you to make the product. They don't. They care about what the product does for them.
3. **The Race to the Bottom:** When every competitor uses cost-based pricing, the only way to win is to lower your costs. This leads to cutting corners, lower quality, and eventually, bankruptcy.

---

## Practical Application: The "Floor" Strategy

Use cost-based pricing to establish your **Price Floor**. 

1. Calculate your total burdened cost for the job.
2. Add a minimum survival margin (e.g., 20%) to cover basic overhead.
3. This number is your "Walk Away" point. If the market won't pay this, the job is a liability, not an opportunity. 

Once the floor is set, move to Value-Based Pricing to determine the actual price you charge.
