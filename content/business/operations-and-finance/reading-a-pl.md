## Reading a Basic P&L (Profit and Loss Statement)

A Profit and Loss (P&L) statement—also called an Income Statement—is the primary scoreboard for your business. While a bank balance tells you how much cash you have *right now*, the P&L tells you if your business is actually making money or slowly bleeding out. If you cannot read a P&L, you are flying a plane without an altimeter; you might feel like you're cruising, but you have no idea how close you are to the ground.

---

## The Fundamental Equation

The entire P&L is built on one simple math problem:

**Total Revenue - Total Expenses = Net Income (Profit or Loss)**

If the number is positive, you have a profit. If it is negative, you have a loss (often called "running in the red"). However, the real value of a P&L isn't the final number—it's the breakdown of how you got there.

---

## The Anatomy of a P&L

A professional P&L is read from top to bottom. Each section filters the money further until you reach the bottom line.

### 1. Total Revenue (The Top Line)
This is the total amount of money your business brought in from sales before any expenses are taken out. 
- **Gross Sales:** The total value of all invoices sent.
- **Net Sales:** Gross sales minus any returns, discounts, or allowances.

### 2. Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
COGS are the "direct costs" required to produce your product or deliver your service. If you don't sell anything, your COGS should be zero.
- **Example (Mechanic):** The cost of the oil, filters, and brake pads used for a specific job.
- **Example (Consultant):** The cost of a specific software license required only for one client's project.

### 3. Gross Profit
**Calculation: Total Revenue - COGS = Gross Profit**

Gross Profit tells you if your pricing is correct. If your Gross Profit is too low, you are either charging too little or your materials are too expensive. You cannot fix a business by increasing sales if the Gross Profit is negative—you'll just lose money faster.

### 4. Operating Expenses (OpEx / Overhead)
These are the "indirect costs" of staying in business. You pay these regardless of whether you have one customer or one hundred.
- **Fixed Costs:** Rent, insurance, software subscriptions, salaries.
- **Variable Costs:** Marketing spend, electricity, travel.

### 5. Net Income (The Bottom Line)
**Calculation: Gross Profit - Operating Expenses = Net Income**

This is the actual amount of money the business earned. This is the money available to be paid out to the owner as a draw or reinvested back into the company.

## Key Metrics to Watch

To analyze a P&L, don't just look at the raw numbers; look at the percentages (Vertical Analysis). Divide every line by the Total Revenue.

| Metric | Calculation | What it Tells You |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Gross Margin %** | (Gross Profit / Revenue) x 100 | The efficiency of your production. High margin = high value/low cost. |
| **OpEx Ratio** | (Total OpEx / Revenue) x 100 | How "heavy" your business is. If this is 80%, you have very little room for error. |
| **Net Profit Margin** | (Net Income / Revenue) x 100 | The ultimate health check. A 10-20% net margin is generally healthy for small service businesses. |

## Common Mistakes and Misconceptions

### Profit $\neq$ Cash
This is the most dangerous mistake a new owner makes. You can show a profit on your P&L but have zero dollars in the bank. This happens because of **Timing**:
- You sent an invoice for $5,000 (Revenue is logged on the P&L).
- You paid $2,000 in rent (Expense is logged on the P&L).
- Your P&L shows a $3,000 profit.
- **The Problem:** The customer hasn't paid the invoice yet. Your bank account is actually down $2,000.

### Ignoring the Trend
One month of P&L is a snapshot; twelve months is a story. Always compare the current month to the previous month and the same month from the previous year. 
- **Seasonal Spikes:** If your revenue jumps every November, you need to save cash in July to cover it.
- **Expense Creep:** Watch for "small" $20/month subscriptions. Over a year, ten of these become a significant line item that eats your net margin.

### Misclassifying COGS as OpEx
If you put your parts costs (COGS) into "General Supplies" (OpEx), your Gross Profit will look artificially high, and your overhead will look bloated. This makes it impossible to tell if your pricing is actually profitable.
