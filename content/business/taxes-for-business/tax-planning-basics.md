## Basic Tax Planning

Tax preparation is looking backward; tax planning is looking forward. Preparation is about telling the IRS what happened. Planning is about deciding what will happen so that you pay the legal minimum in taxes. The difference between the two is the difference between a business that survives and a business that scales.

---

## The Core Pillars of Tax Planning

Professional tax planning focuses on three primary levers: **Timing, Classification, and Structure**.

### 1. Timing (The Calendar Play)
When you record an expense or a sale changes your tax liability for that year. 
- **Accelerating Expenses:** If you know you have a high-profit year, you can buy necessary equipment or supplies in December to increase your deductions for the current year, lowering your taxable income.
- **Deferring Income:** If you are close to the top of a tax bracket on December 31st, you might delay invoicing a client until January 1st. This pushes the income into the next tax year, potentially keeping you in a lower bracket.

### 2. Classification (The Label Play)
How you label a cost determines if it's deductible now or spread over years.
- **Expense vs. Asset:** A $200 tool is an expense (deducted immediately). A $20,000 machine is an asset (depreciated over 5-7 years). 
- **The Section 179 Deduction:** The IRS often allows "bonus depreciation" or Section 179, which lets you deduct the full cost of expensive equipment in the first year. This is a powerful tool for reducing a massive tax bill after a high-growth year.

### 3. Structure (The Entity Play)
How your business is legally organized changes how you are taxed.
- **Sole Proprietorship/LLC:** Simple, but you pay the full 15.3% Self-Employment tax on all profits.
- **S-Corp:** Allows you to split income between a "reasonable salary" (taxed for SE) and "distributions" (not taxed for SE). This can save a high-earning owner thousands of dollars a year in payroll taxes.

## The Tax Planning Cycle

A professional business owner does not "do taxes" once a year. They follow a quarterly cycle:

| Frequency | Action | Goal |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Monthly** | Review P&L and Balance Sheet | Ensure bookkeeping is accurate and identify spending trends. |
| **Quarterly** | Pay Estimated Taxes | Avoid underpayment penalties and track actual profit vs. projections. |
| **Mid-Year** | Tax Projection Meeting | Estimate total year-end profit. Decide if you need to buy equipment or adjust retirement contributions. |
| **Year-End** | Final Strategy Push | Execute last-minute deductions to hit the target tax bracket. |

## The "Tax Bracket" Mindset

Understanding the progressive tax system is key. You are not taxed at one flat rate; you are taxed in "buckets." 

**The Strategy:** The goal is to avoid "bracket creep." If you are $2,000 over the limit of a lower bracket, spending $2,000 on a necessary piece of equipment doesn't just give you a tool—it effectively "costs" you less because it removes that income from the higher tax bucket.

## Common Mistakes

### 1. The "Tax Return" Mindset
Thinking that the goal is to get a big refund check in April. A refund is not a "win"; it is an interest-free loan you gave to the government. The goal of tax planning is to owe as close to zero as possible on April 15th while having maximized your deductions throughout the year.

### 2. Over-Aggressive Avoidance
There is a line between **Tax Avoidance** (legal use of the tax code to minimize liability) and **Tax Evasion** (lying about income or fabricating expenses). Avoidance is a strategy; evasion is a crime. If a "tax guru" tells you to claim your personal dog as a business expense, they are leading you toward an audit and potential fraud charges.

### 3. Ignoring State and Local Taxes
Federal taxes are the big ones, but state and local taxes can be complex. Some states have no income tax, while others have aggressive franchise taxes. Always ensure your planning accounts for the specific laws of the state where your business is registered and where you are performing work.
