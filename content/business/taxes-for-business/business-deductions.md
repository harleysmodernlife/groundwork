## Business Deductions You Can Actually Take

A tax deduction is not a "refund"; it is a reduction of your taxable income. If you earn $50,000 and have $10,000 in valid deductions, the IRS taxes you as if you only earned $40,000. The goal of a professional business owner is to capture every single legitimate expense to lower the taxable base.

---

## The Golden Rule of Deductions

For an expense to be deductible, it must be **Ordinary** and **Necessary**.
- **Ordinary:** An expense that is common and accepted in your trade or business. (e.g., A mechanic buying wrenches is ordinary; a mechanic buying a tuxedo is not).
- **Necessary:** An expense that is helpful and appropriate for your trade or business. It doesn't have to be indispensable, just useful.

---

## Primary Deduction Categories

### 1. Materials and Supplies (COGS)
Anything that is physically part of the finished job. 
- Parts, lumber, fasteners, oil, filters.
- Consumables like sandpaper, cleaning solvents, and welding gas.

### 2. Tools and Equipment
- **Small Tools:** Tools under a certain threshold (usually $2,500 under the de minimis safe harbor) can be deducted in full the year you buy them.
- **Large Equipment:** Expensive machinery (lifts, CNC machines) is usually "depreciated" over several years, meaning you take a portion of the deduction each year.

### 3. Vehicle and Travel
This is the most audited area of small business taxes. You have two choices:
- **Standard Mileage Rate:** You track every mile driven for business and multiply it by the IRS rate (e.g., 67 cents per mile). This is the simplest method and covers gas, insurance, and wear.
- **Actual Expense Method:** You track every penny spent on gas, repairs, tires, and insurance, then multiply that total by the percentage of the vehicle's use that was for business.

### 4. Home Office Deduction
If you use a specific area of your home *exclusively* for business (e.g., a dedicated office or a workshop in the garage), you can deduct a portion of your home expenses.
- **Simplified Method:** A flat rate per square foot (up to 300 sq ft).
- **Actual Method:** A percentage of your mortgage interest, utilities, and home insurance based on the square footage of the office vs. the whole house.

### 5. Marketing and Professional Services
- Website hosting, business cards, and ad spend.
- Software subscriptions (QuickBooks, Adobe, CRM).
- Legal fees for contracts and accounting fees for tax prep.

## The Documentation Standard

If you cannot prove the expense, the IRS will disqualify the deduction during an audit. A "feeling" that you spent money is not proof.

| Level of Proof | Status | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bank Statement** | Weak | Shows money left the account, but not *what* was bought. |
| **Credit Card Record** | Moderate | Better, but still lacks itemization. |
| **Itemized Receipt** | Strong | Shows exactly what was purchased, the date, and the vendor. |
| **Invoice + Payment Proof** | Gold Standard | Professional record of the transaction and the payment. |

## Common Mistakes

### 1. Mixing Personal and Business Expenses
This is called "commingling." If you buy groceries and a box of nails on the same Target receipt, you make your bookkeeping a nightmare and trigger red flags for auditors. **Always use a separate business bank account and credit card.**

### 2. Deducting "Entertainment"
In the past, business owners could deduct fancy dinners and sporting events. Current laws have tightened this. Meals are generally only 50% deductible, and "entertainment" (like tickets to a game) is largely non-deductible, even if you are with a client.

### 3. Forgetting Small Expenses
A $15 subscription or a $20 parking fee seems insignificant, but across a year, these "micro-expenses" can add up to thousands of dollars in missed deductions. Use an app or a dedicated ledger to log everything in real-time.
