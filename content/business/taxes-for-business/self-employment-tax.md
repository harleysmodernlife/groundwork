## Self-Employment Tax Explained

When you work for a company, you only pay half of your Social Security and Medicare taxes. The employer pays the other half. When you are the business owner, you are both the employer and the employee. This means you are responsible for both halves. This is what is known as the Self-Employment (SE) tax.

---

## The Math of SE Tax

Self-employment tax is not a separate income tax; it is a payroll tax. It is calculated based on your net earnings—your profit after business expenses, but before income taxes.

**The Rate:** The current SE tax rate is 15.3%.
- **Social Security:** 12.4% (capped at a specific income limit each year).
- **Medicare:** 2.9% (no cap).

**The Calculation:**
1. You take your net profit for the year.
2. You multiply that by 92.35% (the IRS allows you to exclude a portion of the SE tax from the taxable base).
3. You multiply that result by 15.3%.

**Example:** If your business nets $50,000 in profit:
- $50,000 x 0.9235 = $46,175
- $46,175 x 0.153 = **$7,065 in SE Tax**

This $7,065 is owed *in addition* to your standard federal and state income taxes.

---

## The "Silver Lining": The SE Tax Deduction

Because the employer half of the SE tax is a business expense, the IRS allows you to deduct 50% of your SE tax from your adjusted gross income (AGI) when calculating your income tax. 

Using the example above: If you paid $7,065 in SE tax, you can deduct $3,532.50 from your taxable income. This doesn't eliminate the SE tax, but it lowers the amount of income tax you owe.

---

## SE Tax vs. Income Tax

It is critical to understand that these are two different buckets of money. Many new owners save for their income tax but forget the SE tax, leading to a massive bill in April.

| Tax Type | What it Funds | Based On | When it's Paid |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Income Tax** | Government operations, infrastructure | Taxable Income (after deductions) | Quarterly / Annually |
| **SE Tax** | Social Security & Medicare | Net Business Profit | Quarterly / Annually |

## Common Mistakes

### 1. Confusing Gross with Net
SE tax is not calculated on your total sales (Gross Revenue). It is calculated on your profit (Net Income). If you make $100k in sales but spend $60k on parts and rent, you only pay SE tax on the $40k profit.

### 2. Forgetting the "Double Hit"
New owners often calculate their taxes as a flat percentage (e.g., "I'll just save 20%"). For a self-employed person, 20% is almost always too low. Between SE tax (15.3%) and federal/state income tax (which varies), a safe rule of thumb is to set aside 25-35% of every dollar of profit.

### 3. Thinking an LLC Eliminates SE Tax
A standard single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity" for tax purposes. This means the IRS ignores the LLC and taxes you as a sole proprietor. You still pay the full 15.3% SE tax on all profits. To avoid this, you would need to elect S-Corp status, which allows you to split your income between a "reasonable salary" (taxed for SE) and "distributions" (not taxed for SE). However, S-Corps come with significantly higher administrative costs and payroll requirements.
