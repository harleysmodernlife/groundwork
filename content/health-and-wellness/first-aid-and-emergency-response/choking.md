## Choking: The Heimlich Maneuver

Choking — airway obstruction by a foreign object — kills thousands of people annually, many in situations where a bystander was present. The Heimlich maneuver is a skill that anyone can learn and that can save a life in the time it takes EMS to arrive.

---

## Recognizing Choking

A conscious choking victim typically:
- Clutches their throat with one or both hands (the universal choking sign)
- Cannot speak, cry, or make meaningful sounds
- Cannot cough effectively
- May be able to make high-pitched squeaking sounds (partial obstruction)
- Face may become red, then bluish (cyanosis) as oxygen decreases

**Partial vs. complete obstruction:**
- *Partial:* The person can still cough forcefully or breathe some air. Encourage them to cough — this is often sufficient to dislodge the obstruction. Stand by and don't interfere if coughing is effective.
- *Complete:* No effective air movement, no effective cough. Act immediately.

**Ask:** "Are you choking?" If they can answer verbally, they're not fully obstructed. If they signal yes with their hands and can't speak, act immediately.

---

## Heimlich Maneuver: Conscious Adult or Child (over 1 year)

**Step 1: Call 911 (or have someone else call) while you begin**

**Step 2: Stand behind the person**

Position yourself slightly to the side, with one foot between their feet and one outside their stance for stability.

**Step 3: Position your hands**

Make a fist with one hand. Place the thumb side of your fist against the person's abdomen — above the navel and well below the breastbone (sternum). Grab your fist with your other hand.

**Step 4: Deliver abdominal thrusts**

Give quick, firm, inward-and-upward thrusts. Each thrust should be a distinct, forceful motion — not a squeeze. Think of trying to lift the person off the ground with each thrust.

**Step 5: Repeat**

Give 5 abdominal thrusts, then reassess. If the object hasn't been expelled, repeat the thrusts in sets of 5. Continue until the object is dislodged, the person loses consciousness, or professional help arrives.

**If the object is dislodged:** Even if the person appears fine, seek medical evaluation — abdominal thrusts can cause internal injury.

---

## If the Person Becomes Unconscious

Carefully lower them to the floor, supporting their head. Call 911 if not already done. Begin CPR — chest compressions may help dislodge the object. Before giving rescue breaths, look into the mouth for the object. If you can see it, remove it with a finger sweep — never do blind finger sweeps (you may push the object deeper).

---

## Heimlich on Yourself

If you're alone and choking:

**Self-Heimlich:** Make a fist and deliver abdominal thrusts as described — inward and upward.

**Chair or counter technique:** Position yourself so the edge of a firm surface (the back of a hard chair, a countertop, a railing) is at your abdominal level. Thrust your abdomen forcefully against the edge — this mimics the Heimlich and can be very effective.

---

## Choking in Pregnancy or Obesity

When the abdomen cannot be compressed effectively (pregnancy, obesity), use **chest thrusts** instead:
- Stand behind the person
- Place your fist at the center of the chest (same as for CPR compression location — lower half of the sternum)
- Give firm, backward thrusts

---

## Choking in Infants (under 1 year)

Infants need a different approach. **Never use abdominal thrusts on an infant.**

**Back blows and chest thrusts:**

1. Support the infant face-down on your forearm, head lower than the chest, supporting the head
2. Give 5 firm back blows with the heel of your hand between the shoulder blades
3. Turn the infant face-up, supporting the head
4. Give 5 chest thrusts — two fingers on the center of the chest, just below the nipple line, pressing down about 1.5 inches
5. Alternate 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts until the object is dislodged or the infant loses consciousness

If the infant becomes unconscious, begin infant CPR and call 911.

---

## After Choking

Anyone who required the Heimlich maneuver should see a doctor:
- Abdominal thrusts can injure internal organs
- A fragment of the object may remain
- The episode may reveal an underlying swallowing problem

For infants, any choking episode that required intervention warrants medical evaluation.
