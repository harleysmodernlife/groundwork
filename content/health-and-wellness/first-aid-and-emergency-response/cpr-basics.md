## CPR: The Fundamentals

Cardiac arrest kills within minutes if nothing is done. CPR — cardiopulmonary resuscitation — manually circulates blood and oxygen when the heart has stopped, buying time until a defibrillator is used or advanced care arrives. Bystander CPR can double or triple survival rates.

This lesson teaches the concepts. To actually be prepared to use CPR, take a hands-on certified course (American Heart Association, Red Cross). The physical practice matters — reading about CPR isn't the same as doing compressions on a mannequin.

---

## When to Use CPR

Start CPR when someone is:
- Unresponsive to tapping and shouting
- Not breathing, or only gasping (occasional, irregular gasps — this is not normal breathing)

Cardiac arrest can happen from heart attack, drowning, electrical shock, severe allergic reaction, drug overdose, or any other cause that stops the heart's normal electrical activity.

**If you're unsure:** If someone is unresponsive and you're not sure if they're breathing, start CPR. Doing CPR on someone who didn't need it can cause minor injuries; not doing CPR on someone who needed it can cause death.

## The Sequence: Hands-Only CPR

The American Heart Association endorses "Hands-Only CPR" for untrained bystanders or as the default when you're uncertain — it's simpler and removes hesitation.

**Step 1: Call 911 (or have someone call)**

**Step 2: Position the person**
Place them on their back on a firm, flat surface. On a soft surface (bed, couch), compressions aren't effective — move them to the floor if possible.

**Step 3: Hand placement**
Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest — on the lower half of the breastbone (sternum). Place your other hand on top, interlocking fingers. Keep fingers off the chest — only heel of hand contacts.

**Step 4: Body position**
Position yourself so your shoulders are directly over your hands. Arms straight. You'll use your body weight, not arm strength.

**Step 5: Compress**
Push down hard and fast — at least 2 inches (5 cm) deep. Allow the chest to fully recoil between compressions (don't lean on the chest). Rate: 100-120 compressions per minute.

A helpful tempo reference: the beat of "Stayin' Alive" by the Bee Gees is approximately 100 BPM. Many CPR trainers use it for practice.

**Step 6: Keep going**
Continue until:
- The person shows clear signs of recovery (normal breathing, movement, response)
- An AED is available and ready to use
- Another trained responder takes over
- You are physically unable to continue

**Don't stop to check for pulse repeatedly** — this interrupts circulation unnecessarily and pulse checks are unreliable under stress.

## CPR with Rescue Breaths

Trained rescuers use compressions combined with rescue breaths — the traditional "30:2" method:
- 30 compressions
- 2 rescue breaths (head tilt-chin lift to open airway, seal mouth around mouth, give breath over 1 second — enough to see chest rise)
- Repeat

Rescue breaths are particularly important for:
- Children (more often respiratory arrest than cardiac)
- Drowning victims (oxygen deprivation is primary)
- People who've been in cardiac arrest for more than a few minutes

For adults in sudden cardiac arrest, hands-only CPR is comparably effective for the first several minutes when someone else is calling 911.

---

## CPR in Children and Infants

**Children (ages 1 to puberty):**
- Use one or two hands depending on child's size
- Compress to 2 inches depth
- Give rescue breaths (child CPR typically uses 30:2)

**Infants (under 1 year):**
- Use two fingers on the center of the chest, just below the nipple line
- Compress to 1.5 inches depth
- Give gentle puffs — just enough to see chest rise
- For infants: cover mouth AND nose with your mouth

**For children who drowned or collapsed due to respiratory cause:** Give 2 minutes of CPR before calling 911 if you're alone (respiratory arrest responds faster than cardiac arrest to CPR).

## What CPR Can and Can't Do

CPR circulates some blood — not nearly as effectively as a beating heart, but enough to preserve brain function for a few more minutes. CPR alone rarely restarts a heart in cardiac arrest — it buys time for a defibrillator to deliver the shock that can restart a normal rhythm.

This is why getting an AED quickly matters as much as starting CPR quickly.

The survival chain:
1. Call 911
2. Start CPR
3. Use an AED as soon as available
4. Advanced care from paramedics

Every link in the chain matters. Starting CPR immediately — before EMS arrives — is the link bystanders provide.

## Getting Trained

A 2-hour CPR course provides hands-on practice that makes a genuine difference. Organizations offering certified training:
- American Heart Association (heart.org)
- American Red Cross (redcross.org)
- Many hospitals, community centers, YMCAs, and workplaces

Training is inexpensive, widely available, and teaches skills that last years. The difference between knowing about CPR and having done it on a mannequin is significant in an actual emergency.
