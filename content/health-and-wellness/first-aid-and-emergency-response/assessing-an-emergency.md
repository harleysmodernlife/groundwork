## Assessing an Emergency Scene

In an emergency, the first few seconds determine whether you help or become another victim. Acting without thinking — rushing to someone who's collapsed without checking the scene — can put you in danger and leave two people needing rescue instead of one.

Emergency response has a sequence for a reason. The sequence is: scene safety, then the victim.

---

## Scene Safety First

Before approaching anyone, pause — even briefly — to assess the scene.

**Is the scene safe to enter?**
- Traffic: Is the roadway clear? Is there ongoing hazard?
- Fire or smoke: Is the building safe to enter?
- Electrical hazard: Downed power lines, water and electricity combined?
- Violence or threat: Is the person who hurt the victim still present?
- Chemical exposure: Unusual smells, multiple collapsed people (possible gas leak or chemical agent)
- Structural instability: Collapsed building, damaged floor?

If the scene is not safe, **do not enter.** Call 911. Your job is to get professional help there, not to become another casualty. Heroes who rush into unsafe scenes without training frequently become victims themselves.

**What if the scene becomes unsafe after you enter?** Leave. Get yourself out first, then help from outside.

---

## Personal Protective Equipment

If available, use gloves before touching anyone — ideally non-latex gloves. Blood-borne pathogens (HIV, hepatitis B and C) can theoretically be transmitted through mucous membranes or open wounds. The risk is low but real.

First aid kits should include gloves. In emergencies without them: improvise with bags, extra clothing, or use the palms of your hands rather than fingers for wounds where possible. Don't let the absence of gloves prevent you from providing necessary help in a life-threatening situation — just be aware and take reasonable precautions.

---

## Initial Assessment of the Victim

Once the scene is safe, approach and assess the victim:

**Step 1: Check for responsiveness.**

Tap the person's shoulders firmly and shout: "Are you okay? Can you hear me?"

- If they respond: They're conscious. Assess what's wrong, reassure them, call 911 if needed.
- If they don't respond: They're unresponsive. Continue to next step.

**Step 2: Call for help.**

If unresponsive, call 911 immediately (or direct someone specifically: "You — call 911 right now"). In a crowd, pointing directly at an individual works better than general calls for help — bystander effect means people assume others will act.

If there are other bystanders:
- Assign one person to call 911
- Assign another to retrieve an AED if one is available
- You focus on assessment and care

**Step 3: Check for breathing.**

Look at the chest for rise and fall. Listen and feel for breath. Do this for no more than 10 seconds.

- **Normal breathing:** Continue assessment
- **No breathing or only gasping:** This is a cardiac arrest. Begin CPR immediately.

---

## Primary Survey: The ABCs

Once you've established responsiveness and breathing status, work through the primary survey:

**Airway:** Is the airway open? Unconscious people often have the tongue fall back and block the airway. Tilt the head back and lift the chin (head-tilt chin-lift) to open the airway, unless you suspect a neck or spine injury (then use jaw thrust only).

**Breathing:** Is the person breathing adequately? Look for chest rise, listen for breath sounds, note the rate and quality.

**Circulation:** Is there major bleeding? Check quickly for significant external wounds. Control life-threatening bleeding (see the bleeding lesson).

**D for Disability (often added as D):** Brief neurological check — are they conscious? Can they follow commands? Pupils equal?

**E for Exposure:** Expose injuries enough to see what you're dealing with — don't leave major wounds hidden under clothing.

---

## Calling 911: What to Say

When you call:
1. State your location — be as specific as possible (address, cross streets, landmarks)
2. What happened — "An adult man collapsed and is unconscious, I don't see breathing"
3. How many people are injured
4. Your name and phone number

Stay on the line. The dispatcher will tell you what to do next and can guide you through CPR or other interventions.

Do not hang up until told to. The dispatcher coordinates first responders and needs ongoing information.

---

## Communicate and Document

After stabilizing an immediate threat:
- Stay with the victim and monitor for changes
- Gather any medication bottles, relevant medical information (medical ID bracelet, allergy information)
- Note time of collapse and any changes in status
- Brief arriving paramedics: "He collapsed about 5 minutes ago, unconscious, we've been doing CPR for 3 minutes"

The goal in any emergency assessment: do no additional harm, get professional help there, and provide what immediate aid you safely can while waiting.
