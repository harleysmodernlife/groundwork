## Controlling Bleeding and Wound Care

Severe bleeding can kill in minutes. The ability to control significant bleeding — with your hands if nothing else is available — is one of the highest-value first aid skills. Wound care for less serious injuries prevents infection and supports healing.

---

## Recognizing Life-Threatening Bleeding

Not all bleeding is equal. Signs of severe, potentially life-threatening bleeding:
- Blood is spurting (arterial bleeding — bright red, pulsating)
- Blood is flowing continuously and not slowing
- The wound is large or deep
- A limb or body part has been severed
- Clothing is soaked quickly
- The person shows signs of shock: pale skin, rapid weak pulse, confusion, loss of consciousness

**Life-threatening bleeding is a priority equal to airway and breathing.** Control it before addressing other injuries.

---

## Direct Pressure: The Primary Method

**Step 1: Protect yourself**
Use gloves if available. If not, improvise (a bag, extra fabric, or accept the risk for a life-threatening bleed).

**Step 2: Apply direct pressure**
Place a clean cloth, gauze, or any available fabric directly on the wound. Press hard and hold.

If you don't have anything: use your hands directly.

**Step 3: Press harder than you think you need to**
The most common mistake is not pressing hard enough. You need to compress the bleeding vessel. For a deep wound, this may require pressing significantly harder than feels comfortable.

**Step 4: Don't remove the bandage/cloth**
If it soaks through, add more material on top without removing what's there. Removing the cloth disturbs any clot that's forming.

**Step 5: Maintain pressure**
At least 10 minutes of continuous, firm pressure. Clotting takes time. Checking every 2 minutes delays clotting. Set a mental timer and press without interruption.

**Step 6: Secure the dressing**
Once bleeding slows, secure the pressure dressing with a bandage or tape. Seek medical care.

---

## Tourniquets

For severe limb bleeding that direct pressure can't control — or when hands are needed elsewhere — a tourniquet is appropriate. The previous recommendation against tourniquet use has been reversed: studies from military trauma medicine show tourniquets save lives when used appropriately.

**Commercial tourniquet (CAT, SOFTT-W):** If available and you're trained, use it. Apply 2-3 inches above the wound, tighten until bleeding stops, and note the time.

**Improvised tourniquet:**
- Use a strip of material at least 2 inches wide (cloth, belt)
- Apply 2-3 inches above the wound on the limb
- Tighten using a stick or pen twisted through the material (windlass) until bleeding stops
- Secure the windlass so it can't unwind
- Note the time applied

Tourniquets are painful and will cause tissue damage if left on too long — but uncontrolled limb bleeding is a faster killer. Professional providers will remove or reassess the tourniquet. Your job is to stop the bleeding.

---

## Wound Packing

For deep wounds (especially gunshot wounds, stab wounds, or wounds in areas where a tourniquet can't be applied — neck, groin, armpit):

Pack the wound with gauze (or any cloth if gauze isn't available). Push the material firmly into the wound until it's packed. Then apply direct pressure over the packed wound.

This is uncomfortable and the person may resist — for life-threatening bleeding, the priority is stopping the bleed.

---

## Minor Wound Care

For cuts, lacerations, and scrapes that aren't life-threatening:

**Step 1: Control bleeding with direct pressure**

**Step 2: Clean the wound**
Once bleeding is controlled, rinse with clean water — this is the most important step for infection prevention. Remove visible debris. You don't need antibacterial soap in the wound — water is sufficient.

**Step 3: Apply antibiotic ointment (optional)**
Not mandatory, but a thin layer of antibiotic ointment (bacitracin, Neosporin) may reduce infection risk in minor wounds.

**Step 4: Cover with a bandage**
Keep the wound covered while it heals. Change the bandage daily or when it's wet or dirty.

**Step 5: Watch for infection signs:**
- Increasing redness spreading from the wound
- Increasing warmth
- Swelling
- Pus or discharge
- Red streaks spreading from the wound (sign of serious spreading infection)
- Fever

Signs of infection warrant medical care.

---

## When to Get Stitches

A wound likely needs medical care for closure (stitches, staples, or skin adhesive) if it:
- Won't stop bleeding after 10-20 minutes of pressure
- Gapes open and won't stay closed
- Is longer than about 1/2 inch (1.5 cm)
- Is on the face, over a joint, or involves a hand or foot
- Is a bite wound (puncture wounds from animals have high infection rates)
- Involves the eyelid, ear, genitals, or scalp (these have specific closure considerations)

Closure works best within 6-12 hours of injury. After that, infection risk from closing increases.

**Tetanus:** If the wound is deep, contaminated, or you can't confirm your tetanus vaccination is current, get a tetanus shot. Current vaccination means a booster within 5-10 years.
