## Calling 911: What to Say and Do

Calling 911 is the single most important action in most medical emergencies. Everything else you do — CPR, controlling bleeding, keeping someone calm — is buying time until professional help arrives. Knowing how to call effectively, what information to provide, and what to do when you're in unfamiliar territory makes that call more effective.

---

## When to Call 911

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) for:

**Life-threatening emergencies:**
- Unconsciousness or unresponsiveness
- Absent or abnormal breathing
- Chest pain or pressure
- Signs of stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty)
- Severe allergic reaction (throat swelling, difficulty breathing, anaphylaxis)
- Severe bleeding that isn't controlled
- Suspected drug overdose with unconsciousness or altered breathing
- Suspected poisoning
- Major trauma (serious accidents, falls from height, significant impacts)
- Seizure in someone not known to have epilepsy, or a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes
- Severe burns
- Near-drowning
- Suspected spinal injury

**When in doubt, call.** Emergency dispatchers can help you decide whether you need emergency response. It's not an abuse of the system to call when you're unsure.

---

## What Happens When You Call

Emergency calls are answered by dispatchers (also called telecommunicators) who are trained to gather information, coordinate response, and guide callers through immediate care.

**The dispatcher will ask:**
1. What is the nature of your emergency?
2. What is your location?
3. What is your phone number?

After these basics, they'll ask for more detail depending on the situation.

**Your most important job:** Stay calm enough to communicate clearly. The dispatcher's training is designed to work with upset callers — you don't need to be perfectly composed, just coherent.

---

## What to Tell Them

**Location first.** This is most critical — responders can't help if they can't find you.

- Street address with city
- Cross streets if you don't have an exact address
- Apartment number, floor, or building name
- If you're on a highway: direction of travel, mile marker, nearest exit
- If you're in a rural area without clear landmarks: describe what you can see, what road you turned off from

**Location in unfamiliar places:** Look for a sign, building number, or ask someone nearby. Most public buildings have their address posted near entrances or exits. If you're truly lost, cell phones can sometimes give coordinates — dispatchers can use GPS data.

**Describe what happened:**
- "A man collapsed and isn't breathing"
- "A woman is having a seizure and it won't stop"
- "There's a car accident with multiple injured people"
- "I found someone unconscious — I don't know what happened"

Be concise but complete. You'll be asked follow-up questions.

**Number of victims.** This affects how many units respond.

---

## Stay on the Line

Do not hang up after giving information. The dispatcher may:
- Guide you through CPR
- Talk you through controlling bleeding
- Help you monitor the victim's breathing
- Provide instructions until responders arrive
- Update you on estimated arrival time
- Relay information to responders ahead of their arrival

If you need to put the phone down to provide care, tell the dispatcher: "I need to start CPR — I'm leaving the phone on." Leave it on.

---

## When You're the Responder

If you're providing care while someone else calls:
- Give that person specific instructions: "Call 911. Tell them [address]. Say a man collapsed and isn't breathing."
- If you're alone with the victim and they're not breathing: Call 911 first (unless it's a child who drowned or has a respiratory emergency — then do 2 minutes of CPR first, then call if you can't do both simultaneously). Modern 911 systems can also use hands-free options.

**If you're a bystander in a crowd:** Don't assume someone else called. Ask directly: "Has someone called 911?" If you get uncertain answers, call yourself or assign someone specifically.

---

## After the Call

- Keep monitoring the victim and report any changes when responders arrive
- If you were doing CPR: don't stop until responders take over or the person clearly recovers
- Clear a path for responders if you can — move cars, open doors, meet them at the entrance of a building
- Brief arriving responders immediately: what happened, for how long, what you've done

**Documentation:** After the emergency, write down what happened as soon as possible — timeline, what you observed, what you did. This information may be needed for medical treatment decisions.
