## Burns, Fractures, and Sprains

Three of the most common injuries requiring first aid. None of these typically require the immediate urgency of cardiac arrest or severe bleeding, but knowing how to respond appropriately reduces pain, prevents further damage, and helps the person get appropriate care.

---

## Burns

**Classifying burns:**

*Superficial (first-degree):* Affects only the outer layer of skin (epidermis). Red, dry, painful. Sunburns are usually first-degree. Heal in 3-5 days without scarring.

*Partial thickness (second-degree):* Affects epidermis and part of the dermis. Red, wet, blistered, intensely painful. Heal in 2-3 weeks; may scar. Large or deep partial-thickness burns need medical care.

*Full thickness (third-degree):* Destroys all skin layers and underlying tissue. May appear white, brown, or black. Surprisingly, may not be painful — nerve endings are destroyed. Always requires medical care.

**First aid for burns:**

1. **Stop the burning process.** Remove from the heat source. For chemical burns, brush off dry chemicals, then flush with large amounts of water for 20+ minutes.

2. **Cool the burn.** Cool with cool (not cold, not ice) running water for 10-20 minutes. This reduces tissue damage — but works only within the first 30 minutes of the burn. Ice causes additional tissue damage and should not be used.

3. **Do not:**
   - Apply butter, toothpaste, oil, or other home remedies — these trap heat and increase infection risk
   - Break blisters — they protect the underlying tissue
   - Remove clothing stuck to the burn — cut around it

4. **Cover loosely.** With a clean non-fluffy material (not cotton balls, which stick). A plastic wrap or a clean plastic bag works well for transport.

**Seek medical care for:**
- Burns covering large areas or involving the face, hands, feet, genitals, or joints
- All full-thickness burns
- Burns from electricity or chemicals
- Burns with signs of inhalation injury (singed eyebrows/nasal hair, hoarse voice, breathing difficulty)
- Any serious burn in children or elderly

## Fractures (Broken Bones)

**Recognizing a fracture:**
- Significant pain, especially at one specific spot
- Swelling and bruising
- Deformity or abnormal shape
- Inability to move the limb normally
- A sound (crack, snap) at the time of injury

You often cannot confirm a fracture without an X-ray. Treat for a fracture if you're unsure.

**Open vs. closed fracture:**
- *Closed:* Bone broken, skin intact
- *Open (compound):* Bone breaks through skin. High infection risk. Do not push bone back in. Cover the wound with a clean dressing and seek emergency care immediately.

**First aid:**

1. **Don't move a suspected fracture of the spine, pelvis, or femur without training.** These can cause serious additional injury if moved improperly. Keep the person still and wait for EMS.

2. **For other suspected fractures:** Immobilize the injury in the position you find it. Don't try to straighten or realign the bone. Splint it — improvise with boards, rolled-up magazines, or other rigid material padded for comfort — to prevent movement.

3. **Ice and elevation:** Reduce swelling with ice (wrapped to prevent skin damage) and elevation if possible.

4. **Treat for shock if needed:** Pain and blood loss from fractures (especially long bones — the femur can bleed several liters internally) can cause shock. Keep the person lying down, covered, and calm.

**All fractures need medical evaluation.** The timing depends on severity — open fractures are emergencies; a possible fracture of a small bone can be evaluated at urgent care.

## Sprains

A sprain is a stretched or torn ligament (the tissue connecting bone to bone). The most common is the ankle sprain.

**Signs of a sprain:**
- Pain around a joint
- Swelling
- Bruising (may develop gradually)
- Limited range of motion
- The joint still supports some weight (unlike a fracture, usually)

**RICE protocol:**

*Rest:* Avoid putting weight on or using the injured area. Use crutches if needed for a significant ankle sprain.

*Ice:* Apply ice (wrapped in cloth) for 20 minutes every 2-3 hours for the first 48 hours. Reduces swelling and pain.

*Compression:* Wrap the joint with an elastic bandage (ACE wrap) — firm but not tight enough to cause numbness or tingling.

*Elevation:* Raise the injured area above heart level when possible to reduce swelling.

**When to seek care:**
- If you can't bear weight at all (may be a fracture)
- Significant deformity
- Numbness or tingling
- Severe pain or swelling
- No improvement after 2-3 days
- Previous repeated injuries to the same joint (structural damage may need evaluation)

Mild sprains improve significantly within days. Severe sprains (complete ligament tears) may require immobilization, physical therapy, or sometimes surgery.

## Head Injuries

Any significant impact to the head warrants caution. After a head injury, signs of serious brain injury include:
- Loss of consciousness (even briefly)
- Confusion or disorientation
- Repeated vomiting
- Worsening headache
- Seizures
- Unequal pupils
- Slurred speech
- Inability to recognize people or places
- Clear fluid from nose or ears
- Weakness or numbness in limbs

**If any of these are present:** Call 911 or go to an emergency department.

For concussion (a mild traumatic brain injury): rest, avoid screens, avoid physical activity, and follow up with a healthcare provider. Do not return to sports or high-risk activity until medically cleared.
