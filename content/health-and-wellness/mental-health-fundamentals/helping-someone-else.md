## How to Help Someone Else Who Is Struggling

Watching someone you care about struggle with mental health is painful. The instinct is to fix it — offer solutions, provide reassurance, explain why things will be okay. These instincts are understandable and usually unhelpful. Knowing how to actually help is a skill that can make a real difference.

---

## What Helps (and What Doesn't)

**What helps:**

*Listening without agenda.* The most valuable thing you can offer is presence and attention without rushing to fix anything. "Tell me what's been going on" — and then actually listen. Don't formulate your response while they're still talking.

*Acknowledging and validating.* "That sounds really hard." "I can see why you'd feel that way." "That makes sense given everything you're dealing with." Validation doesn't mean agreeing or condoning — it means acknowledging that the person's emotional experience makes sense.

*Asking what they need.* "What would be most helpful right now? Do you want me to listen, help you think through something, or just be here with you?" People often know what they need — they're not always asked.

*Practical help.* When someone is struggling, basic functioning (meals, chores, logistics) can be overwhelming. Offering specific practical help — "I'm going to bring you dinner on Thursday" rather than "let me know if you need anything" — is far more effective. "Let me know if you need anything" puts the burden on the person who's struggling to ask, which is hard.

*Checking in consistently.* Not just once after something bad happens. Regular, low-pressure check-ins over time — a text, a brief call — communicate that you're still there and still care.

**What doesn't help:**

*"Have you tried..." / "You should..." / "Just..."* Solutions, especially ones the person has probably already considered or tried. This communicates that you think the problem is simple and they haven't thought of the obvious answer.

*Silver linings.* "At least..." "Things could be worse." "You have so much to be grateful for." This dismisses the person's actual experience and suggests they shouldn't feel how they feel.

*"I know how you feel."* Usually followed by a pivot to your own experience. You probably don't know how they feel — and the person isn't helped by the conversation becoming about you.

*Minimizing.* "You'll be fine." "It's not that bad." This can feel invalidating, like their suffering doesn't warrant concern.

*Excessive reassurance.* For someone with anxiety, repetitive reassurance can actually maintain anxiety — it provides temporary relief but prevents the person from developing their own coping.

## Encouraging Professional Help

If someone's struggles seem significant and persistent, they may benefit from professional support. How you raise this matters.

**Frame it as care, not criticism.** "I've noticed you've been having a hard time and I care about you. Have you thought about talking to someone?" is different from "You really need therapy."

**Normalize it.** "A lot of people find talking to a therapist helpful, even without a crisis. It helped me / a friend / [someone they respect]."

**Offer to help with logistics.** Sometimes the barrier is practical: "I could help you look up therapists who take your insurance" or "I'd be happy to come with you to a first appointment."

**Don't push too hard.** You can share your concern, offer support, and let the person make their own decision. Applying pressure can cause them to dig in or feel judged.

## Talking About Suicide

If you're concerned someone is thinking about suicide, ask directly. Research consistently shows that asking about suicide does not plant the idea or increase risk — it opens a conversation that needed to happen.

"Are you thinking about suicide?" or "Are you having thoughts of hurting yourself?" — clear, direct, non-dramatic.

If they say yes:
- Take it seriously. Don't minimize.
- Stay with them or make sure they're not alone.
- Help them access crisis support: 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988). Crisis Text Line (text HOME to 741741).
- If they're in immediate danger, call 911 or take them to an emergency department.
- Don't promise to keep suicidal ideation a secret.

**Warning signs of increased suicide risk:**
- Talking about wanting to die or having no reason to live
- Giving away possessions
- Saying goodbye in unusual ways
- Sudden calmness after a period of depression (sometimes indicates a decision has been made)
- Increased alcohol or substance use
- Withdrawal from social connections

If you're unsure, ask. If you're worried, err on the side of taking it seriously.

## Taking Care of Yourself

Supporting someone who's struggling is emotionally draining. You can't pour from an empty cup.

- Your own wellbeing matters
- You're not responsible for fixing another person's mental health — that's not in your power
- Setting limits on what you can provide isn't abandonment; it's sustainability
- Supporting someone through a mental health crisis can be traumatic — consider your own support
- You can care deeply about someone and still need to maintain your own boundaries

You can be a meaningful source of support for someone without being their therapist or their only support. Encouraging them to build a network of support — including professional support — helps both of you.
