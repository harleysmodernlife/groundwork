## Getting Professional Help: Types of Therapy

Deciding to get professional help for mental health is the right decision — but navigating the options is genuinely confusing. Therapist, psychologist, psychiatrist, counselor — what's the difference? CBT, DBT, EMDR, psychodynamic — what do these mean and which works? Here's a practical guide.

---

## Types of Mental Health Professionals

**Psychiatrist:** A medical doctor (MD or DO) who specializes in mental health. Psychiatrists can prescribe medication. Many focus primarily on medication management — a shorter appointment model where they assess symptoms and adjust prescriptions. Some also provide therapy.

**Psychologist:** A doctoral-level clinician (PhD or PsyD) who specializes in assessment and psychotherapy. Psychologists are trained in psychological testing and can diagnose. In most states they cannot prescribe medication (a few states have prescribing privileges for specially trained psychologists).

**Licensed Clinical Social Worker (LCSW):** A master's-level clinician who provides therapy. Trained to address mental health within social and environmental context. Cannot prescribe medication.

**Licensed Professional Counselor (LPC) / Licensed Mental Health Counselor (LMHC):** Master's-level therapists. The title varies by state. Provide individual, group, or family therapy. Cannot prescribe medication.

**Marriage and Family Therapist (MFT):** Specialized in relationship and family systems. Can work with individuals, couples, and families.

For therapy, the specific license often matters less than the therapist's training, approach, and fit with you. For medication, you need a prescriber — psychiatrist, or sometimes a primary care physician for common conditions.

## How to Find One

**Through insurance:** Your insurance company has an online provider directory. Search for in-network therapists or psychiatrists in your area. Call your insurance member services number to understand your mental health benefits (copays, session limits, whether you need a referral).

**Psychology Today directory:** psychologytoday.com/us/therapists allows filtering by location, insurance, specialty, and approach. Therapist profiles include background and approach descriptions.

**Open Path Collective:** Low-cost therapy for people without insurance or adequate coverage. Sliding scale rates.

**Community mental health centers:** Every county typically has a community mental health center that provides services on a sliding fee scale based on income. Often the option for people without insurance or with significant financial constraints.

**Primary care physician:** Your PCP can often manage common mental health conditions (depression, anxiety) with medication, provide referrals, and screen for conditions that have physical causes.

**Crisis resources:** If you're in crisis now — 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988 in the U.S.). Crisis Text Line: text HOME to 741741. Emergency departments for immediate safety.

## Types of Therapy

**Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT):** The most researched psychological therapy. Focuses on the relationship between thoughts, emotions, and behaviors — and how to change unhelpful patterns. Present-focused and skill-based. Usually 12-20 sessions. Strong evidence for anxiety, depression, OCD, PTSD, eating disorders, and many other conditions.

**Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT):** Originally developed for borderline personality disorder, now used broadly for emotional dysregulation. Skills-based: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness.

**EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing):** Used primarily for trauma and PTSD. Involves processing traumatic memories while engaging in bilateral stimulation (eye movements, taps, sounds). Strong evidence base.

**Exposure Therapy:** Core component of anxiety treatment. Involves gradual, systematic exposure to feared situations or thoughts to reduce anxiety response. Prevents the avoidance that maintains anxiety disorders.

**Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT):** Focuses on accepting difficult thoughts and feelings rather than fighting them, and committing to behavior aligned with personal values. Less focused on changing thought content than CBT.

**Psychodynamic therapy:** Explores unconscious patterns, past experiences, and relationship dynamics as they affect current functioning. Less structured than CBT. Evidence is more mixed but meaningful.

**Couples and family therapy:** Addresses relationship patterns. Various modalities — the most evidence-based for couples is Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT) and the Gottman Method.

## What Makes Therapy Work

Research consistently shows that **therapeutic alliance** — the quality of the relationship between therapist and client — is one of the strongest predictors of therapy outcome, often more predictive than the specific modality.

This means:

If you don't feel reasonably comfortable with a therapist after 2-3 sessions, it's okay to try a different therapist. It's not therapy that isn't working — it may be that particular match.

But also: therapy often involves discussing difficult things. Mild discomfort is different from a bad fit. Talking about trauma, confronting avoidance, and examining patterns is hard by nature.

---

## What to Expect

The first session is usually an assessment — the therapist gathers information about what brings you in, your history, and your goals. They explain their approach.

Therapy isn't quick for most conditions. 10-20 sessions is common for targeted work on a specific issue. Longer-term therapy may be appropriate for complex presentations.

Progress isn't linear. Things sometimes get harder before they get easier — especially when addressing avoided material. This is normal, not a sign therapy isn't working.

Medication and therapy together often produce better outcomes than either alone for conditions like depression and anxiety disorders.
