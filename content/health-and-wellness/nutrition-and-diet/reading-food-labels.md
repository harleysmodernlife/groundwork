## Reading Food Labels

Food labels exist so consumers can make informed choices. But they're also designed by companies who want their products to look as good as possible. Learning to read labels accurately — ignoring the marketing and looking at the actual data — is a practical skill that directly affects what you eat.

---

## The Nutrition Facts Panel

This is the regulated, standardized section — the numbers. Unlike front-of-package marketing, these are governed by FDA rules.

**Serving size:** The first thing to check. Everything on the label is per serving. A bag of chips with 3 servings means all the calories, fat, and sodium multiply by 3 if you eat the bag. Serving sizes are often smaller than what people actually eat. "2/3 cup" of cereal looks like less than most people pour.

**Calories:** Total energy per serving from all sources. Multiply by servings eaten for actual intake.

**% Daily Value (DV):** The percentages show how much of the recommended daily amount one serving provides, based on a 2,000 calorie diet. Quick interpretation:
- 5% DV or less = low
- 20% DV or more = high

Use %DV to quickly compare products and identify high or low amounts of specific nutrients.

**Nutrients to limit:**
- *Saturated fat:* Associated with increased LDL cholesterol. Dietary guidelines recommend keeping this low.
- *Sodium:* The average American consumes far more than the recommended 2,300 mg/day. High sodium is associated with high blood pressure.
- *Added sugars:* Added during processing (versus sugars naturally present in fruit or dairy). The label now shows added sugars separately. Recommendations: less than 10% of total calories (about 50g/day on a 2,000-calorie diet). The less the better.
- *Trans fat:* Mostly eliminated from food supply. Aim for 0.

**Nutrients to get enough of:**
- *Dietary fiber:* Recommended 25-38g/day; most people get far less.
- *Vitamin D, Calcium, Iron, Potassium:* Commonly underconsumed. These are shown because deficiency is a public health concern.

## The Ingredient List

Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight — the first ingredient is present in the largest amount.

**What to look for:**
- If sugar (or any of its aliases) appears in the first few ingredients, the product is primarily sugar
- Long lists of unrecognizable chemical names aren't necessarily dangerous — many are preservatives, emulsifiers, or colorings that are safe — but they indicate a highly processed product
- "Whole grain" should be the first or second ingredient in products claiming to be whole grain; "enriched flour" means refined flour with some nutrients added back

**Sugar aliases:** Sugar appears under many names — sucrose, high-fructose corn syrup, glucose, maltose, dextrose, cane juice, agave nectar, honey, maple syrup, molasses, barley malt. They all function similarly in the body.

**Healthy-sounding oils:** "Vegetable oil" is usually soybean or corn oil. "Palm oil" and "palm kernel oil" are highly saturated plant oils. "Partially hydrogenated oil" means trans fat — though now largely prohibited.

## Front-of-Package Claims: Marketing vs. Reality

**"Natural":** Not regulated by the FDA in any meaningful way. "Natural" can appear on almost anything.

**"Organic":** Regulated. Means the product was produced without synthetic pesticides, artificial fertilizers, GMOs, and certain other practices. Organic does not necessarily mean healthy — organic cookies are still cookies.

**"Whole grain":** A product must contain some whole grain to make this claim, but if "whole grain" is the third ingredient and refined flour is the first, it's mostly not whole grain.

**"Low fat":** Contains 3g or less fat per serving. Often replaced fat with sugar to maintain palatability. "Low fat" does not mean low calorie or healthy.

**"Sugar-free":** No added sugars, but may contain sugar alcohols (erythritol, xylitol, sorbitol) or artificial sweeteners. Sugar alcohols have fewer calories than sugar but can cause GI distress in large amounts.

**"Multi-grain":** Contains multiple grains — but they may all be refined. Not the same as "whole grain."

**"Heart healthy" / "good source of":** Regulated claims with specific criteria, though the criteria don't always align with what evidence shows is beneficial.

## A Quick Label-Reading Approach

1. Check the serving size first
2. Look at calories per serving and number of servings you'll eat
3. Check added sugars and sodium — two consistently overconsumed nutrients
4. Check fiber and protein — two consistently underconsumed nutrients
5. Scan the ingredient list — shorter and more recognizable is generally better
6. Ignore front-of-package health claims and compare labels directly

The goal isn't to optimize every label — it's to have a realistic picture of what's in what you're eating.
