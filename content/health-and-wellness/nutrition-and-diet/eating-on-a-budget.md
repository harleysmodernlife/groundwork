## Eating Well on a Tight Budget

The idea that eating healthy is expensive is partly true and mostly a myth. Processed food companies spend significant money marketing the idea that convenience food is affordable — it's good for their business. But many of the healthiest foods are also among the cheapest.

What costs money is specialty health food, organic products, and prepared food marketed as nutritious. Basic whole foods are inexpensive.

---

## The Cheapest Healthy Foods

**Dry beans and lentils:** Among the cheapest foods per serving on earth. Also among the most nutritious — high in protein, fiber, iron, and folate. A pound of dry lentils costs around $1-2 and makes 6+ servings. Dry beans take some planning (soaking, longer cooking) but canned beans are only slightly more expensive and extremely convenient.

**Frozen vegetables:** Nutritionally equivalent to fresh (often frozen at peak ripeness), much cheaper, no waste. Frozen spinach, broccoli, peas, mixed vegetables, edamame. These should be a budget staple.

**Eggs:** One of the cheapest complete protein sources. Versatile and nutrient-dense.

**Oats:** Rolled or steel-cut oats are cheap, filling, high in fiber and protein. A large container of plain oats costs a few dollars and provides weeks of breakfasts.

**Canned fish:** Canned sardines, tuna, and salmon are cheap, protein-rich, and high in omega-3 fatty acids. Often overlooked.

**Cabbage, carrots, onions, and potatoes:** Among the cheapest fresh vegetables. Nutritionally solid and store well.

**Bananas:** One of the cheapest fruits per serving. High in potassium and fiber.

**Brown rice and whole grains in bulk:** Brown rice, barley, whole wheat pasta. Cheap per serving. Slightly more nutritious than refined versions.

**Peanut butter:** Cheap protein and healthy fat. The main ingredient should be peanuts. Avoid varieties with added sugar and hydrogenated oil.

---

## Shopping Strategies

**Buy in bulk where it matters.** Dry goods (beans, lentils, rice, oats) store indefinitely and are significantly cheaper in larger quantities. Canned goods in bulk are also economical.

**Frozen over fresh for most vegetables.** Cheaper, less waste, nutritionally equivalent. Fresh vegetables are worth buying when you'll use them quickly or for specific dishes where texture matters.

**Generic and store brands.** In most cases, the nutritional content is identical to name brands. The difference is marketing cost.

**Shop seasonally for produce.** In-season vegetables and fruit are cheaper and better quality. Out-of-season fresh produce is expensive and often disappointing.

**Don't buy pre-cut, pre-washed, and individually portioned.** You're paying for labor. A head of broccoli is much cheaper per serving than pre-cut florets in a bag.

**Make a list and buy what you'll use.** Wasted food is wasted money. Plan meals before shopping to buy what you actually need.

**Limit beverages.** Soda, juice, specialty coffee, energy drinks, and alcohol cost significant money and add calories without nutrition. Water is free.

## Meal Planning on a Budget

**Beans and rice is a complete protein** — together they provide all essential amino acids. This combination has fed populations throughout history at almost no cost.

**Eggs as the protein anchor.** A dozen eggs can provide a week of breakfasts or be incorporated into multiple dinners. Scrambled eggs, omelets, frittatas, shakshuka, egg fried rice.

**Soups and stews stretch ingredients.** A cheap soup from canned tomatoes, canned beans, onion, garlic, and whatever vegetable you have is filling, nutritious, and costs almost nothing per serving. Makes multiple servings from one batch.

**Cook in bulk.** Make a pot of rice, a batch of beans, a batch of roasted vegetables on the weekend. Combine them in different ways throughout the week. Reduces time and saves money compared to cooking from scratch every night.

**Breakfast for dinner.** Eggs and oats are cheap. A dinner of eggs, a vegetable, and toast costs very little and is nutritionally solid.

## SNAP and Food Assistance

If your income qualifies, SNAP (Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) can provide significant food assistance. Apply through your state's SNAP office or benefits.gov. Many people who qualify don't apply.

Local food banks, community fridges, and church food pantries are also resources when needed — and there's no shame in using them.

---

## The Honest Bottom Line

You can eat a nutritious diet of beans, lentils, eggs, frozen vegetables, oats, canned fish, and seasonal produce for very little money. It takes planning and cooking. The barrier to eating well on a limited budget is usually time and cooking knowledge more than money — which is one reason learning to cook basic meals is valuable.

What truly costs money is organic produce, specialty items, prepared health foods, and restaurant meals marketed as healthy. These are optional.
