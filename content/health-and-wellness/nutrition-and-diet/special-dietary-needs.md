## Special Dietary Considerations

Most nutrition advice applies broadly, but some people have conditions, life stages, or choices that change what they need. This lesson covers the most common situations where general dietary advice needs to be adjusted.

---

## Vegetarian and Vegan Diets

**Well-planned plant-based diets are nutritionally adequate** and associated with health benefits — lower rates of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers. The key word is "well-planned."

**Nutrients requiring attention on plant-based diets:**

*Vitamin B12:* Found almost exclusively in animal products. Vegans must supplement or consume B12-fortified foods (plant milks, nutritional yeast, fortified cereals). This is non-negotiable — deficiency causes irreversible nerve damage. Vegetarians who eat dairy and eggs get some B12 but should monitor levels.

*Iron:* Plant foods contain non-heme iron, which is less bioavailable than heme iron from meat. Eating iron-rich plant foods (legumes, spinach, fortified cereals) with vitamin C improves absorption. Vegans typically need to eat more iron-containing foods than meat-eaters.

*Omega-3 fatty acids:* Fatty fish provides EPA and DHA — the active forms. Plant foods (flaxseed, walnuts, chia seeds) provide ALA, which the body converts to EPA/DHA inefficiently. Algae-based omega-3 supplements provide EPA and DHA directly and are a good option for vegans.

*Zinc:* Less bioavailable from plant foods. Legumes and seeds are good sources; soaking and fermenting improves bioavailability.

*Calcium:* Without dairy, must come from fortified plant milks, tofu made with calcium sulfate, leafy greens (bok choy, kale, chard), and almonds.

*Vitamin D:* Not unique to vegans — most people don't get enough. Sunlight or supplementation.

*Protein:* Obtainable from plants, but requires eating sufficient total protein and including varied sources to cover all essential amino acids.

## Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Nutrient needs increase significantly during pregnancy and lactation.

**Folate/folic acid:** Critical before and during early pregnancy to prevent neural tube defects. Recommended 400-800 mcg/day starting before conception. Most prenatal vitamins provide this.

**Iron:** Needs increase significantly during pregnancy. Prenatal vitamins include iron. Iron deficiency anemia is common in pregnancy and can affect fetal development.

**Calcium and vitamin D:** Support fetal bone development. Continue into breastfeeding.

**DHA (omega-3):** Important for fetal brain and eye development. Found in fatty fish (limit to 2-3 servings/week due to mercury) and algae-based supplements.

**Iodine:** Required for thyroid hormone production; fetal brain development depends on adequate maternal iodine.

**Foods to avoid during pregnancy:** Raw or undercooked meat and fish (listeria, salmonella risk), high-mercury fish (shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish), unpasteurized dairy, raw sprouts. Limit caffeine to under 200mg/day.

Prenatal vitamins fill gaps but don't replace a nutritious diet.

## Infants and Children

**First 6 months:** Breast milk or formula exclusively. Breast milk is nutritionally optimal and provides immune benefits; formula is a safe alternative when breastfeeding isn't possible.

**6-12 months:** Solid foods introduced gradually (single-ingredient, soft foods first). Iron-rich foods are particularly important. Introduce common allergens (peanut butter, eggs) early — evidence now supports early introduction to prevent allergy development, not delay it.

**Children:** Need adequate protein, calcium, and vitamin D for growth. Whole milk until age 2; then 2% or 1%. Variety of foods from all groups. Avoid juice — whole fruit is far preferable.

## Older Adults

Nutritional needs shift with age:

**Protein needs increase.** Muscle loss (sarcopenia) accelerates with age. Higher protein intake (1.0-1.2g/kg or more) combined with resistance exercise helps preserve muscle mass and function.

**Calcium and vitamin D become more important.** Bone density loss accelerates; risk of fractures rises. Many older adults are vitamin D deficient.

**B12 absorption decreases.** Stomach acid (needed to release B12 from food) decreases with age. Supplementation or fortified foods are often recommended.

**Calorie needs typically decrease** as lean mass declines and activity often decreases. But nutrient needs don't decrease proportionally — diet quality becomes more important than ever.

**Hydration:** Thirst sensation decreases with age; older adults are more susceptible to dehydration. Drinking water regularly regardless of thirst is important.

## Diabetes and Blood Sugar Management

People with type 1 or type 2 diabetes need to pay attention to how food affects blood glucose.

**Focus on fiber:** Fiber slows glucose absorption, blunting blood sugar spikes. Vegetables, legumes, and whole grains over refined carbs.

**Watch refined carbs and sugars:** These cause rapid glucose rises. This doesn't mean eliminating carbs, but choosing low-glycemic sources.

**Portion control and consistent eating:** Large, infrequent meals cause larger glucose swings. Distributing carbs across meals helps.

**Specific guidance should come from a physician or registered dietitian** — diabetes management varies significantly based on type, medications, and individual glucose responses.

## Food Allergies and Intolerances

**Food allergies:** Immune-mediated reactions. Can range from mild to anaphylactic (life-threatening). Common allergens: peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, wheat, soy, fish, shellfish, sesame. Management is avoidance.

**Food intolerances:** Non-immune-mediated. Typically cause GI symptoms rather than immune reactions. Lactose intolerance (inability to digest lactose, the sugar in dairy) is common — managed by limiting dairy or using lactase supplements.

**Celiac disease:** An autoimmune condition (not a simple allergy) triggered by gluten. Requires strict gluten avoidance.
