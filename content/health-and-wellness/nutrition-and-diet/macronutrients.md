## Macronutrients: Protein, Carbs, and Fat

Macronutrients are the three main categories of nutrients that provide energy and the raw materials the body needs to function. Every food you eat is composed primarily of some combination of protein, carbohydrates, and fat — plus water, fiber, vitamins, and minerals.

Understanding what each macronutrient does helps you make sense of nutrition claims, evaluate your diet, and recognize when advice is oversimplified.

---

## Protein

**What it is:** Chains of amino acids. The body uses 20 amino acids total. 9 are "essential" — the body can't make them, so they must come from food. Complete proteins contain all 9 essential amino acids; incomplete proteins are missing one or more.

**What it does:**
- Builds and repairs tissue — muscle, skin, organs, hair, nails
- Makes enzymes (which catalyze biochemical reactions throughout the body)
- Makes hormones (insulin, growth hormone, and many others are proteins)
- Makes antibodies
- Transports molecules (hemoglobin carries oxygen; lipoproteins carry fats)
- Can be used for energy when carbs and fat are insufficient

**Sources:**
- Complete: meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy, soy, quinoa
- Incomplete (but complete when combined): beans, lentils, nuts, seeds, grains

**Calories:** 4 calories per gram.

**How much:** General recommendation is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day (about 0.36g per pound) — the minimum to prevent deficiency. Active people and those trying to build muscle need more: 1.2-2.0 g/kg is commonly recommended for athletes.

---

## Carbohydrates

**What it is:** Sugars, starches, and fiber. Simple carbohydrates are short chains of sugars (glucose, fructose, sucrose); complex carbohydrates are longer chains (starches, most fiber). All digestible carbs break down to glucose, which cells use as their primary fuel.

**What it does:**
- Primary energy source, especially for the brain and during high-intensity exercise
- Provides fiber (which isn't absorbed — but feeds gut bacteria, slows digestion, and supports bowel health)
- Stored as glycogen in liver and muscles for quick energy access

**Sources:**
- Starches: grains (rice, bread, pasta, oats), potatoes, legumes
- Sugars: fruit, dairy, honey, added sugars in processed foods
- Fiber: vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes

**Calories:** 4 calories per gram (fiber has minimal net calories since it's not fully absorbed).

**Glycemic response:** Different carbs raise blood glucose at different rates. Pure glucose raises it fastest; fiber and resistant starches raise it slowly. Large, rapid blood glucose spikes require larger insulin responses. Over time, repeated large spikes contribute to insulin resistance.

**The quality distinction:** "Carbs are bad" is too simple. Refined carbs (white bread, sugar, white rice) digest quickly and provide little fiber. Whole food carbs (vegetables, whole grains, legumes) come with fiber, vitamins, and minerals and digest more slowly. These behave quite differently in the body.

## Fat

**What it is:** Molecules made of fatty acid chains. Categorized by chemical structure:
- *Saturated:* Solid at room temperature (butter, lard, coconut oil, fat in meat). Raises LDL cholesterol.
- *Unsaturated (monounsaturated and polyunsaturated):* Liquid at room temperature (olive oil, most vegetable oils, avocado, nuts, fish). Generally beneficial for cardiovascular health.
- *Trans fats:* Artificially produced by partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils. Strongly associated with heart disease. Now largely banned from the food supply.
- *Omega-3 fatty acids:* A type of polyunsaturated fat (found in fatty fish, flaxseed, walnuts). Anti-inflammatory and important for brain and cardiovascular health.

**What it does:**
- Long-term energy storage
- Absorbs fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) — these can't be absorbed without dietary fat
- Builds cell membranes (membranes are mostly phospholipids)
- Makes hormones (steroid hormones — estrogen, testosterone, cortisol — are made from cholesterol)
- Insulates and protects organs
- Required for brain function (the brain is about 60% fat by dry weight)

**Sources:** Meat, fish, dairy, eggs, oils, nuts, seeds, avocado, olives.

**Calories:** 9 calories per gram — more than twice protein and carbs, which makes fat calorie-dense.

## Energy Balance: The Basics

Your body needs energy constantly — not just for movement, but for breathing, circulation, temperature regulation, cellular repair, and thought. The total energy your body uses at rest is called the basal metabolic rate (BMR). Add in activity and you get total daily energy expenditure.

Eat more than you expend: the excess is stored (mostly as body fat). Eat less: your body draws on stored energy. This isn't the full picture of body weight (hormones, appetite regulation, and other factors matter), but energy balance is the foundation.

All three macronutrients contribute to energy intake. The idea that one macronutrient is the cause of obesity or the key to weight loss is almost always an oversimplification.
