## Understanding Your Health Insurance

Health insurance is designed to be confusing. The terminology is dense, the math isn't transparent, and most people don't understand their coverage until they get a bill they didn't expect. Learning the basics prevents costly surprises and helps you make better decisions about care.

---

## The Key Terms

**Premium:** The monthly amount you pay for insurance regardless of whether you use it. For employer-sponsored plans, the employer pays part of the premium; you pay the rest through payroll deduction.

**Deductible:** The amount you pay out of pocket for covered services before your insurance starts paying. A $2,000 deductible means you pay the first $2,000 of covered services each year. After that, the insurance starts sharing costs.

Exception: most plans cover preventive care (annual physicals, screenings, vaccinations) at 100% without requiring the deductible to be met first.

**Copay:** A fixed amount you pay for a specific service, regardless of the deductible. "Your primary care copay is $25" means you pay $25 every time you visit a PCP, regardless of where you are relative to your deductible. Copays are common for office visits and prescriptions.

**Coinsurance:** After your deductible is met, some plans split the remaining costs as a percentage. "80/20 coinsurance" means your plan pays 80%, you pay 20%. This continues until you hit your out-of-pocket maximum.

**Out-of-pocket maximum:** The most you'll pay in a year for covered services. Once you hit this amount, insurance pays 100% for the rest of the year. This is the ceiling on your financial exposure from healthcare costs for the year.

**Network:** The group of providers (doctors, hospitals, labs) that have agreements with your insurance company and provide services at negotiated rates.

---

## In-Network vs. Out-of-Network

**In-network:** Providers who have contracts with your insurance. You pay contracted rates, which are much lower than list prices. Your plan covers a much higher percentage of costs.

**Out-of-network:** Providers who don't have contracts with your insurance. You pay much more — sometimes everything. Out-of-network care can result in very large unexpected bills.

**Why this catches people:** You can be in an in-network hospital but treated by an out-of-network physician (a specialist, an anesthesiologist, an ER doctor who isn't employed by the hospital). The No Surprises Act limits some of this, but you should always verify network status before non-emergency care.

---

## Types of Plans

**HMO (Health Maintenance Organization):** Requires you to choose a primary care physician who coordinates all your care. Need referrals from your PCP to see specialists. Generally no out-of-network coverage (except emergencies). Lower premiums and copays.

**PPO (Preferred Provider Organization):** More flexibility — no PCP requirement, no referrals for specialists, some out-of-network coverage (at higher cost). Higher premiums.

**EPO (Exclusive Provider Organization):** Like a PPO but with no out-of-network coverage except emergencies. No referral required.

**HDHP (High Deductible Health Plan):** Higher deductible, lower premium. Often paired with an HSA. Makes sense if you're healthy and rarely use care, or if the premium savings plus HSA contributions offset the higher deductible risk.

---

## HSA and FSA

**HSA (Health Savings Account):** Available only with qualifying HDHPs. You (and/or your employer) contribute pre-tax money. You can use it for qualifying medical expenses now, or invest it and let it grow tax-free for future healthcare costs. Funds roll over indefinitely — it's a tax-advantaged account specifically for healthcare. Maxing your HSA contribution if you have an HDHP is generally good financial practice.

**FSA (Flexible Spending Account):** Pre-tax contribution to pay for medical expenses. Usually "use it or lose it" — funds expire at year end (though some plans allow limited rollover). Lower limit than HSA. Doesn't require an HDHP.

## Before Getting Care: Verify

**Is my provider in-network?** Call the insurance company or check the online portal. Don't rely on the provider's office — they may be wrong or their information may be outdated.

**Does this require prior authorization?** Some procedures, medications, and specialty care require insurance approval before you get the service. If you skip this step, the claim may be denied. Your doctor's office typically handles this, but confirm.

**What's my coverage for this?** Call the member services number on your insurance card and ask specifically: "If I go to [hospital] for [procedure], what will my costs be?"

---

## If You Get a Surprise Bill

1. Request an itemized bill — a line-by-line breakdown of every charge. Errors are common.
2. Review the Explanation of Benefits (EOB) from your insurer — what they paid vs. what they say you owe.
3. If the bill seems wrong: call your insurer first, then the provider. Many billing errors are correctable.
4. If you can't pay the full amount: ask about payment plans, financial assistance programs, or charity care. Many hospitals have significant financial assistance programs for lower-income patients.
5. If you received out-of-network care unexpectedly: the No Surprises Act may protect you — contact your insurer.

---

## When You Don't Have Insurance

**Medicaid:** Government insurance for people below a certain income level. Free or very low cost. Eligibility and coverage varies by state. Apply through your state's Medicaid office or healthcare.gov.

**Marketplace plans:** Government exchange (healthcare.gov or state equivalent). Subsidized based on income. Open enrollment is typically November-January; qualifying life events (losing job-based coverage, having a baby) allow enrollment at other times.

**Community health centers:** Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) serve patients regardless of ability to pay, with sliding-scale fees based on income. findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov.

**Free clinics:** Many areas have free or low-cost clinics run by nonprofits and volunteer providers.
