## Medical Billing: How to Read and Fight Bills

Medical bills are notoriously opaque, frequently wrong, and often negotiable. Most people pay whatever they receive without question — this is a mistake that costs real money. Medical billing errors are common, prices are often arbitrary, and financial assistance is frequently available for those who ask.

---

## The Basic Structure

Medical billing flows through multiple parties and multiple documents:

**Chargemaster price:** The hospital or provider's "sticker price" — a list of charges that is deliberately set high and that almost no one actually pays. Insurance companies negotiate it down dramatically; self-pay patients are often charged close to this rate unless they negotiate.

**Claim:** The bill submitted by your provider to your insurance company.

**Explanation of Benefits (EOB):** The statement your insurance company sends after processing a claim. Shows: what the provider billed, what the insurance paid, what was adjusted (written off), and what you owe. The EOB is not a bill — it's an explanation of how insurance processed the claim.

**Bill from the provider:** The actual bill to you, showing what remains after insurance payment. This is what you owe (subject to errors and negotiation).

---

## Request an Itemized Bill

Always request an itemized bill for any significant medical charge. A line-by-line breakdown of every service and supply charged.

The summary bill shows only the total. The itemized bill shows:
- Every service billed (and the billing code)
- Every supply (including individual items like gloves or gauze that can be charged separately)
- Every procedure

Errors in itemized bills are extremely common:
- Duplicate charges (the same service billed twice)
- Charges for services you didn't receive
- Upcoding (billing for a more complex service than was performed)
- Wrong billing codes that affect coverage
- Charges for time you weren't actually at the facility

Review your itemized bill against your own recollection of what care you received.

---

## Compare Against Your EOB

Your EOB from the insurance company shows what the insurer processed and what they say you owe. Compare this to the provider's bill. They should match.

If the provider's bill is higher than what the EOB says you owe: the provider may be billing you for more than your insurance says you owe. Call both parties.

If services appear on your bill that weren't on the EOB: they may not have been submitted to insurance, or the claim may have been rejected. Find out why.

---

## Appeal Denied Claims

Insurance companies deny claims. Sometimes the denial is correct; often it isn't.

If a claim is denied:
1. Read the denial reason carefully (it's on the EOB or a separate denial notice)
2. Common denial reasons: not medically necessary, prior authorization not obtained, out-of-network provider, incorrect billing code
3. File an appeal with your insurer. Request forms from member services. Appeal deadlines are usually 30-180 days.
4. Ask your provider's billing department to help with the appeal — they do this regularly
5. Internal appeal first. If that fails, external review by an independent third party (required by law for most plans)

**Success rate:** Appeals are frequently successful. Even if you think the denial is legitimate, filing an appeal often results in coverage because insurers reconsider or providers submit additional documentation.

## Negotiate the Bill

Medical bills are often negotiable, especially if you're uninsured or the bill exceeds the negotiated rate your insurance would have paid.

**Ask about financial assistance / charity care.** Most hospitals (especially nonprofits) have financial assistance programs for lower-income patients. Income-based discounts can be substantial. Ask the billing department or financial counselor about eligibility.

**Offer a lump-sum payment.** Providers and collections agencies often accept less than the full amount if you can pay immediately. A cash offer of 40-60% of the bill is often accepted, especially for old debt or debt in collections.

**Ask for the "cash pay" or "insurance rate."** If you're uninsured, ask for the rate at which the hospital accepts insurance payment — which is dramatically lower than the chargemaster price.

**Payment plans.** Most providers will establish a payment plan. For a plan that doesn't charge interest (many providers offer zero-interest plans), this is preferable to using a credit card.

## Medical Debt

Medical debt is the leading cause of personal bankruptcy in the United States. Some facts that matter:

**Medical debt and credit reports:** As of 2023, paid medical debt no longer appears on credit reports, and medical debt under $500 is not reported. Larger unpaid medical debt may still appear but typically not for the first 12 months, giving you time to resolve it.

**You can negotiate medical debt in collections.** If debt has been sent to collections, offer a lump sum settlement. Collectors buy debt for cents on the dollar and can often accept significantly less than the original amount.

**Do not ignore medical bills.** While there are protections, ignoring bills doesn't make them go away and can result in collection accounts, credit damage, and in some cases lawsuits.

**Look into medical debt forgiveness programs.** Some hospitals have programs that forgive medical debt entirely for qualifying patients. RIP Medical Debt is a nonprofit that buys and forgives medical debt. State programs also exist in some states.

---

## The Patient Advocate Option

Medical billing advocates and patient advocates are professionals who help patients navigate billing disputes, understand insurance, and negotiate bills. They often work on contingency — a percentage of what they save you. For large, complex bills, this can be worthwhile.
