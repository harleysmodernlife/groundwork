## Your Medical Records and Your Rights

Your medical records contain your complete health history — diagnoses, medications, test results, imaging, surgical reports, and provider notes. They're yours. Knowing your rights to access them, how to get them, and why maintaining your own records matters puts you in control of your healthcare.

---

## HIPAA: Your Privacy Rights

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) is the federal law governing medical privacy. It gives you several important rights:

**Right to access your records:** You have the right to see and get copies of your medical records from healthcare providers and insurers. Providers must respond to access requests within 30 days (60 days with an extension).

**Right to amend your records:** If you believe information in your records is inaccurate or incomplete, you can request an amendment. The provider can deny the request (with justification) but must document your request.

**Right to an accounting of disclosures:** You can request a list of who your records have been shared with.

**Right to restrict disclosures:** You can request that your information not be shared with certain parties in some circumstances.

**Right to a copy of your records:** Providers can charge a reasonable fee for copying, but cannot deny access because of inability to pay.

## How to Request Your Records

Most healthcare providers now have patient portals — online systems where you can view visit summaries, lab results, medications, and sometimes full records. Set up your patient portal with every provider you see regularly.

For records not in a portal:
1. Contact the medical records department (not the front desk) of the provider or hospital
2. Submit a written request (they may have a form; if not, a letter works)
3. Specify what you want: all records, records from a date range, specific types (labs, imaging reports, visit notes)
4. Provide your identifying information and a valid ID

**Electronic records:** Under the 21st Century Cures Act, providers are required to give you free electronic access to your full health record through APIs — you should be able to export your records through patient portals.

**Time limit:** Providers must fulfill requests within 30 days.

## Why You Should Maintain Your Own Records

The healthcare system is fragmented. Your cardiologist may not know what your dermatologist prescribed. Your urgent care records may not reach your primary care doctor. Tests may be repeated unnecessarily because results aren't shared. In emergencies, providers may not have access to critical information.

Maintaining your own records fills these gaps.

**What to keep:**
- Problem list: all current diagnoses and conditions
- Medication list: names, doses, prescribing provider, start dates
- Allergy list: medications and substances, with reactions noted
- Vaccination record
- Key test results: baseline labs, imaging reports, EKGs
- Surgical and hospitalization history
- Family medical history (useful for assessing your own risk)

A simple document that you update and bring to appointments — or have available on your phone — makes every healthcare encounter more effective.

## Sharing Your Records

When seeing a new provider:
- Request records from your previous provider to be sent ahead
- Bring key documents yourself (don't rely entirely on transferred records)
- Fill out the new patient forms completely and accurately

**Records transfer:**
- Providers can transfer records directly to other providers with your authorization
- You can also request your records, then bring or mail them yourself

For specialists: make sure the specialist's report gets back to your primary care doctor. This coordination often doesn't happen automatically.

## Emergency Access

If you're incapacitated in an emergency, providers need access to critical information quickly. Options:

**Emergency medical ID:** The health app on iPhones and some Android phones has a medical ID accessible from the lock screen — viewable by first responders without unlocking the phone. Include: conditions, medications, allergies, emergency contacts, blood type.

**Medical alert jewelry:** Bracelets or necklaces listing critical conditions (diabetes, epilepsy, severe allergies, anticoagulant use) that emergency providers are trained to look for.

**Paper card in wallet:** A medical information card listing key conditions, medications, allergies, and emergency contact.

These can make the difference in emergencies where you can't speak for yourself.

## Mental Health Records: Special Protections

Mental health records receive additional protections beyond HIPAA in many states. Psychotherapy notes (a therapist's personal notes about sessions) are separately protected — providers can withhold these even when you request records, though they must provide a summary. Mental health treatment records may have stricter rules about who can access them without your consent.

---

## Your Records Are Yours

Don't assume healthcare providers have everything, that records have been transferred correctly, or that your full history is visible to any provider who treats you. Take an active role in knowing your own medical history and ensuring that information is available when and where it's needed.
