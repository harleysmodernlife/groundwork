## Prescriptions, Generic Drugs, and Medication Safety

Medications are a significant part of healthcare for most adults at some point, and the prescription drug system in the United States is particularly confusing and expensive. Understanding how it works helps you save money, avoid dangerous errors, and get the most from your medications.

---

## Brand vs. Generic Drugs

**Brand-name drugs** are drugs under patent, sold by the company that developed them. The FDA approval process is expensive, and companies charge high prices to recoup those costs and profit.

**Generic drugs** are chemically identical to the brand-name drug — same active ingredient, same dosage, same route of administration, same safety profile. The FDA requires generics to be "bioequivalent" — they must enter the bloodstream at the same rate and to the same extent as the brand.

**The practical reality:** A generic is, for clinical purposes, the same drug. The differences are in the inactive ingredients (fillers, dyes, binders) and the price — generics typically cost 80-90% less than brand-name equivalents.

**When brand might matter:** Narrow therapeutic index medications — drugs where even small differences in absorption can matter (certain seizure medications, thyroid medications, blood thinners) — sometimes warrant brand consistency. Your doctor can advise if this applies to your medication.

For the vast majority of prescriptions: ask for the generic. If your insurance requires the brand, ask your doctor if a generic equivalent exists.

## Navigating Prescription Costs

**Your insurance formulary:** Insurance plans have a formulary — a tiered list of covered drugs and what you pay for each tier. Tier 1 (generic) = lowest cost. Tier 2 (preferred brand) = moderate. Tier 3 and above (non-preferred brand, specialty) = highest.

Check your formulary before starting a new medication. If a prescribed drug is expensive, ask your doctor or pharmacist if an equivalent drug at a lower tier is available.

**GoodRx and similar tools:** Discount cards and services that negotiate prescription prices. In many cases, using GoodRx at a pharmacy is cheaper than using insurance. Compare both — you can't use both for the same transaction, but you can choose the better price. GoodRx has a free app and website.

**Manufacturer coupons and patient assistance programs:** Drug manufacturers often offer savings programs for brand-name medications. If you need a brand-name drug and can't afford it, look for the manufacturer's patient assistance program — often free or very low cost for qualifying patients.

**Mail-order pharmacies:** Often cheaper for maintenance medications (drugs you take daily long-term). Many insurance plans offer 90-day supplies at reduced cost through mail-order.

**Mark Cuban's Cost Plus Drugs (costplusdrugs.com):** Transparent cost-plus pricing for many generic drugs. Some medications are dramatically cheaper here than at traditional pharmacies.

## How to Take Medications Safely

**Take as prescribed.** Skipping doses, taking extra, or stopping early (especially with antibiotics) can reduce effectiveness, cause resistance, or cause withdrawal effects with some medications.

**Keep an updated medication list.** Know every medication you take — prescription, over-the-counter, vitamins, supplements. Share this list with every healthcare provider. Many dangerous drug interactions happen because providers don't know what else a patient is taking.

**Understand what your medication is for and its common side effects.** Ask your pharmacist — this is a free service, pharmacists are trained medication experts, and they're often more accessible than your doctor for medication questions.

**Report side effects.** Don't just stop a medication because of a side effect — contact your prescriber. Some side effects are manageable or resolve with time; others are serious enough to warrant stopping or switching.

**Storage matters.** Most medications should be stored in a cool, dry place — not the bathroom medicine cabinet (heat and humidity degrade medications). Some medications require refrigeration.

**Expiration dates:** Medications degrade over time. Most aren't dangerous after expiration but may be less effective. Don't use expired medications for serious conditions.

## Common Drug Interactions

Some combinations are dangerous. The most important to know:

**Alcohol with medications:** Alcohol interacts with many medications — sedatives, sleep aids, pain medications, some antibiotics, and many others. Read the warning label and ask your pharmacist.

**Blood thinners (warfarin):** Interacts with many drugs and foods (especially vitamin K in leafy greens). Requires careful monitoring.

**Grapefruit:** Contains compounds that inhibit an enzyme that metabolizes many drugs, causing drug levels to rise unpredictably. Many statins, some blood pressure medications, and other drugs are affected. Check with your pharmacist.

**St. John's Wort:** A common herbal supplement that significantly alters metabolism of many drugs, including birth control, HIV medications, and many psychiatric drugs. Not harmless because it's "natural."

**The lesson:** Always tell providers and pharmacists about everything you take — prescription, OTC, supplements, herbals. Never assume something is safe to combine without checking.

## Polypharmacy

Polypharmacy — taking many medications simultaneously — is common, particularly in older adults. It increases risk of interactions, falls, confusion, and adverse effects.

If you're taking five or more medications, periodic medication reviews with your pharmacist or doctor are valuable. Some medications prescribed years ago may no longer be necessary; dosages may need adjustment as age affects drug metabolism.
