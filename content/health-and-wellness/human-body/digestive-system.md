## The Digestive System

The food you eat is mostly large, complex molecules that your cells can't use directly. The digestive system breaks everything down into components small enough to absorb into the bloodstream — and extracts as much nutritional value as possible along the way.

---

## The Digestive Tract: A Long Tube

The digestive system is essentially a tube running from mouth to anus, about 30 feet long when stretched out. Each section is specialized for a different phase of digestion.

**Mouth:** Digestion starts here, not in the stomach. Teeth mechanically break food into smaller pieces. Saliva (produced by salivary glands) moistens food for swallowing and contains amylase — an enzyme that begins breaking down starch into sugars.

**Esophagus:** A muscular tube connecting mouth to stomach. Food moves down by peristalsis — coordinated waves of muscle contraction that push food forward. The lower esophageal sphincter (a ring of muscle) opens to let food into the stomach and closes to prevent stomach acid from flowing back up. When it doesn't close properly: acid reflux.

**Stomach:** A muscular pouch that churns food mechanically and bathes it in gastric acid (hydrochloric acid). The acid kills most pathogens in food and activates pepsin, an enzyme that begins protein digestion. The stomach produces a thick mucus lining to protect itself from its own acid — when this protection breaks down: stomach ulcers. Food leaves the stomach as chyme (a thick liquid) through the pyloric sphincter into the small intestine.

**Small intestine:** Where most digestion and absorption happens. About 20 feet long, divided into three sections (duodenum, jejunum, ileum). The inner surface is covered in villi (finger-like projections) and microvilli that massively increase surface area for absorption — the total absorptive surface is roughly the size of a tennis court.

Digestive enzymes from the pancreas (amylase for carbs, proteases for proteins, lipase for fats) and bile from the liver/gallbladder (which emulsifies fats) enter the small intestine to complete breakdown. Nutrients — glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals — are absorbed here into the bloodstream and lymphatic system.

**Large intestine (colon):** The remaining material (primarily water, electrolytes, and indigestible fiber) moves into the colon. The colon absorbs water and some minerals, compacting what remains into feces. Trillions of bacteria live in the colon — the gut microbiome — playing important roles in health, immune function, and even mental health through the gut-brain axis.

**Rectum and anus:** Storage and elimination of feces.

## Accessory Organs

**Liver:** Receives nutrient-rich blood directly from the small intestine. Processes nutrients, removes toxins, produces bile (stored in the gallbladder), makes blood proteins, and regulates blood glucose by storing or releasing glucose as needed. The liver is the body's primary chemical processing plant.

**Gallbladder:** Stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver. Releases bile into the small intestine when fat is present. Gallstones form when bile components crystallize — can cause severe pain if they block bile ducts.

**Pancreas:** Two functions: endocrine (producing insulin and glucagon hormones, released into the bloodstream) and exocrine (producing digestive enzymes, released into the small intestine). Pancreatic insufficiency or inflammation (pancreatitis) significantly impairs digestion.

## Common Digestive Conditions

**Acid reflux / GERD:** Stomach acid enters the esophagus, causing heartburn. Triggers include lying down after eating, certain foods, excess weight, smoking. Chronic GERD can damage the esophageal lining.

**Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS):** Altered bowel function — diarrhea, constipation, or both — often with abdominal pain. Not caused by structural damage; often related to gut-brain axis dysfunction. Common and often manageable with diet and stress management.

**Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD):** Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. Chronic inflammation of the digestive tract. More serious than IBS — involves actual tissue damage and requires medical treatment.

**Celiac disease:** An autoimmune reaction to gluten (a protein in wheat, barley, and rye) that damages the small intestine lining. Impairs nutrient absorption. Treated with a strict gluten-free diet.

## The Gut Microbiome

The human gut contains roughly 100 trillion bacteria representing hundreds of species. This microbiome:
- Helps digest certain fibers that human enzymes can't break down
- Produces vitamins (B12, K)
- Trains and regulates the immune system
- Communicates with the brain (the gut-brain axis affects mood, stress, and cognition)
- Protects against pathogenic bacteria

Microbiome health is influenced by diet (fiber-rich diets support diverse, beneficial bacteria), antibiotics (which can disrupt it), and other factors. Research on the microbiome is one of the most active areas in health science.
