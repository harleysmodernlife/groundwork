## The Respiratory System: Lungs and Breathing

Every cell in your body needs oxygen and produces carbon dioxide as a waste product. The respiratory system solves both problems simultaneously — bringing oxygen in and removing carbon dioxide — around 20,000 times a day.

---

## The Path Air Takes

**Nose and mouth:** Air enters here. The nose warms, humidifies, and filters air. Nose hairs and mucus trap particles. Breathing through your nose is better than mouth breathing for most situations — the nose does filtration and temperature adjustment that the mouth doesn't.

**Pharynx and larynx:** The throat and voice box. The epiglottis — a flap of cartilage — covers the trachea when you swallow, preventing food from entering the airway.

**Trachea (windpipe):** A tube reinforced with C-shaped cartilage rings to keep it open. Lined with cilia and mucus that trap particles and move them upward (the mucociliary escalator) to be coughed out or swallowed.

**Bronchi:** The trachea splits into two bronchi — one for each lung. These branch repeatedly into smaller and smaller tubes (bronchioles), forming the bronchial tree.

**Alveoli:** The end point. Tiny air sacs at the end of the bronchioles — the lungs contain about 480 million of them. Alveoli are surrounded by capillaries. Gas exchange happens here: oxygen crosses from the alveolus into the blood; carbon dioxide crosses from the blood into the alveolus and is exhaled.

---

## How Breathing Works

**Inhalation:** The diaphragm (a dome-shaped muscle below the lungs) contracts and flattens downward. The rib cage expands. Lung volume increases, pressure inside the lungs drops below atmospheric pressure, and air flows in — the same principle as a bellows.

**Exhalation:** The diaphragm relaxes and rises. Rib cage returns to resting position. Lung volume decreases, pressure inside the lungs rises above atmospheric pressure, and air flows out. At rest, exhalation is mostly passive — no muscle contraction needed. During exercise, abdominal muscles actively help push air out.

**Breathing rate:** Controlled by the brainstem, primarily in response to blood CO2 levels (not oxygen, as most people assume). When CO2 rises, breathing rate increases. When CO2 falls (like during hyperventilation), breathing slows or a breath-hold occurs.

---

## Gas Exchange

**Oxygen transport:** Oxygen that crosses from alveoli into the blood binds to hemoglobin in red blood cells. Hemoglobin is well-designed for this: it picks up oxygen in the high-oxygen environment of the lungs and releases it in the low-oxygen, high-CO2 environment of active tissues.

**Carbon dioxide transport:** CO2 is transported in three ways: dissolved in plasma, bound to hemoglobin, and (most commonly) converted to bicarbonate ions in the blood. At the lungs, bicarbonate converts back to CO2, which is exhaled.

**Oxygen saturation:** The percentage of hemoglobin carrying oxygen. Normal is 95-100%. Below 90%, cells aren't getting enough oxygen — a medical concern. Pulse oximeters clip to a finger and measure this non-invasively using light wavelengths.

## Common Respiratory Conditions

**Asthma:** Airway inflammation and bronchospasm (tightening of the bronchiole muscles) narrow the airways. Triggered by allergens, exercise, cold air, irritants. Symptoms: wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, shortness of breath. Treated with bronchodilators (rescue inhalers) and anti-inflammatory medications (corticosteroids).

**COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease):** Usually caused by smoking. Emphysema (destruction of alveolar walls, reducing gas exchange surface area) and chronic bronchitis (chronic airway inflammation and excess mucus). Irreversible but manageable. Smoking cessation is the most important intervention.

**Pneumonia:** Infection of the alveoli. Alveoli fill with fluid or pus, impairing gas exchange. Symptoms: fever, productive cough, shortness of breath. Caused by bacteria, viruses, or fungi.

**Pulmonary embolism:** A blood clot in the lungs' blood vessels. Blocks blood flow to a section of lung — that area can still breathe but can't exchange gas with blood. Symptoms: sudden chest pain, shortness of breath, rapid heart rate.

---

## Why This Matters

The respiratory and cardiovascular systems are tightly coupled — one brings oxygen into the blood, the other moves it around. Disease in either system affects the other. Respiratory conditions reduce oxygen delivery; cardiovascular conditions reduce blood flow to the lungs. This is why shortness of breath is a symptom of both lung and heart problems.

Exercise is the most effective way to strengthen both systems. Regular aerobic exercise increases the efficiency of gas exchange and the heart's ability to pump oxygenated blood to working muscles.
