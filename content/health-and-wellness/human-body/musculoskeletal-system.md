## Muscles and Bones

The musculoskeletal system gives the body its structure and enables movement. Bones provide a rigid framework; muscles generate force to move that framework. Together they allow everything from a heartbeat to a sprint.

---

## The Skeletal System

The adult human body has 206 bones. They do far more than provide structure.

**Functions of bone:**
- **Support:** The skeleton holds the body upright and provides attachment points for muscles
- **Protection:** The skull protects the brain; the rib cage protects the heart and lungs; the vertebrae protect the spinal cord
- **Movement:** Bones act as levers that muscles pull against
- **Blood cell production:** Red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets are produced in bone marrow (red marrow, primarily in flat bones and the ends of long bones)
- **Mineral storage:** Bones store calcium and phosphorus. When blood calcium drops, the body draws calcium from bone — a process that, over time, can weaken bones (osteoporosis)

**Bone is alive.** It's constantly being broken down (by cells called osteoclasts) and rebuilt (by osteoblasts) in a process called remodeling. Exercise creates stress that stimulates osteoblasts to build more bone — which is why weight-bearing exercise is protective against osteoporosis.

**Types of bone:**
- *Long bones* (femur, humerus): Provide levers for movement
- *Short bones* (wrist, ankle): Allow complex range of motion
- *Flat bones* (skull, ribs, sternum): Protect organs, provide large muscle attachment surfaces
- *Irregular bones* (vertebrae): Specialized shapes for specific functions

**Joints:** Where bones meet. Types range from immovable (fused skull bones) to slightly movable (vertebrae) to freely movable synovial joints (knees, hips, shoulders). Synovial joints are lined with cartilage and filled with synovial fluid — a lubricant that reduces friction. Cartilage has no blood supply and repairs slowly or not at all — which is why cartilage damage (as in osteoarthritis) tends to be lasting.

## The Muscular System

There are roughly 600 named skeletal muscles in the body. Muscle works by contracting — shortening to pull two attachment points closer together.

**Skeletal muscle structure:** Each muscle is made of bundles of muscle fibers (each fiber is a single muscle cell). Each fiber contains myofibrils, which contain the proteins actin and myosin. Contraction occurs when myosin heads pull actin filaments, shortening the sarcomere — the basic contractile unit.

**How muscles move bones:** Muscles attach to bones via tendons (strong connective tissue cables). Most skeletal muscles work in pairs — one contracts (the agonist) while the opposite relaxes (the antagonist). The bicep flexes the elbow; the tricep extends it. They can't both contract maximally at the same time.

**Muscle fiber types:**
- *Slow-twitch (Type I):* Fatigue-resistant, aerobic. Suited for endurance — long-distance running, postural muscles.
- *Fast-twitch (Type II):* More powerful, fatigue quickly. Suited for speed and power — sprinting, lifting.

**How muscles grow:** Resistance exercise creates micro-tears in muscle fibers. During recovery, satellite cells repair the damage and add new contractile protein — the muscle grows back slightly larger and stronger. This is hypertrophy. No recovery, no growth — why rest days matter.

## Common Musculoskeletal Conditions

**Osteoarthritis:** Cartilage in joints wears away, causing bones to rub against each other. Painful and progressive. Most common in knees, hips, and hands. Treatment focuses on managing pain and maintaining function; joint replacement surgery for severe cases.

**Osteoporosis:** Loss of bone density. Bones become brittle and prone to fracture. Common in older women after menopause, when estrogen (which protects bone) declines. Prevented with adequate calcium and vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, and sometimes medication.

**Tendinitis:** Inflammation of a tendon from overuse. Common at the shoulder, elbow (tennis elbow, golfer's elbow), knee (patellar tendinitis), and heel (Achilles tendinitis). Treated with rest, ice, anti-inflammatory medication, and physical therapy.

**Muscle strains and sprains:** A strain is injury to a muscle or tendon; a sprain is injury to a ligament (which connects bone to bone). Graded by severity — from microscopic tears to complete rupture.

**Lower back pain:** Extremely common. Most often involves muscles, ligaments, or discs (the cushioning between vertebrae). The majority of acute low back pain resolves on its own in a few weeks with movement (not bed rest — immobility slows recovery) and appropriate pain management.

## Bone and Muscle Health

- **Weight-bearing exercise** (walking, running, strength training) builds both muscle and bone
- **Protein** is essential for muscle repair and growth
- **Calcium and vitamin D** are essential for bone health — found in dairy, fortified foods, and sunlight exposure
- **Stretching and flexibility work** maintain joint range of motion and reduce injury risk
- **Smoking and alcohol** both impair bone density and muscle recovery
