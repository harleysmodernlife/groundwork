## The Cell: Basic Unit of Life

Everything your body does — breathing, thinking, moving, healing — happens because of cells. The human body contains roughly 37 trillion of them. Understanding what cells are and how they work is the foundation of understanding biology, health, and disease.

---

## What a Cell Is

A cell is the smallest unit of life. Every living thing is made of cells — bacteria are single cells, while humans are made of trillions of specialized cells working together. Cells carry out all the chemical processes that keep you alive.

Most human cells share a common structure:

**Cell membrane:** The outer boundary of the cell. A thin, flexible layer made of lipids (fats) that controls what enters and exits the cell. It's selectively permeable — some substances can cross freely, others need help, and some are blocked entirely.

**Nucleus:** The control center. Contains your DNA — the genetic instructions for everything the cell does. The nucleus directs cell activity and reproduction. Not all cells have a nucleus (red blood cells lose theirs as they mature), but most do.

**Cytoplasm:** The fluid-filled interior of the cell. Mostly water with dissolved proteins, salts, and other molecules. The cytoplasm is where many of the cell's chemical reactions occur.

**Mitochondria:** The energy producers. Often called the "powerhouse of the cell" — and for good reason. Mitochondria convert nutrients (especially glucose and oxygen) into ATP, the energy currency the cell uses to do everything. Cells that need a lot of energy (heart muscle, brain cells) have more mitochondria.

**Ribosomes:** Protein builders. These tiny structures read genetic instructions and assemble proteins. Proteins do almost everything in the body — they're enzymes, structural materials, hormones, antibodies, and more.

**Endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus:** Processing and shipping. The ER builds and processes proteins and lipids; the Golgi packages and distributes them to where they're needed inside or outside the cell.

## Cell Functions

Different cell types are specialized for different jobs:

- **Muscle cells** contract to produce movement
- **Nerve cells (neurons)** carry electrical signals
- **Red blood cells** transport oxygen
- **White blood cells** fight infection
- **Epithelial cells** line surfaces (skin, gut, lungs) as barriers
- **Fat cells (adipocytes)** store energy
- **Bone cells (osteocytes)** maintain bone structure

One cell type can't do another's job — this specialization is what makes complex organisms possible.

---

## Cell Division

Cells reproduce by dividing. There are two types:

**Mitosis:** Normal cell division. One cell splits into two identical daughter cells. This is how the body grows, replaces worn-out cells, and heals wounds. You make billions of new cells every day through mitosis.

**Meiosis:** Reproductive cell division. Produces sperm and egg cells with half the normal chromosome count — so that when they combine at fertilization, the result has the right total.

Cell division is tightly regulated. When that regulation breaks down — cells divide uncontrollably — the result is cancer.

---

## Why Cells Matter to Your Health

Almost every disease ultimately involves something going wrong at the cellular level:

- **Infection:** Viruses hijack cells to copy themselves; bacteria can damage or destroy cells
- **Cancer:** Cells divide out of control
- **Diabetes:** Cells can't effectively use insulin to absorb glucose
- **Heart disease:** Heart muscle cells are damaged by reduced blood flow
- **Neurological disease:** Nerve cells degenerate or are damaged

When doctors talk about treating disease, they're ultimately trying to fix cellular problems — restoring normal function, killing abnormal cells, or protecting healthy ones.
