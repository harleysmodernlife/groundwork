## Tissues, Organs, and Organ Systems

Individual cells don't work in isolation. They organize into tissues, tissues form organs, and organs work together in systems. This hierarchy is how the body coordinates trillions of cells into one functioning whole.

---

## The Four Types of Tissue

A tissue is a group of similar cells working together to perform a specific function.

**Epithelial tissue:** Covers surfaces and lines cavities. Your skin is epithelial tissue. So are the linings of your lungs, digestive tract, and blood vessels. Epithelial tissue acts as a barrier — protecting underlying structures and controlling what moves between compartments. It also forms glands that secrete hormones, enzymes, and other substances.

**Connective tissue:** Supports, connects, and holds things together. This is the most diverse tissue type. Bone, cartilage, tendons, ligaments, fat, and blood are all connective tissues — all different, but all share the property of having cells embedded in an extracellular matrix (the material between cells). Connective tissue binds organs together, cushions joints, stores fat, and transports blood.

**Muscle tissue:** Contracts to produce movement. Three types:
- *Skeletal muscle* — attached to bone, under voluntary control, responsible for movement you choose to make
- *Cardiac muscle* — found only in the heart, involuntary, contracts continuously
- *Smooth muscle* — in walls of organs (blood vessels, gut, uterus), involuntary, responsible for things like digestion and blood pressure

**Nervous tissue:** Carries electrical signals. Neurons transmit information throughout the body; glial cells support and protect neurons. Nervous tissue is found in the brain, spinal cord, and nerves throughout the body.

## Organs

An organ is a structure made of two or more tissue types working together to perform a specific function.

Your heart is a good example: it's made of cardiac muscle tissue (to contract and pump blood), connective tissue (to provide structure), epithelial tissue (lining its interior chambers), and nervous tissue (to regulate its rhythm). All four tissue types, one function.

Major organs include:
- **Heart** — pumps blood
- **Lungs** — exchange gases (oxygen in, carbon dioxide out)
- **Liver** — processes nutrients, filters blood, produces bile
- **Kidneys** — filter waste from blood, regulate fluid balance
- **Brain** — processes information, controls the body
- **Stomach and intestines** — digest food and absorb nutrients
- **Skin** — the body's largest organ; barrier, temperature regulation, sensation

## Organ Systems

Organs don't work alone either — they're grouped into systems that perform coordinated functions.

**Cardiovascular system:** Heart, blood vessels, blood. Circulates oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and waste products throughout the body.

**Respiratory system:** Lungs, airways, diaphragm. Brings oxygen in, removes carbon dioxide.

**Digestive system:** Mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, liver, gallbladder, pancreas. Breaks down food and absorbs nutrients.

**Nervous system:** Brain, spinal cord, nerves. Controls and coordinates all body functions.

**Musculoskeletal system:** Muscles, bones, cartilage, tendons, ligaments. Provides structure and enables movement.

**Immune system:** White blood cells, lymph nodes, spleen, thymus. Defends against infection and disease.

**Endocrine system:** Glands that produce hormones (thyroid, adrenal glands, pancreas, pituitary, and others). Regulates metabolism, growth, reproduction, and stress response.

**Urinary system:** Kidneys, bladder, ureters, urethra. Filters waste from blood and excretes it as urine.

**Reproductive system:** Produces offspring, regulates sex hormones.

---

## Why This Organization Matters for Health

Disease rarely affects a single cell in isolation. It propagates through tissues, affects organs, and can disrupt entire systems.

A heart attack damages cardiac muscle tissue, impairing the cardiovascular system, which then reduces oxygen delivery to every organ in the body. Understanding this hierarchy helps explain why treating complex conditions requires thinking about multiple systems at once — and why the body's systems must be kept in balance.
