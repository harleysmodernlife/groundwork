## Cardiovascular vs. Strength Training

Two fundamentally different types of exercise, two different adaptations, two different benefits. The question isn't which is better — both matter, and the answer depends on what you're trying to accomplish. Understanding each one helps you make smarter choices about how to spend your exercise time.

---

## Cardiovascular (Aerobic) Training

Cardio is exercise that elevates your heart rate and keeps it elevated for an extended period, with oxygen as the primary energy source.

**Examples:** Walking, running, cycling, swimming, rowing, dancing, hiking, elliptical.

**What it trains:** The cardiovascular system — heart, lungs, blood vessels. And the muscles' ability to use oxygen efficiently (aerobic capacity).

**Adaptations from consistent cardio:**
- Heart becomes more efficient (larger stroke volume, lower resting heart rate)
- Lungs become better at oxygen exchange
- Muscles develop more and larger mitochondria — more energy production capacity
- Blood vessels become more elastic
- The body becomes better at using fat as a fuel source at moderate intensities
- VO2 max (maximal oxygen uptake — a key measure of fitness) increases

**Heart rate zones:**

Exercise intensity is often described by heart rate as a percentage of maximum heart rate. A rough formula for maximum heart rate: 220 minus your age.

- *Zone 1 (50-60% max HR):* Very light — active recovery, gentle walking
- *Zone 2 (60-70%):* Comfortable pace — can hold a conversation. Most "base building" training happens here. Very important for aerobic development despite feeling easy.
- *Zone 3 (70-80%):* Moderate — harder to hold a full conversation. Threshold of significant cardiovascular challenge.
- *Zone 4 (80-90%):* Hard — very limited conversation. Significant calorie burn, strong cardiovascular stimulus.
- *Zone 5 (90%+):* Maximum effort — not sustainable for long. Sprint intervals.

Most cardio recommendations refer to "moderate intensity" (zone 3) or "vigorous" (zone 4+).

**Benefits emphasized by cardio:**
- Cardiovascular health and disease prevention
- Improved endurance and stamina
- Mental health benefits (especially mood and anxiety)
- Calorie expenditure
- Brain health and cognition

## Strength Training (Resistance Training)

Strength training involves working muscles against resistance — body weight, free weights, machines, resistance bands.

**Examples:** Weight lifting, bodyweight exercises (pushups, squats, pullups), resistance machines, kettlebells.

**What it trains:** Muscles, connective tissue (tendons, ligaments), and the nervous system's ability to recruit muscle fibers.

**Adaptations from consistent strength training:**
- *Muscular hypertrophy:* Muscle fibers enlarge. This is what people mean by "building muscle."
- *Neural adaptations:* The nervous system becomes better at recruiting muscle fibers. Strength gains in the first weeks of training are mostly neural, not muscle size.
- *Connective tissue strengthening:* Tendons and ligaments adapt to handle greater forces.
- *Bone density:* Mechanical loading from resistance training stimulates bone formation.
- *Resting metabolic rate:* Muscle tissue burns more energy at rest than fat tissue — more muscle mass means higher resting calorie burn.

**Key principles:**
- *Progressive overload:* Muscles must be challenged progressively — adding weight, reps, or sets over time — to continue adapting.
- *Specificity:* Training adaptations are specific. Bicep curls make you better at bicep curls. Full-body compound movements (squat, deadlift, row, press) produce broader functional strength.
- *Compound movements:* Exercises that work multiple joints and muscle groups simultaneously (squat, deadlift, bench press, overhead press, pull-up, row) are more time-efficient and functionally relevant than isolation exercises.

**Benefits emphasized by strength training:**
- Muscle mass (protective against sarcopenia — age-related muscle loss)
- Bone density (protective against osteoporosis)
- Functional strength and reduced injury risk
- Metabolic health (insulin sensitivity, resting metabolic rate)
- Appearance and body composition
- Reduced risk of falls in older adults

## Which Is Better?

Neither. They produce different adaptations and provide complementary benefits.

The research is clear that both types are independently valuable, and ideally everyone would do both. Current physical activity guidelines recommend:
- 150+ minutes per week of moderate cardio (or equivalent)
- 2+ days per week of muscle-strengthening activity

**If you can only do one:** Which one depends on your current baseline and goals.
- If you're very sedentary with cardiovascular disease risk factors, building a cardio base matters most.
- If you're older and losing muscle mass or bone density, strength training may be the priority.
- If you're generally healthy and starting from zero, doing a bit of both is better than optimizing for one.

**Concurrent training:** Doing both cardio and strength training. They don't cancel each other out — but very intense cardio immediately before or after strength training can blunt some strength adaptations. Separate sessions or careful sequencing helps, though for general health this is minor.

---

## The Most Important Factor

The best program is the one you'll actually do consistently. A "suboptimal" program you stick with produces far better results than an "optimal" program you abandon after three weeks.

Pick activities you don't hate. Start where you are. Build gradually.
