## Estimation and Rounding

Estimation is not a lazy shortcut — it is a precision skill. A good estimate tells you whether your exact answer is in the right ballpark, catches calculation errors before they cause real problems, and often provides all the precision a situation actually requires. In professional contexts, estimation is used constantly: engineers check whether designs are feasible, accountants sanity-check totals, and workers determine if a load will fit before they start hauling it.

---

## Why Estimation Matters

Exact calculation is not always possible on the fly — and often not necessary. If you're buying supplies and wondering whether $400 will be enough, a quick mental estimate is more useful than a precise total that takes five minutes to calculate.

More importantly: estimation is how you catch errors. If you calculate 47 × 82 and get 3,854, rounding to 50 × 80 = 4,000 immediately tells you the answer should be in the thousands — so 3,854 is plausible, but 38,540 would be obviously wrong.

---

## Rounding Review

Rounding is the foundational tool of estimation. Before estimating, round numbers to convenient values.

**The rule:** Look at the digit immediately right of the place you're rounding to.
- 5 or greater: round up
- 4 or less: round down

**Common rounding targets:**
- To the nearest 10: 347 → 350; 343 → 340
- To the nearest 100: 3,476 → 3,500; 3,429 → 3,400
- To the nearest 1,000: 47,600 → 48,000
- To the nearest whole number: 3.7 → 4; 3.2 → 3

Choose the rounding level based on how precise you need the estimate to be.

---

## Front-End Estimation

Use the leading (leftmost) digit of each number and ignore the rest.

**Example:** Estimate 4,812 + 3,241 + 1,978
- Keep front digits: 4,000 + 3,000 + 2,000 = **9,000**
- Exact answer: 10,031

This is a rough estimate — useful for checking order of magnitude (are we talking about 10,000 or 100,000?).

**Improved front-end estimation:** Also consider the second digit.
- 4,800 + 3,200 + 2,000 = **10,000** — much closer to 10,031.

---

## Compatible Numbers

Compatible numbers are numbers that are easy to compute mentally — typically multiples of 10, 25, or 100.

**Example:** Estimate 47 × 19
- Replace with compatible numbers: 50 × 20 = **1,000**
- Exact: 893. The estimate of 1,000 tells us we're in the right range.

**Example:** Estimate 3,780 ÷ 42
- 3,780 is close to 4,000; 42 is close to 40.
- 4,000 ÷ 40 = **100**
- Exact: 90. Close enough for a quick check.

The goal with compatible numbers is to find the nearest "friendly" problem that you can compute instantly.

---

## Estimating Sums and Differences

Round each number to the same place, then calculate.

**Example:** 786 + 449
- Round to nearest hundred: 800 + 400 = **1,200**
- Exact: 1,235

**Example:** 9,241 - 4,887
- Round to nearest thousand: 9,000 - 5,000 = **4,000**
- Exact: 4,354

**When to round to a smaller place:** If precision matters more, round to tens:
- 786 + 449 → 790 + 450 = **1,240** (closer to exact 1,235)

---

## Estimating Products

Round each factor to one significant digit (or a convenient number), then multiply.

**Example:** 38 × 61
- 40 × 60 = **2,400**
- Exact: 2,318

**Example:** 6.8 × 4.2
- 7 × 4 = **28**
- Exact: 28.56 — the estimate is excellent here.

**Example:** 312 × 48
- 300 × 50 = **15,000**
- Exact: 14,976 — very close.

---

## Estimating Quotients

Replace with compatible numbers that divide cleanly.

**Example:** 743 ÷ 8
- 740 ÷ 8 is awkward. Use 720 ÷ 8 = **90** or 800 ÷ 8 = 100.
- Exact: 92.875. The estimate of 90 is excellent.

**Example:** 4,612 ÷ 53
- Replace with 4,500 ÷ 50 = **90** (compatible: 4,500 ÷ 50 is clean)
- Or 5,000 ÷ 50 = 100.
- Exact: 87. Estimate of 90 is close.

---

## Clustering

When several numbers are close to the same value, estimate each as that value.

**Example:** 318 + 295 + 304 + 287
- All numbers cluster around 300.
- Four numbers × 300 = **1,200**
- Exact: 1,204

---

## Overestimating vs. Underestimating

In real-world situations, the direction of your error matters.

**Overestimate when:** You're managing risk or cost.
- Buying materials: estimate high so you don't run short.
- Quoting a project timeline: estimate high to ensure on-time delivery.

**Underestimate when:** You're checking a lower bound.
- Can this truck carry the load? Estimate the load low — if even the low estimate is too high, you have a problem.

Knowing which way to err is a professional judgment call, not a math problem.

---

## Estimation as a Error-Check

Always estimate before doing a complex calculation. If your exact answer differs significantly from the estimate, recheck.

**Example:** You calculate 89 × 47 = 6,383.
- Estimate: 90 × 50 = 4,500.
- 6,383 is way off from 4,500. Recheck. (89 × 47 is actually 4,183.)

The estimate caught a calculation error immediately. This habit prevents costly mistakes in any numeric work.
