## The Coordinate Plane

The coordinate plane is a grid that lets you locate any point in two-dimensional space using two numbers. It connects algebra — which works with equations and numbers — to geometry, which works with shapes and positions. This connection, known as analytic geometry, is one of the most powerful ideas in mathematics. Every graph you've ever seen — stock charts, weather maps, data plots — is built on this framework.

---

## The Structure of the Coordinate Plane

The coordinate plane has two perpendicular number lines:
- The **x-axis** runs horizontally (left and right)
- The **y-axis** runs vertically (up and down)
- They intersect at the **origin**, which is the point (0, 0)

The x-axis has positive values to the right of the origin and negative values to the left. The y-axis has positive values above the origin and negative values below.

---

## Coordinates: The Address System

Every point on the coordinate plane has an address called an **ordered pair**: (x, y).

- The first number is the **x-coordinate** (horizontal position): how far left or right from the origin.
- The second number is the **y-coordinate** (vertical position): how far up or down from the origin.

The order matters — (3, 5) and (5, 3) are different points.

**Examples:**
- (4, 2): move 4 units right, 2 units up
- (-3, 1): move 3 units left, 1 unit up
- (0, -5): stay at x = 0, move 5 units down
- (-2, -4): move 2 units left, 4 units down

---

## The Four Quadrants

The two axes divide the plane into four **quadrants**, numbered counterclockwise from the upper right:

| Quadrant | x-sign | y-sign | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| I (upper right) | + | + | (3, 4) |
| II (upper left) | - | + | (-3, 4) |
| III (lower left) | - | - | (-3, -4) |
| IV (lower right) | + | - | (3, -4) |

Points on the axes are not in any quadrant — they lie on the boundary.

---

## Plotting Points

To plot the point (x, y):
1. Start at the origin (0, 0).
2. Move horizontally: right if x is positive, left if x is negative.
3. From there, move vertically: up if y is positive, down if y is negative.
4. Mark the point.

**Practice plotting these points:** A(2, 5), B(-4, 3), C(-1, -2), D(0, 4), E(3, -3).

---

## Reading Points from a Graph

To read a point already plotted:
1. Trace straight down (or up) to the x-axis to find the x-coordinate.
2. Trace straight left (or right) to the y-axis to find the y-coordinate.
3. Write as (x, y).

---

## Distance Between Points

**Horizontal distance:** If two points share the same y-coordinate, the distance between them is |x₂ - x₁|. Example: (2, 4) and (7, 4) → distance = |7 - 2| = 5.

**Vertical distance:** If two points share the same x-coordinate, the distance is |y₂ - y₁|. Example: (3, -1) and (3, 5) → distance = |5 - (-1)| = 6.

**General distance** (any two points): Use the distance formula, which is an application of the Pythagorean theorem:
```
distance = √((x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²)
```

Example: Distance from (1, 2) to (4, 6):
- Horizontal change: 4 - 1 = 3
- Vertical change: 6 - 2 = 4
- Distance = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = **5**

---

## The Midpoint

The **midpoint** between two points is the point exactly halfway between them. Average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates:

```
Midpoint = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)
```

**Example:** Midpoint of (2, 4) and (8, 10):
- x: (2 + 8)/2 = 5
- y: (4 + 10)/2 = 7
- Midpoint: **(5, 7)**

---

## Graphing Relationships

The coordinate plane becomes powerful when you use it to graph relationships between variables.

**Example:** The equation y = 2x. For each value of x, calculate y.

| x | y = 2x | Point |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| -2 | -4 | (-2, -4) |
| 0 | 0 | (0, 0) |
| 1 | 2 | (1, 2) |
| 3 | 6 | (3, 6) |

Plot these points and connect them — you get a straight line through the origin. Every point on that line satisfies the equation y = 2x. This is the fundamental idea behind graphing: the graph of an equation is the set of all points (x, y) that make the equation true.

---

## Real-World Applications

**Maps:** Every GPS coordinate system is a coordinate plane (latitude and longitude are the two axes).

**Business:** A line graph of monthly revenue plots time (x-axis) against dollars (y-axis). Reading the point (6, 24000) means month 6 had $24,000 in revenue.

**Science:** Graphs of experimental data — temperature vs. time, force vs. distance — are all coordinate plane plots where reading a point gives you two related measurements.

**Navigation:** If you're 3 miles east and 4 miles north of a starting point, you're at coordinates (3, 4). Your actual distance from the start is √(3² + 4²) = 5 miles.
