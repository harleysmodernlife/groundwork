Not all sources of information are equal. A peer-reviewed journal article, a press release from an advocacy organization, and a viral social media post are not equivalent — even if they make similar claims. Evaluating sources means assessing their credibility, accuracy, purpose, and context before accepting or sharing what they say.

---

## 1. Why Source Evaluation Matters

Anyone can publish anything online. The old gatekeeping functions — editors, publishers, peer reviewers — have been bypassed. This is democratizing in some ways and chaotic in others. With lower barriers to publication, the burden of quality assessment shifts from institutions to individual readers.

Source evaluation is not about dismissing information from unfamiliar sources — important truths have come from non-mainstream outlets. It's about applying appropriate skepticism proportional to what's at stake.

---

## 2. The SIFT Method

SIFT is a practical four-step approach to evaluating online information, developed by Mike Caulfield:

**S — Stop**
Before reacting or sharing, pause. Ask: Am I about to share something based solely on a headline? Do I actually know this is accurate?

**I — Investigate the Source**
Who is behind this? What's their track record? What's their purpose? This doesn't require deep research — a quick search often reveals whether a source is credible or problematic.

**F — Find Better Coverage**
Is this claim covered by other credible sources? If a significant event occurred, major news organizations will cover it. If you can only find the claim in one place, be suspicious.

**T — Trace Claims, Quotes, and Media**
Claims often originate somewhere else. Find the original source of a statistic, quote, or image to see if it's being accurately represented.

---

## 3. Questions to Ask About Any Source

**Who created this?**
- Is there a named author with verifiable credentials?
- What organization published it?
- Can you find information about this organization (About page, contact information, leadership)?

**What is their purpose?**
- To inform? Persuade? Sell? Entertain? Fundraise?
- Is there a clear agenda or conflict of interest?
- Is it a nonprofit? A corporation? A government agency?

**What are the evidence standards?**
- Are claims supported with sources you can check?
- Are methods explained (for research)?
- Are there corrections when errors occur?

**What do others say about this source?**
- Is it cited or linked to by other credible sources?
- Do fact-checkers treat it as reliable?
- Is it listed in journalism databases or journalism school resources?

---

## 4. Types of Sources and Their Typical Credibility

**Peer-reviewed academic journals**: Highest evidence standard for empirical claims; reviewed by independent experts before publication. Can still be wrong; findings can be later revised. Best for scientific and social science claims.

**Major news organizations with editorial standards**: AP, Reuters, BBC, New York Times, Washington Post, NPR, and others have reporters, editors, and correction policies. They can make errors and have biases, but they have professional accountability.

**Government sources**: Official data from government agencies (Census Bureau, CDC, Bureau of Labor Statistics) is generally reliable for factual data — though the interpretation of that data can be contested. Be aware of politically influenced agencies.

**Think tanks and research organizations**: Quality varies widely. Check their funding, their methodology, and whether their findings are peer-reviewed or just internally produced. Many think tanks advocate for specific policy positions.

**Advocacy organizations**: Transparent advocates for a cause. Their claims should be fact-checked against independent sources; their data may be selectively presented.

**Social media posts**: No editorial standards; anyone can claim anything. Even posts from public figures are not inherently reliable.

**AI-generated content**: Should not be treated as a primary source. AI systems produce plausible text that may be fabricated.

---

## 5. Lateral Reading

**Lateral reading** is the practice used by professional fact-checkers: rather than evaluating a source by reading deeply within it (what it says about itself), open multiple new tabs and read about the source from other sources.

Most people evaluate sources by reading more of them — which doesn't help if the source itself is designed to seem credible while being misleading. Lateral reading gets you outside the source's own framing immediately.

---

## 6. Primary vs. Secondary Sources

**Primary sources** are original, firsthand materials: research data, official documents, transcripts, legislation, original studies. They are what secondary sources interpret.

**Secondary sources** interpret or summarize primary sources: news articles about a study, textbooks, review articles.

When possible, trace claims back to the primary source. A newspaper article about a study may accurately reflect the study's findings — or it may mischaracterize them. Checking the original is the only way to be certain.

---

## Summary

Source evaluation requires asking who created the content, for what purpose, with what evidence, and what others say about the source. The SIFT method (Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims) provides a practical framework. Lateral reading — looking at a source from outside it — is more effective than reading deeply within it. Primary sources are generally more reliable than secondary accounts of them. Calibrated skepticism — applied proportionally to the claims being made and the stakes involved — is the goal.
