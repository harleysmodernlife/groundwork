A logical fallacy is an error in reasoning — an argument that feels persuasive or is presented as valid, but contains a flaw in its logic. Recognizing fallacies helps you evaluate arguments more carefully, avoid being misled, and make stronger arguments yourself.

---

## 1. Why Fallacies Matter

Fallacies are common in political speech, advertising, social media, and everyday argument. They persist because they often work — they activate emotions, exploit cognitive shortcuts, and sound like reasoning even when they aren't.

Knowing the names of fallacies isn't the goal. The goal is recognizing when an argument fails to prove what it claims to prove — and being able to explain why.

---

## 2. Fallacies of Relevance

These arguments offer premises that are irrelevant to the conclusion.

### Ad Hominem (Attack the Person)
Attacking the person making the argument instead of the argument itself.

*"You can't trust his research on climate change — he drives a gas-powered truck."*

Whether the researcher drives a gas truck is irrelevant to whether the research is sound. Even hypocrites can be right.

### Appeal to Authority
Using an authority's opinion as if it settled the matter, without considering whether the authority is actually expert in the relevant area.

*"A Nobel Prize-winning physicist says vaccines cause autism."* — Physics expertise doesn't confer expertise in immunology.

Note: Citing genuine experts in their field is not a fallacy. The fallacy is treating authority as a replacement for evidence.

### Appeal to Popularity (Ad Populum)
Arguing that something is true because many people believe it.

*"Millions of people believe in homeopathy — there must be something to it."*

Popular belief doesn't establish truth.

### Appeal to Emotion
Substituting emotional manipulation for reasoned argument.

*[Sad music plays while photos of suffering animals appear] "How can you not support our legislation?"*

Emotional appeals can be appropriate — they can illustrate why something matters. The fallacy is when emotion replaces argument rather than supporting it.

---

## 3. Fallacies of Presumption

These arguments rest on unwarranted assumptions.

### Straw Man
Misrepresenting an opponent's position to make it easier to attack.

*"My opponent wants to reduce military spending. Clearly she wants to leave America defenseless."*

The speaker replaced the actual position (modest reduction) with an extreme version (defenselessness) they didn't argue for.

### False Dichotomy (Either/Or Fallacy)
Presenting only two options when more exist.

*"You're either with us or against us."*
*"If you don't support this policy, you must not care about children."*

Most issues have more than two positions.

### Slippery Slope
Arguing that one step will inevitably lead to extreme consequences, without showing why.

*"If we allow same-sex marriage, next people will want to marry their pets."*

The chain of causation is assumed, not argued.

### Begging the Question (Circular Reasoning)
The conclusion is assumed in the premise.

*"The Bible is true because it says so in the Bible."*
*"We can trust him because he's trustworthy."*

The argument goes in circles rather than providing evidence.

---

## 4. Fallacies of Insufficient Evidence

### Hasty Generalization
Drawing a broad conclusion from too few examples.

*"My neighbor's electric car broke down twice. EVs are unreliable."*

One or two examples are not sufficient to generalize to all members of a category.

### Post Hoc (False Cause)
Assuming that because one thing followed another, the first caused the second.

*"Crime rates fell after the new mayor took office — the mayor reduced crime."*

Correlation is not causation. Many factors affect crime rates simultaneously.

### Appeal to Ignorance
Arguing that something is true because it hasn't been proven false (or false because it hasn't been proven true).

*"No one has proved ghosts don't exist, so they must."*

Absence of proof is not proof of absence — but it's also not proof of presence.

---

## 5. Fallacies of Ambiguity

### Equivocation
Using the same word in two different senses within an argument.

*"Laws of nature never fail. Human laws sometimes fail. Therefore, human laws aren't real laws."*

"Laws" means something different in each sentence.

### False Equivalence
Treating two things as equivalent when they aren't.

*"Both sides have extreme views."* (when one side's "extreme" involves minor variations from the mainstream and the other's involves documented violence)

---

## 6. Spotting Fallacies in Practice

When evaluating an argument, ask:
- Are the premises actually relevant to the conclusion?
- Does the conclusion follow from the premises, or does it require a leap?
- Is the evidence sufficient, or is it a single anecdote?
- Are more than two options being ignored?
- Is the opposing view being accurately represented?
- Is correlation being presented as causation?

You don't need to name the fallacy to identify that an argument fails. The point is the question: *Does the evidence actually support this conclusion?*

---

## Summary

Logical fallacies are errors in reasoning that make arguments seem more persuasive than their logic warrants. Key types include ad hominem (attacking the person), straw man (misrepresenting the opponent), false dichotomy (ignoring alternatives), slippery slope (assuming a chain of consequences), post hoc (confusing correlation with causation), and hasty generalization (drawing broad conclusions from limited evidence). Recognizing these patterns supports more careful evaluation of political speech, media, and everyday argument.
