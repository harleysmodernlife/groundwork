World War I (1914–1918) killed approximately 20 million people and reshaped the global order. The United States entered late (1917) but played a decisive role in the outcome. The war's aftermath — the Treaty of Versailles and the failure of Wilson's idealistic peace program — planted the seeds of the next world war.

---

## 1. Origins

The war's immediate trigger was the assassination of Austro-Hungarian Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist.

Beneath this spark lay decades of structural tensions:
- **Alliance systems**: Europe was divided into two armed blocs — the Triple Entente (France, Britain, Russia) and the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy)
- **Imperial rivalry**: Competition for colonies and trade
- **Nationalism**: Ethnic groups sought independence from multinational empires; national rivalries were intense
- **Militarism**: European powers had built massive armies; military planning emphasized speed (Germany's Schlieffen Plan)

The alliance system meant a local conflict rapidly became a continental war.

---

## 2. The Western Front

Germany's plan required quickly defeating France through Belgium before turning to fight Russia in the east. It failed: the invasion of Belgium brought Britain into the war; France and Britain held; Germany was trapped in a two-front war.

By late 1914, the Western Front had settled into **trench warfare** — a line of opposing trenches stretching 450 miles from Belgium to Switzerland. For years, the lines moved little while casualties mounted in the millions.

Major battles: Verdun (700,000+ casualties in 10 months), the Somme (over 1 million casualties in 5 months). Industrial-scale weapons — machine guns, artillery, poison gas, early tanks, aircraft — made attack suicidal and defense dominant.

---

## 3. American Neutrality and Entry

The United States initially declared neutrality. Public opinion was divided — many immigrants sympathized with their home countries on both sides.

Factors pushing America toward the Allies:
- **Cultural ties** to Britain and France
- **Economic ties**: American banks and businesses were financing the Allied war effort; trade heavily favored the Allies
- **German submarine warfare**: Germany blockaded Britain by sinking ships. The sinking of the *Lusitania* (1915), a British liner that killed 1,198 including 128 Americans, outraged the public

**The Zimmermann Telegram (1917)**: Germany's foreign minister sent a telegram to Mexico proposing an alliance — Mexico would help Germany in exchange for help recovering Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. British intelligence intercepted and published it. American outrage was intense.

When Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare in early 1917, Wilson asked Congress for a declaration of war. Congress voted 82–6 in the Senate and 373–50 in the House.

---

## 4. American Contribution

American forces arrived gradually — by mid-1918, about 2 million American soldiers were in France. Fresh troops and resources helped tip the balance after Germany's final major offensive failed in spring 1918.

The home front: women entered the workforce in large numbers; the government mobilized the economy through new agencies; the Espionage Act (1917) and Sedition Act (1918) criminalized anti-war speech.

**African Americans**: About 400,000 Black Americans served in a segregated military. The "Great Migration" of Black Americans from the South to Northern cities accelerated, driven partly by war employment — and was met with racial violence in many Northern cities.

---

## 5. Wilson's Fourteen Points and the Peace

Wilson proposed a peace based on his **Fourteen Points**: self-determination for European peoples, open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, and a **League of Nations** to resolve future disputes peacefully.

The **Treaty of Versailles (1919)** was a compromise that satisfied few. The Allied powers stripped Germany of territory, colonies, and military capacity; imposed heavy reparations; and assigned sole "war guilt" to Germany. Wilson secured the League of Nations but had to sacrifice much of the Fourteen Points.

The U.S. Senate refused to ratify the treaty. **Irreconcilables** (who opposed any entanglement) and **Reservationists** (who wanted conditions on the League) combined to block ratification. The U.S. never joined the League; it limped along without American participation and proved unable to prevent the next war.

---

## 6. Consequences

- Four empires collapsed: Ottoman, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and German
- New nations were created from their wreckage, often with borders that ignored ethnic realities
- The Treaty of Versailles humiliated Germany, fueling nationalist resentment that Hitler would exploit
- The Russian Revolution (1917) produced the Soviet Union
- The Middle East was divided by Britain and France through the Sykes-Picot Agreement, shaping the region's conflicts for a century
- The Spanish flu pandemic (1918–1919) killed 50+ million worldwide, partly spread by troop movements

---

## Summary

World War I grew from the collision of alliance systems, nationalism, and imperial competition. The Western Front produced unprecedented industrial slaughter. America entered in 1917, providing the resources and fresh troops that enabled Allied victory. Wilson's peace vision — self-determination and a League of Nations — was partially realized at Versailles but fatally compromised by Allied punitive demands and rejected by the U.S. Senate. The war's unresolved legacy — a humiliated Germany, unstable new states, collapsed empires — made World War II all but inevitable.
