The Cold War (1947–1991) was the defining geopolitical conflict of the second half of the 20th century — a prolonged competition between the United States and the Soviet Union that shaped international relations, domestic politics, and the lives of hundreds of millions of people worldwide. It was called "cold" because the two superpowers, both armed with nuclear weapons, never directly fought each other — but the conflict was intensely hot in proxy wars, arms races, and ideological competition.

---

## 1. Origins

The wartime alliance between the U.S. and Soviet Union was always uneasy. By 1945, profound differences emerged:

- The Soviets had suffered 27 million deaths and were determined to control neighboring states as a buffer against future invasion; they installed communist governments in Eastern Europe
- The Americans believed Soviet expansion threatened Western Europe, democracy, and free markets
- Both sides had nuclear weapons (the Soviets tested their first atomic bomb in 1949); both knew direct war meant catastrophe

---

## 2. The Truman Doctrine and Containment

President Truman articulated the American strategy in 1947: the U.S. would support "free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation" — meaning it would provide economic and military aid to resist Soviet influence wherever it appeared.

**Containment** (associated with diplomat George Kennan) argued that the Soviet system, if prevented from expanding, would eventually collapse from its own internal contradictions.

**The Marshall Plan (1947)**: $13 billion to rebuild Western European economies — preventing the economic desperation that might drive populations toward communism.

**NATO (1949)**: The North Atlantic Treaty Organization committed the U.S. and Western European nations to collective defense — an attack on one was an attack on all. The Soviets responded with the Warsaw Pact (1955).

---

## 3. Korea (1950–1953)

When communist North Korea invaded South Korea in 1950, Truman committed American forces under a UN mandate. General Douglas MacArthur's forces nearly conquered all of Korea before China intervened; the front stabilized roughly at the 38th parallel.

The Korean War ended in an armistice (not a peace treaty) in 1953. The two Koreas remain technically at war; U.S. troops remain in South Korea today.

---

## 4. The Nuclear Arms Race

Both superpowers developed hydrogen bombs, intercontinental ballistic missiles, and submarine-launched missiles — eventually capable of destroying civilization multiple times over. **MAD** (Mutually Assured Destruction) was the doctrine that kept them from using these weapons: any attack would result in the complete destruction of both sides.

The arms race produced:
- **Duck and cover** drills in American schools
- Nuclear bunker construction
- The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962): The closest the Cold War came to nuclear war. The Soviet Union placed nuclear missiles in Cuba; Kennedy imposed a naval blockade; thirteen days of intense crisis ended with Soviet withdrawal in exchange for American removal of missiles from Turkey

---

## 5. Vietnam

The most consequential American proxy war. Vietnam had been divided in 1954 between communist North Vietnam and the U.S.-backed South. American involvement escalated under Eisenhower (advisors), Kennedy (more advisors), and Johnson (combat troops after the Gulf of Tonkin incident in 1964).

At peak involvement, 500,000+ American troops were in Vietnam. The war killed approximately 58,000 Americans and 2–3 million Vietnamese. Despite massive military superiority, the U.S. could not defeat the North Vietnamese and Viet Cong.

The war divided American society deeply. Anti-war protests grew; the **Tet Offensive** (1968) shattered the government's credibility about progress. Nixon pursued "Vietnamization" — handing the war to South Vietnamese forces — and eventually withdrew. South Vietnam fell in 1975.

---

## 6. Civil Rights and the Cold War

American racial segregation was a Cold War liability. Soviet propaganda highlighted American racism to undermine U.S. claims to champion freedom. The State Department recognized that Jim Crow damaged America's global standing — one of the Cold War pressures that supported civil rights reform.

---

## 7. Détente and the Late Cold War

Nixon's **détente** policy in the early 1970s reduced tensions: he opened diplomatic relations with China (1972), signed arms control agreements with the Soviets (SALT I), and pursued a less confrontational approach.

Carter's human rights emphasis, Reagan's aggressive anti-communist rhetoric ("evil empire"), and a renewed arms race marked the late Cold War.

**Gorbachev's reforms** (1985–1991): Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev introduced *glasnost* (openness) and *perestroika* (restructuring) to revitalize the Soviet system. Instead, reforms unleashed forces he couldn't control. Eastern European communist governments collapsed in 1989; the Berlin Wall fell; the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991.

---

## Summary

The Cold War structured global politics for four decades around the competition between American liberal capitalism and Soviet communism. Containment strategy, the nuclear arms race, proxy wars (Korea, Vietnam), and ideological competition defined the era. The Cold War ended not through military victory but through the Soviet system's internal collapse under the pressures of economic stagnation and political reform. Its legacy — including nuclear arsenals, military alliances, and geopolitical commitments — continues to shape the world.
