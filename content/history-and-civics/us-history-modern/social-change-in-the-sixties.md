The 1960s were a decade of profound social transformation in the United States. Beyond the Civil Rights Movement, this era saw the rise of feminism, the anti-war movement, counterculture, and other challenges to established norms and institutions. The decade's upheavals reshaped American culture, politics, and law in ways that are still contested.

---

## 1. The Context: Postwar America

The 1950s have a reputation for conformity and prosperity — but this is partly myth. McCarthyism's anti-communist crusade enforced political conformity; suburban development and consumer culture spread; women were pressured back into domestic roles after wartime employment; racial segregation was enforced everywhere.

Beneath this surface, tensions were building. The Baby Boom generation — 76 million born between 1946 and 1964 — came of age in the 1960s. They had grown up in relative prosperity, were attending college in unprecedented numbers, and were shaped by the Civil Rights Movement's moral clarity.

---

## 2. The Women's Movement (Second Wave Feminism)

Women had won the vote in 1920 but continued to face severe legal and economic discrimination: limited educational and professional opportunities, legal subordination in marriage, no workplace protections against harassment or discrimination.

**Betty Friedan's *The Feminine Mystique* (1963)** named "the problem that has no name" — the widespread dissatisfaction of educated women confined to domestic roles. It launched a new feminist wave.

**The National Organization for Women (NOW, 1966)** organized to achieve legal equality. Key achievements:

- **Equal Pay Act (1963)**: Required equal pay for equal work (enforcement was weak)
- **Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964)**: Prohibited employment discrimination based on sex
- **Title IX (1972)**: Prohibited sex discrimination in educational programs receiving federal funds — transforming women's athletics and academic access

**Reproductive rights**: The Supreme Court's *Griswold v. Connecticut* (1965) established a constitutional right to contraception; *Roe v. Wade* (1973) recognized a right to abortion. Both decisions were deeply contested.

**The Equal Rights Amendment (ERA)**: Passed Congress in 1972; needed 38 states for ratification. Fell three states short by the ratification deadline (1982), due partly to organized conservative opposition led by Phyllis Schlafly.

---

## 3. The Anti-War Movement

American involvement in Vietnam escalated dramatically after 1965. The draft — which sent mostly working-class young men to fight while college students could obtain deferments — generated intense opposition.

Anti-war protests grew from small marches to massive demonstrations:
- **March on the Pentagon (1967)**: 100,000 protesters
- **1968 Democratic National Convention (Chicago)**: Violent clashes between police and protesters outside the convention where Hubert Humphrey was nominated, broadcast nationally
- **Kent State (1970)**: National Guard troops shot and killed four student protesters at Kent State University, sparking a national student strike

The movement included Vietnam veterans — **Vietnam Veterans Against the War** brought particular moral authority. John Kerry's congressional testimony (1971) was nationally televised.

Growing public opposition to the war, combined with the enormous casualties and evidence that the government had misled the public (the **Pentagon Papers**, published 1971, revealed systematic deception), eroded the war's political support.

---

## 4. Youth Culture and the Counterculture

A segment of young people rejected mainstream culture entirely. The **counterculture** emphasized:
- Communal living and rejection of materialism
- Sexual freedom ("free love")
- Psychedelic drugs (LSD, marijuana)
- Rock and roll music as cultural expression and political statement (Bob Dylan, the Beatles, Jimi Hendrix, Janis Joplin)
- **Woodstock (1969)**: 400,000 gathered for a music festival; became a symbol of the era

The counterculture's political dimensions overlapped with the anti-war movement and challenged authority broadly — in dress, music, sexual norms, and relationship to institutions.

---

## 5. Other Movements

The civil rights model inspired other groups:

**American Indian Movement (AIM, 1968)**: Organized to address Indigenous poverty, police brutality, and treaty violations. Occupied Alcatraz Island (1969–1971) and Wounded Knee (1973).

**Chicano Movement**: Mexican American activists organized for labor rights (Cesar Chavez and the United Farm Workers), educational equity, and political representation.

**LGBTQ+ Rights**: The **Stonewall riots (1969)** — when patrons of a New York gay bar resisted police harassment — marked the start of the modern LGBTQ+ rights movement. The Gay Liberation Front and other organizations formed immediately.

**Environmental movement**: The first **Earth Day** (April 22, 1970) drew 20 million Americans. The environmental movement led to the Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, and formation of the EPA — all in 1970.

---

## 6. The Backlash

The 1960s also produced a powerful conservative backlash. Nixon's appeal to the "silent majority" — Americans who felt threatened by social upheaval — won the 1968 election. The backlash against feminism, civil rights, and counterculture formed the basis for a resurgent conservative movement that would define the 1970s and beyond.

---

## Summary

The 1960s saw intersecting social movements — civil rights, feminism, anti-war activism, counterculture, and Indigenous and LGBTQ+ rights movements — reshape American culture and law. These movements achieved significant legal victories (Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act, Title IX, environmental legislation) while generating intense cultural conflict. The decade's upheavals produced both lasting progressive change and a durable conservative backlash, setting the terms of American cultural and political conflict for decades.
