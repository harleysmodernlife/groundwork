The Civil Rights Movement was one of the most transformative social movements in American history. Between the mid-1950s and mid-1960s, Black Americans and their allies challenged the legal apparatus of white supremacy through nonviolent protest, legal action, and political organizing — winning landmark legislative victories that dismantled official segregation and expanded voting rights.

---

## 1. The World the Movement Confronted

After Reconstruction, the South had constructed a system of racial oppression backed by law and violence:

- **Jim Crow laws**: Legal segregation in schools, transportation, restaurants, hotels, hospitals, parks, and virtually every area of public life
- **Disenfranchisement**: Poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, and violence that effectively stripped Black voters of the rights won in the 15th Amendment
- **Economic subordination**: Black Southerners were confined largely to sharecropping and low-wage labor, with limited economic mobility
- **Violence**: Lynching, riots, and the constant threat of terror maintained white supremacy. The NAACP recorded over 4,700 lynchings between 1882 and 1968.

In the North, de facto segregation in housing (through redlining and restrictive covenants), employment discrimination, and police brutality produced different but still severe inequality.

---

## 2. Seeds of the Movement

Several postwar developments created conditions for the movement:

- **The Great Migration**: Millions of Black Americans moved North, building political power in cities
- **World War II**: Black veterans who had fought fascism abroad refused to accept second-class status at home; the "Double V" campaign articulated this demand
- **NAACP legal strategy**: The NAACP Legal Defense Fund, led by Thurgood Marshall, systematically challenged segregation in court throughout the 1940s and 50s

**Brown v. Board of Education (1954)**: The Supreme Court unanimously ruled that racial segregation in public schools was unconstitutional — overturning *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896). Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal."

---

## 3. Montgomery and the Rise of King

In December 1955, Rosa Parks was arrested for refusing to give her seat to a white passenger on a Montgomery, Alabama bus. Black community leaders organized a **bus boycott** that lasted 381 days — nearly a year. Black residents walked miles to work rather than ride segregated buses.

The boycott brought national attention to a young Baptist minister: **Martin Luther King Jr.**, who became the movement's most visible leader. King articulated a philosophy of **nonviolent direct action** rooted in Gandhi's methods and the Christian tradition — confronting opponents with moral force, accepting suffering, and appealing to the conscience of the nation.

The Supreme Court ruled Montgomery's bus segregation unconstitutional in 1956.

---

## 4. Sit-ins, Freedom Rides, and SNCC

In February 1960, four Black college students sat at a segregated Woolworth's lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, and refused to leave when denied service. The sit-in movement spread rapidly across the South. Young activists founded the **Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC)** to organize youth-led direct action.

**Freedom Riders (1961)**: Interracial groups rode interstate buses through the South, challenging segregated facilities. They were met with savage mob violence — buses firebombed, riders beaten. The Kennedy administration was pressured to enforce desegregation.

---

## 5. Birmingham and the March on Washington

In 1963, King and the **Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC)** targeted Birmingham, Alabama — one of the most segregated cities in America. Police Commissioner Bull Connor responded to peaceful demonstrations with fire hoses and attack dogs. The images, broadcast nationally, shocked the country.

**The March on Washington (August 28, 1963)**: 250,000 people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial. King delivered his "I Have a Dream" speech — articulating a vision of racial equality and national redemption. It is one of the most celebrated speeches in American history.

---

## 6. Legislative Victories

**Civil Rights Act of 1964**: Prohibited discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in employment and public accommodations. Banned segregation in hotels, restaurants, theaters, and other public places.

**Voting Rights Act of 1965**: Prohibited discriminatory voting practices and authorized federal oversight of elections in jurisdictions with histories of discrimination. Black voter registration across the South surged.

The **Selma to Montgomery marches** (1965) — particularly "Bloody Sunday," when marchers were brutally beaten by police on the Edmund Pettus Bridge — directly catalyzed the Voting Rights Act.

---

## 7. The Movement's Internal Tensions and Later Phase

As legal victories accumulated, the movement confronted deeper inequalities: economic inequality, Northern urban segregation, and police brutality.

**Malcolm X** and Black nationalist movements offered a different analysis — emphasizing Black pride, self-determination, and skepticism of integration. The **Black Power** movement of the late 1960s reflected this.

King's assassination on April 4, 1968, prompted riots in cities across the country. The Fair Housing Act passed within a week.

---

## Summary

The Civil Rights Movement confronted a system of racial oppression sustained by law and violence through nonviolent direct action, legal challenges, and political organizing. Brown v. Board, Montgomery, the sit-ins, Freedom Rides, Birmingham, and the March on Washington built to the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965 — legislative dismantling of official segregation. The movement revealed that legal equality, while essential, was insufficient to address the full dimensions of racial inequality — debates that continue today.
