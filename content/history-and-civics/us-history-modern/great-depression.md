The Great Depression was the worst economic catastrophe in American history. Between 1929 and 1933, the economy contracted by a third, unemployment reached 25%, thousands of banks failed, and millions of Americans were plunged into poverty. The government's response — Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal — reshaped the relationship between Americans and their federal government.

---

## 1. The Roaring Twenties and the Crash

The 1920s were a period of economic expansion, technological change, and cultural transformation. Automobile ownership, radio, movies, and consumer goods spread broadly. Credit was easy; stock market speculation was rampant.

The agricultural sector hadn't shared in the prosperity — farmers had suffered from falling prices throughout the decade. And beneath the surface of urban prosperity were structural weaknesses: over-leveraged financial institutions, over-production in some industries, and extreme inequality.

**The Stock Market Crash (October 1929)**: The market had been rising speculative bubble — stocks valued far beyond companies' actual worth. On "Black Tuesday" (October 29, 1929), the market collapsed. Billions of dollars of value vanished in days.

---

## 2. The Spiral

The crash triggered a devastating chain reaction:
- Banks that had invested in stocks failed; depositors lost savings
- Bank failures reduced the money supply, contracting the economy
- Businesses couldn't get credit; they cut production and laid off workers
- Unemployed workers couldn't buy goods; more businesses failed
- Deflation made debt worse — as prices fell, the real value of debts rose
- International trade contracted as countries raised tariffs (the U.S. Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930 worsened the global contraction)

By 1933: unemployment at 25%; GDP down 30%; 9,000 banks had failed; hundreds of thousands were homeless, living in "Hoovervilles" (shantytowns named mockingly after President Herbert Hoover).

---

## 3. Hoover's Response

President Herbert Hoover (1929–1933) believed government should not provide direct relief — doing so would undermine self-reliance. He favored voluntary action and indirect support (loans to banks and businesses). 

Hoover's approach was inadequate to the scale of the crisis. The **Reconstruction Finance Corporation** (1932) lent money to banks and businesses, but with too little effect too late. When WWI veterans marched on Washington demanding early payment of promised bonuses (the "Bonus Army"), Hoover had troops disperse them violently — an image that crystallized his failure.

Hoover lost to Franklin Roosevelt in a landslide in 1932.

---

## 4. The New Deal

Roosevelt promised a "New Deal" for Americans and delivered a wave of legislation in his first hundred days. The New Deal had three broad aims: **relief** (help people now), **recovery** (restart the economy), and **reform** (prevent future crises).

**Banking and Finance**:
- **Emergency Banking Act (1933)**: Closed banks for inspection; reopened sound ones. Roosevelt's first "fireside chat" on radio explained it clearly; Americans trusted it.
- **Glass-Steagall Act (1933)**: Separated commercial and investment banking; created the **FDIC** (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation) to insure deposits
- **SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission, 1934)**: Regulated the stock market

**Relief and Employment**:
- **Federal Emergency Relief Administration**: Direct grants to states for relief
- **Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC)**: Employed young men in conservation projects
- **Works Progress Administration (WPA)**: Employed millions building public works — roads, bridges, schools — and employed artists, writers, and musicians

**Agricultural Recovery**:
- **Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA)**: Paid farmers to reduce production to raise prices

**Long-term Reform**:
- **Social Security Act (1935)**: Created retirement insurance, unemployment insurance, and aid for dependent children — the foundation of the modern American welfare state
- **National Labor Relations Act (1935)**: Guaranteed workers' right to organize and bargain collectively; created the NLRB

---

## 5. The New Deal's Limits

The New Deal helped but did not end the Depression. Unemployment remained above 14% in 1940. What finally ended the Depression was World War II mobilization.

The New Deal also had profound racial failures:
- Agricultural programs often excluded Black tenant farmers and sharecroppers
- Social Security initially excluded domestic workers and agricultural laborers (disproportionately Black)
- FDR refused to support anti-lynching legislation for fear of alienating Southern Democrats

Despite this, Roosevelt won overwhelming Black support because the New Deal programs, however imperfectly distributed, provided more relief to Black Americans than they had received before.

---

## 6. The New Deal's Legacy

The New Deal permanently changed the federal government's role. Before the Depression, the prevailing view was that government should not intervene in the economy beyond basic functions. After the New Deal:
- The federal government accepted responsibility for unemployment and economic stability
- A social safety net (Social Security, unemployment insurance) was established
- Financial regulation became accepted
- Labor unions gained legal protection and grew dramatically

The New Deal coalition — an unlikely alliance of Southern whites, urban machine politicians, labor unions, Black voters, and progressive reformers — dominated American politics for a generation.

---

## Summary

The Great Depression followed the stock market crash of 1929 and spiraled into the worst economic crisis in American history through bank failures, deflation, and collapsing trade. Hoover's inadequate response discredited the Republican establishment. Roosevelt's New Deal brought relief, partial recovery, and lasting structural reform — Social Security, banking regulation, labor rights — that permanently expanded the federal government's role in American life. The Depression ended not through New Deal policy alone but through World War II mobilization.
