World War II (1939–1945) was the deadliest conflict in human history — an estimated 70–85 million people died, including approximately 6 million Jews and millions of others murdered in the Holocaust. The United States, initially neutral, was pulled into the war by Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor and emerged from it as the world's dominant military and economic power.

---

## 1. Origins

**In Europe**: World War I's humiliating aftermath left Germany economically devastated, politically unstable, and seething with resentment. Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party exploited this. Hitler rose to power in 1933, dismantled democracy, and began systematic persecution of Jews and other groups. German rearmament, the annexation of Austria and Czechoslovakia, and the invasion of Poland (September 1939) triggered the war. Britain and France declared war on Germany; much of Europe quickly fell to German advances.

**In Asia**: Japan had been pursuing imperial expansion in East Asia since the early 1930s, invading Manchuria (1931) and China (1937). Japanese expansion threatened American interests in the Pacific.

**American neutrality**: Strong public sentiment against involvement kept the U.S. out of the European war initially. The **Lend-Lease Act (1941)** allowed the U.S. to supply Britain and later the Soviet Union with war material without direct military involvement.

---

## 2. Pearl Harbor and American Entry

On December 7, 1941, Japan launched a surprise attack on the U.S. naval base at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii — destroying much of the Pacific fleet and killing over 2,400 Americans. Roosevelt called it "a date which will live in infamy."

Congress declared war on Japan the next day. Germany and Italy declared war on the United States; Congress declared war on them in return. The United States was fully in the war.

---

## 3. The Home Front

American mobilization was massive:
- Industrial production shifted entirely to war needs; GDP doubled
- Unemployment ended — war production employed everyone
- Women entered the workforce in large numbers (symbolized by "Rosie the Riveter")
- War bonds, rationing, and price controls financed and managed the economy

**Japanese American Internment**: In February 1942, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, authorizing the forced removal of 120,000 Japanese Americans (most of them U.S. citizens) from the West Coast to internment camps. This remains one of the most significant civil liberties violations in American history; the Supreme Court upheld it in *Korematsu v. United States* (1944), a decision later repudiated.

**African Americans** served in a segregated military. The "Double V" campaign — victory abroad, victory at home — demanded that fighting fascism abroad required fighting racism at home. The war accelerated the Great Migration and laid groundwork for the postwar civil rights movement.

---

## 4. The War in Europe

**North Africa and Italy (1942–1943)**: Allied forces defeated Germany's Afrika Korps, then invaded and slowly fought up the Italian peninsula.

**The Eastern Front**: Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union (June 1941) was the war's most consequential theater. The Soviets bore enormous losses — approximately 27 million Soviet deaths — but ultimately destroyed much of Germany's military capacity.

**D-Day (June 6, 1944)**: The Allied invasion of Normandy — the largest amphibious assault in history — opened a second front in Western Europe. Allied forces fought across France; Germany was squeezed between East and West.

**Germany's defeat (May 8, 1945)**: Hitler died by suicide as Soviet troops reached Berlin. Germany surrendered unconditionally. **V-E Day** (Victory in Europe) on May 8, 1945.

---

## 5. The Holocaust

As Allied forces advanced into Germany, they discovered the full horror of the **Holocaust** — the systematic murder of 6 million Jews and millions of others (Roma, disabled people, LGBTQ+ people, political opponents, Slavic peoples) by the Nazi regime. The industrialized death camps — Auschwitz, Treblinka, Sobibor, and others — were designed specifically for mass extermination. The Holocaust stands as one of history's most extreme examples of state-organized genocide.

---

## 6. The War in the Pacific

**Island-hopping campaign**: The U.S. "island-hopped" across the Pacific — capturing strategic islands to use as bases for advancing toward Japan.

**Iwo Jima and Okinawa (1945)**: Brutal battles that took enormous casualties on both sides. American planners projected catastrophic casualties from a land invasion of Japan.

**The Atomic Bomb**: The **Manhattan Project** had secretly developed an atomic bomb. On August 6, 1945, an atomic bomb was dropped on Hiroshima; on August 9, on Nagasaki. Approximately 200,000 people were killed, mostly civilians. Japan surrendered on August 15, 1945 — **V-J Day**.

The decision to use the atomic bomb remains controversial: it ended the war without a land invasion, but it killed hundreds of thousands of civilians and inaugurated the nuclear age.

---

## 7. Aftermath

**The United Nations**: Established in 1945 to promote peace and international cooperation; the U.S. was a founding member.

**The Nuremberg Trials**: Nazi leaders were tried for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and crimes against peace — establishing the principle of individual accountability for mass atrocities.

**American dominance**: The U.S. emerged from the war with the world's only intact major economy, a nuclear monopoly, and global military presence. This would define the postwar world order.

---

## Summary

World War II grew from the unresolved aftermath of World War I, Nazi aggression in Europe, and Japanese imperialism in Asia. American entry followed Pearl Harbor. The Allied victory required total mobilization of economies and populations. The Holocaust demonstrated the catastrophic potential of racially motivated state violence. The war ended with nuclear weapons — inaugurating a new era — and with the United States as the undisputed global superpower, setting the stage for the Cold War.
