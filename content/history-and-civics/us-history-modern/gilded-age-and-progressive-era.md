Between the Civil War and World War I, the United States was transformed by industrialization. The Gilded Age brought enormous wealth and devastating inequality; the Progressive Era brought the first systematic effort to use government to address the problems industrialization created.

---

## 1. The Gilded Age (1870s–1900)

The term "Gilded Age" — from Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner's 1873 novel — captured the era's character: glittering on the surface, corrupt underneath.

**Industrialization** produced spectacular growth. By 1900, the U.S. had the world's largest economy. New industries — steel, oil, railroads, finance — created enormous fortunes.

**The Robber Barons**: A small number of men — Andrew Carnegie (steel), John D. Rockefeller (oil), J.P. Morgan (finance), Cornelius Vanderbilt (railroads) — amassed unprecedented wealth through aggressive business practices, including monopolization, price manipulation, and brutal suppression of labor.

**Monopolies and trusts**: Large corporations eliminated competition by buying up competitors or forming trusts — coordinated control across multiple companies. Standard Oil controlled 90% of American oil refining.

**Inequality**: The wealth was concentrated at the top to a degree not seen since. Workers labored for low wages, in dangerous conditions, for 60–70 hour weeks, with no safety net.

**The Gilded Age political culture** was characterized by corruption: politicians were often in the pocket of railroads or other corporate interests. The spoils system — distributing government jobs based on political loyalty — permeated government at every level.

---

## 2. Labor and Immigration

Industrialization was built on labor — both domestic and immigrant.

**Immigration**: Millions of immigrants arrived, primarily from southern and eastern Europe (Italians, Poles, Jews, Greeks) and from East Asia. They provided cheap industrial labor and were often subjected to discrimination, violence, and legal restrictions (the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 was the first major federal law restricting immigration by national origin).

**Labor movement**: Workers organized to demand better wages, shorter hours, and safer conditions. The **Knights of Labor** and later the **American Federation of Labor** (AFL) organized workers. Major strikes — the Great Railroad Strike (1877), the Homestead Strike (1892), the Pullman Strike (1894) — were often met with violent suppression by state and federal forces.

---

## 3. The Progressive Era (1900–1920)

Progressivism was a middle-class reform movement that sought to use government to address the problems of industrialization: inequality, corruption, dangerous working conditions, and corporate power.

**Muckrakers**: Journalists exposed social ills to a mass audience. Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle* (1906) depicted conditions in meatpacking plants so vividly that Congress passed the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act. Ida Tarbell exposed Standard Oil's monopolistic practices.

**Trust-busting**: Presidents Theodore Roosevelt and William Howard Taft used the Sherman Antitrust Act (1890) to break up monopolies. Roosevelt's Justice Department filed suit against Standard Oil; the Supreme Court ordered its breakup in 1911.

**Regulatory expansion**: The **Pure Food and Drug Act** (1906), the **Federal Trade Commission** (1914), the **Federal Reserve Act** (1913), and other legislation created new regulatory capacity.

**Constitutional amendments**: Four amendments were ratified in the Progressive Era:
- **16th (1913)**: Authorized a federal income tax
- **17th (1913)**: Direct election of senators (previously chosen by state legislatures)
- **18th (1919)**: Prohibition of alcohol
- **19th (1920)**: Women's suffrage

**Women's suffrage**: After decades of organizing — including the 1848 Seneca Falls Convention, Susan B. Anthony's campaigns, and the more militant tactics of the 20th century suffragists — the 19th Amendment gave women the right to vote.

---

## 4. Race and Progressivism

The Progressive Era's reforms largely excluded Black Americans. Many progressives held explicitly racist views and supported segregation. The period saw:
- **Nadir of American race relations**: Lynching peaked in the 1890s–1900s
- **Separate but equal**: *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) upheld segregation; it remained the law until *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954)
- **Resistance**: Booker T. Washington advocated economic advancement; W.E.B. Du Bois demanded full civil rights and helped found the NAACP (1909)

---

## Summary

The Gilded Age created industrial wealth alongside profound inequality, monopoly power, and political corruption. The Progressive Era was the first systematic attempt to use government to address these problems — through antitrust action, regulation, constitutional amendments expanding democracy, and muckraking journalism that informed public opinion. Progressivism succeeded in expanding government's role in the economy and extending political participation, but largely failed to extend its reforms to Black Americans, leaving the racial system of Jim Crow intact.
