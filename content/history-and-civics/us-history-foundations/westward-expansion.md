From the founding, Americans looked west. Within a century of independence, the United States stretched from the Atlantic to the Pacific — a transformation that remade the continent, destroyed Indigenous nations, and intensified the conflict over slavery that would eventually tear the country apart.

---

## 1. Manifest Destiny

By the 1840s, the phrase **Manifest Destiny** captured a widely held belief: that the United States was destined by Providence to expand across the North American continent. This idea combined democratic optimism, racial ideology, and national ambition into a powerful justification for territorial expansion.

Manifest Destiny was not officially U.S. policy, but it shaped the decisions of politicians, settlers, and military commanders. It also provided ideological cover for the dispossession and killing of Indigenous peoples, framing conquest as civilization's natural progress.

---

## 2. Texas, Mexico, and the Mexican-American War

American settlers had been moving into Texas — then part of Mexico — since the 1820s. In 1835, Texan settlers (primarily American-born) revolted against Mexico. After defeats at the Alamo and Goliad, Texan forces under Sam Houston defeated Santa Anna at the Battle of San Jacinto (1836). Texas declared independence as the Republic of Texas.

Texas was admitted to the United States in 1845, immediately escalating tensions with Mexico over the disputed southern border. President James K. Polk, eager for Mexican territory, sent troops to the contested area. When Mexican forces attacked, Polk told Congress this was an act of war on American soil — a claim many disputed, including Congressman Abraham Lincoln.

The **Mexican-American War (1846–48)** ended in total American victory. The **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo** transferred California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona, and parts of Colorado and New Mexico to the United States — roughly a third of Mexico's prewar territory. The purchase price was $15 million.

---

## 3. The Oregon Territory

The United States and Britain had jointly occupied the Pacific Northwest. American settlers began migrating west along the **Oregon Trail** in large numbers in the early 1840s. Expansionist pressure led to the **Oregon Treaty (1846)**, which established the 49th parallel as the northern border — splitting the territory between the U.S. and Britain (later Canada).

---

## 4. The California Gold Rush (1848–1855)

Gold discovered at Sutter's Mill in 1848 triggered a mass migration. By 1849, around 80,000 "forty-niners" had arrived in California from across the United States and the world.

The Gold Rush:
- Drove rapid California statehood (1850)
- Devastated Indigenous Californian populations through violence, disease, and displacement
- Transformed San Francisco from a village to a major city
- Intensified national debate over slavery in the new territories (California entered as a free state)

---

## 5. The Displacement and Destruction of Indigenous Nations

Westward expansion destroyed Indigenous peoples and nations through multiple mechanisms:

**Military conquest**: The U.S. Army fought hundreds of wars against Indigenous nations throughout the 19th century.

**Forced removal**: The Indian Removal Act (1830) was only the beginning. Successive treaties were signed and broken. Indigenous peoples were confined to ever-shrinking reservations.

**Decimation of the buffalo**: The Plains nations depended on the vast buffalo herds for food, clothing, and shelter. By the 1870s and 1880s, commercial hunters had slaughtered the herds nearly to extinction — deliberately, in some cases, as a war strategy against Plains peoples.

**Disease**: Repeated epidemics, exacerbated by disruption of food systems and displacement, continued to kill Indigenous populations.

**Assimilation policy**: The federal government's later approach attempted to destroy Indigenous cultures by force — through boarding schools that removed children from their communities, outlawed Indigenous languages and practices, and punished cultural expression.

Key events:
- **Sand Creek Massacre (1864)**: U.S. troops killed around 150–200 Cheyenne and Arapaho people, mostly women and children, at a peaceful encampment
- **Battle of Little Bighorn (1876)**: Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors defeated Lt. Colonel George Custer's forces in one of the last major Indigenous military victories
- **Wounded Knee Massacre (1890)**: U.S. troops killed approximately 250–300 Lakota, mostly unarmed, in South Dakota — often marked as the end of the "Indian Wars"

---

## 6. The Transcontinental Railroad

The **Transcontinental Railroad**, completed in 1869, linked the East and West coasts. Built primarily by Chinese immigrants (on the western Central Pacific section) and Irish immigrants (on the eastern Union Pacific section), under brutal working conditions, it transformed the nation's economy and accelerated settlement of the West.

The railroad made the mass slaughter of buffalo easier (facilitating commercial hunting) and enabled the movement of both settlers and military forces throughout the West.

---

## Summary

Westward expansion was driven by ideology (Manifest Destiny), land hunger, economic opportunity (the Gold Rush), and federal policy. It produced the transformation of the continent — the United States reached the Pacific — at enormous human cost. The Mexican-American War took roughly half of Mexico's territory. The displacement of Indigenous peoples was systematic and comprehensive, accomplished through warfare, treaty-breaking, environmental destruction, and cultural suppression. Understanding westward expansion requires holding together the genuine achievements and aspirations of settlers alongside the profound violence and injustice inflicted on Indigenous peoples and Mexican communities.
