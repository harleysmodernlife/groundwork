Reconstruction (1865–1877) was the era after the Civil War in which the United States attempted to rebuild the South, integrate four million formerly enslaved people as citizens, and define what the victory over the Confederacy would mean in practice. It was a period of remarkable constitutional change and genuine Black political power — and of violent counterrevolution that dismantled most of what it built.

---

## 1. The Constitutional Revolution

Reconstruction produced three constitutional amendments that transformed American law:

**13th Amendment (1865)**: Abolished slavery throughout the United States.

**14th Amendment (1868)**: Defined citizenship broadly ("all persons born or naturalized in the United States"), guaranteed equal protection of the laws, and prohibited states from denying due process. This amendment overturned *Dred Scott* and became the constitutional foundation for virtually every major civil rights expansion of the 20th century.

**15th Amendment (1870)**: Prohibited denying the right to vote based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

These amendments fundamentally altered the Constitution's relationship between citizens and government and between the federal government and the states.

---

## 2. Competing Visions of Reconstruction

**Lincoln's plan (Ten Percent Plan)**: Lenient — Confederate states could rejoin when 10% of 1860 voters swore loyalty and accepted emancipation.

**Congressional Radicals' vision**: Far more sweeping — a genuine transformation of Southern society, land redistribution to freedpeople, strong federal protection of Black rights.

**The freedpeople's own demands**: Land (particularly the Southern plantations), education, and the protection to exercise political rights. General Sherman's **Special Field Order No. 15** (January 1865) briefly promised "40 acres and a mule" to freedpeople in South Carolina and Georgia — but President Andrew Johnson rescinded it, returning the land to pardoned Confederate owners.

---

## 3. Black Political Power in Reconstruction

For a brief period under federal military occupation, formerly enslaved and free Black men voted in large numbers and won elected office. Reconstruction governments in Southern states included Black legislators, state officials, and two Black U.S. senators (Hiram Revels and Blanche Bruce of Mississippi).

These governments built the South's first public school systems, established hospitals and other institutions, and enacted progressive labor legislation. They were more democratic than anything the South had seen before — or would see again for nearly a century.

---

## 4. The Freedmen's Bureau

The **Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands** (Freedmen's Bureau), established in 1865, was the federal government's first major social welfare agency. It:
- Established schools (educated hundreds of thousands of freedpeople)
- Negotiated labor contracts
- Provided food and medical care
- Adjudicated disputes

The Bureau was underfunded, understaffed, and undermined by President Johnson — but its educational work had lasting impact, including founding historically Black colleges (HBCUs).

---

## 5. The Counterrevolution: Violence and the Ku Klux Klan

White Southerners who opposed Reconstruction used systematic violence to terrorize Black voters and Republican officeholders. The **Ku Klux Klan**, founded in 1865, and similar organizations murdered, beat, and drove out Black political leaders and their white allies.

Congress responded with the Enforcement Acts (1870–71), authorizing federal prosecution of Klan violence. Grant used them effectively, breaking the Klan temporarily. But political will in the North was fading.

---

## 6. The Compromise of 1877 and the End of Reconstruction

The disputed election of 1876 — between Republican Rutherford Hayes and Democrat Samuel Tilden — was resolved by a backroom compromise. Hayes received the presidency; federal troops were withdrawn from the South.

Without federal military protection, Reconstruction governments collapsed. White supremacist "Redeemer" governments took power across the South.

What followed:
- **Black Codes**: Laws that effectively re-enslaved Black Southerners under the guise of labor and vagrancy regulations
- **Poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses**: Voting restrictions that effectively disenfranchised Black voters for nearly a century
- **Jim Crow**: A system of racial segregation enforced by law and violence
- **Lynching**: An estimated 4,000+ documented lynchings of Black Americans in the South between 1877 and 1950

---

## 7. The Meaning of Reconstruction's Failure

Historian W.E.B. Du Bois called Reconstruction "a splendid failure" — it failed not because of anything inherent in Black political participation, but because the federal government withdrew its protection before the transformation was secure.

The failure of Reconstruction made the Civil Rights Movement of the 20th century necessary — because the rights won constitutionally in the 1860s would not be enforced in practice for another century.

---

## Summary

Reconstruction was a period of genuine constitutional transformation and Black political participation, undercut by Presidential obstruction, congressional retreat, and organized white supremacist violence. The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments transformed the Constitution's protections; their practical meaning was nullified by the counterrevolution that followed Reconstruction's end. The failure to protect Black rights after the Civil War established a pattern of racial inequality that required the Civil Rights Movement to confront nearly a century later.
