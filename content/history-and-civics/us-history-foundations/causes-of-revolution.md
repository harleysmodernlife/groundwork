The American Revolution didn't happen suddenly. It emerged from decades of growing tension between the British government and its American colonists — tension rooted in fundamental disagreements about representation, taxation, rights, and the nature of the relationship between colonies and empire.

---

## 1. The French and Indian War (1754–1763)

The immediate trigger for the revolution's causes was the French and Indian War (the North American theater of the Seven Years' War). Britain and its American colonists fought France and its Indigenous allies for control of North America.

Britain won — spectacularly. France ceded Canada and all territory east of the Mississippi. But the war left Britain with enormous debt and a vastly expanded empire to govern and defend.

To pay for this and to fund continued military presence in North America, the British government looked to the colonies for revenue. This decision would prove catastrophic for imperial unity.

---

## 2. Taxation Without Representation

Britain began imposing new taxes on the colonies:

**Stamp Act (1765)**: A direct tax on paper goods — newspapers, legal documents, pamphlets. Colonists were outraged. They had no representatives in Parliament; taxes imposed without their consent violated what they understood as their rights as English subjects.

The phrase **"no taxation without representation"** captured the colonial position. It wasn't primarily about the amount of the taxes — it was about the principle: Parliament had no right to tax people who couldn't vote for it.

**Townshend Acts (1767)**: Taxes on imported goods — glass, paint, paper, tea. More protests; more outrage.

**Boston Massacre (1770)**: British troops, stationed in Boston to enforce order, fired into a crowd killing five colonists. Colonial newspapers framed it as a massacre; it became a rallying point.

---

## 3. The Role of Ideas: Enlightenment and Republican Thought

The colonial intellectual environment was infused with **Enlightenment ideas**: natural rights, social contract, government deriving authority from the consent of the governed. John Locke's arguments that governments that violated natural rights could be legitimately resisted resonated powerfully with colonial grievances.

Colonial leaders — educated in English common law traditions and Whig political thought — framed their resistance in these terms. They weren't revolutionaries seeking to overturn all order; they were, they argued, defending the traditional rights of Englishmen against an overreaching government.

---

## 4. The Tea Act and Boston Tea Party (1773)

The Tea Act gave the British East India Company a monopoly on tea sales in the colonies, undercutting colonial merchants. Though it actually lowered the price of tea, colonists saw it as another assertion of Parliament's right to tax them without consent.

In December 1773, colonists dressed as Mohawk Indians boarded ships in Boston Harbor and dumped 342 chests of tea into the water. The **Boston Tea Party** was not spontaneous — it was organized by groups like the Sons of Liberty.

---

## 5. The Intolerable Acts (1774)

Britain's response to the Boston Tea Party was harsh — and counterproductive. The **Coercive Acts** (called Intolerable Acts by colonists) closed Boston Harbor until the tea was paid for, limited Massachusetts self-governance, and required colonists to house British troops.

Rather than isolating Massachusetts, the acts united the colonies. If Parliament could do this to Massachusetts, it could do it anywhere. The First Continental Congress convened in 1774, coordinating colonial resistance for the first time.

---

## 6. Lexington, Concord, and the Point of No Return

In April 1775, British troops marched to Concord, Massachusetts to seize colonial military supplies. Colonial militiamen confronted them at Lexington — shots were fired (who fired first remains disputed). **The shot heard round the world** began the armed conflict.

By the time the Second Continental Congress met in May 1775, the colonies were at war with Britain. The question was no longer whether to resist, but whether to declare independence — a step many colonists still hesitated to take.

---

## 7. Common Sense and the Decision for Independence

Thomas Paine's pamphlet **Common Sense** (January 1776) made the case for independence in plain language accessible to ordinary colonists. Rather than defending English constitutional rights, Paine attacked the very institution of monarchy and hereditary rule as irrational and unjust. The pamphlet sold 500,000 copies — extraordinarily for the era.

By July 1776, the Continental Congress was ready. The **Declaration of Independence**, primarily authored by Thomas Jefferson, formally announced independence and justified it in philosophical terms: governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed; when government violates natural rights, the people may alter or abolish it.

---

## Summary

The American Revolution grew from specific grievances — taxation without representation, the quartering of troops, limits on self-governance — layered onto a broader ideological framework of natural rights, popular sovereignty, and Enlightenment liberalism. The French and Indian War created the fiscal pressure that led Britain to tax the colonies; colonial resistance hardened over a decade; armed conflict began in 1775; and the decision for independence came in 1776. The revolution was both a political argument and a military struggle, and both dimensions shaped what the new nation would become.
