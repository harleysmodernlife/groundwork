The story of the United States begins long before 1776. For thousands of years before European contact, the Americas were home to hundreds of distinct Indigenous nations with complex political systems, trade networks, and cultures. The colonization of North America — by Spain, France, the Netherlands, and England — transformed this world profoundly, through conquest, settlement, trade, and the devastating consequences of disease.

---

## 1. Indigenous North America Before European Contact

By 1500, tens of millions of people lived in North America across hundreds of distinct groups. Major cultural regions included:

- **Eastern Woodlands**: Algonquian-speaking peoples along the Atlantic coast; Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy in the Northeast — a sophisticated political union of five (later six) nations
- **Southeast**: Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, and others with complex agricultural societies and political structures
- **Great Plains**: Nomadic and semi-nomadic peoples including Lakota, Comanche, and others (many became more nomadic after Spanish horses reached the continent)
- **Southwest**: Pueblo peoples; later Navajo and Apache
- **Pacific Northwest**: Complex societies sustained by salmon fishing, including Chinook, Salish, and others

European contact brought disease catastrophe: smallpox, measles, and other pathogens killed an estimated 50–90% of Indigenous populations within a century of contact. This demographic collapse reshaped the entire continent and fundamentally altered the context of colonization.

---

## 2. Spanish Colonization

Spain was the first major European power to colonize the Americas. After Columbus's voyages (1492), the Spanish established colonies throughout the Caribbean, then moved into Mexico (conquering the Aztec Empire in 1521) and South America (conquering the Inca Empire in the 1530s).

Spain's colonial system centered on the **encomienda** and later **repartimiento** — systems that compelled Indigenous labor. The **mission system** in what is now the American Southwest and California used Catholic conversion as a vehicle for settlement and control.

Spanish colonies in North America included Florida, New Mexico, Texas, and California. Spain's approach was explicitly extractive: take land, extract resources, convert Indigenous people, and enrich the crown.

---

## 3. French Colonization

France focused primarily on the fur trade, establishing relationships with Indigenous nations rather than displacing them. French settlers were relatively few; they integrated into existing trade networks, formed alliances, and often intermarried with Indigenous people.

New France stretched from Quebec through the Great Lakes and down the Mississippi Valley to Louisiana. France's approach relied on Indigenous partnerships and was therefore less immediately destructive — but French competition with England for North America eventually produced a series of devastating wars.

---

## 4. English Colonization

English colonization began in earnest in the early 1600s. England's approach differed from Spain and France: **settler colonialism** — large numbers of English families emigrating to establish permanent communities, displacing Indigenous populations rather than primarily exploiting their labor.

**Jamestown (1607)**: The first permanent English settlement. Struggled with starvation and disease; survived partly through coercive relations with the Powhatan Confederacy and eventually through tobacco cultivation.

**Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay (1620s)**: Puritan settlers seeking religious reform. The Massachusetts Bay Colony became one of the most populous and influential English colonies.

**The Carolinas, New York, Pennsylvania, and Georgia** followed, each with distinct founding purposes — religious freedom (Pennsylvania, founded by Quaker William Penn), proprietary profit, or social reform.

---

## 5. The Colonial Social Order

English colonies developed stratified societies:

**Indentured servitude** was the initial labor system: Europeans contracted to work for 4–7 years in exchange for passage to the colonies. Indentured servants were legally bound but eventually free.

**Slavery** developed as tobacco, rice, and indigo plantations required more labor. By the late 1600s, the colonies were shifting from indentured servitude to enslaved African labor — cheaper, permanent, and inheritable. By 1750, hundreds of thousands of Africans had been enslaved and transported to the colonies against their will.

**Indigenous displacement** accelerated as English settlers expanded. Wars — including King Philip's War (1675–1676) in New England and the Yamasee War (1715–1717) in the Southeast — were devastating to Native peoples.

---

## 6. The Thirteen Colonies

By the mid-1700s, thirteen British colonies lined the Atlantic coast. They were not unified — they had different economies (tobacco in Virginia, fishing and shipbuilding in New England, diverse trade in the Middle Colonies), different religious traditions, and different social structures.

What they shared: English common law traditions, Protestant Christianity (in most), colonial assemblies with some experience of self-governance, and a growing population that was pushing westward against Indigenous territories and against the terms of the Proclamation of 1763, which tried to limit westward expansion after the French and Indian War.

---

## Summary

North America before European contact was home to hundreds of distinct Indigenous nations. European colonization — by Spain, France, and especially England — brought disease, displacement, and ultimately settler colonialism that supplanted Indigenous peoples. The English colonial system developed a racial hierarchy built on African slavery and Indigenous dispossession. The thirteen colonies that eventually became the United States shared British legal and political traditions while developing increasingly distinct identities, laying the groundwork for the revolution to come.
