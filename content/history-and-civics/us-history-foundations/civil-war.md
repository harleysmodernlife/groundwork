The Civil War (1861–1865) was the bloodiest conflict in American history — over 620,000 soldiers died, more than in any other American war. It was fought over the fundamental question of slavery, though Southern secessionists and some Northern leaders framed it in different terms. It ended slavery and preserved the Union, and its unresolved aftermath shaped American life for generations.

---

## 1. Secession and the Confederacy

After Lincoln's election in November 1860, seven Southern states seceded before he even took office: South Carolina, Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas. They formed the **Confederate States of America**, with Jefferson Davis as president and a constitution that explicitly protected slavery.

The Confederate states' own secession declarations were explicit about the cause. South Carolina's declaration: *"an increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery."* Mississippi's: *"Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery — the greatest material interest of the world."*

Four more states seceded after the war began: Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina.

The **border states** — Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri — were slave states that remained in the Union. Lincoln worked carefully to keep them, understanding that losing Maryland would surround Washington D.C. with Confederate territory.

---

## 2. Fort Sumter and the War's Beginning

Confederate forces demanded the surrender of Fort Sumter, a federal installation in Charleston Harbor, South Carolina. When Lincoln moved to resupply it, Confederate artillery opened fire on April 12, 1861. The fort surrendered; the war had begun.

The firing on Fort Sumter galvanized Northern opinion and completed Southern secession. Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers; thousands came.

---

## 3. The Two Sides

**The Union (North)**: 22 million people, most of the country's industrial capacity, most of its railroads, a functioning navy.

**The Confederacy (South)**: 9 million people (including nearly 4 million enslaved people who could not be considered reliable soldiers and who actively undermined Confederate war efforts), less industrial capacity, but fighting a defensive war on home terrain.

Confederate strategy: survive long enough for the North to tire of the war. Union strategy: blockade the South, control the Mississippi, and destroy Confederate armies.

---

## 4. Key Turning Points

**First Bull Run (1861)**: Union troops were routed, dispelling illusions of a short war.

**The Anaconda Plan**: General Winfield Scott's strategy of naval blockade + Mississippi control gradually strangled Confederate resources.

**Antietam (1862)**: The bloodiest single day in American military history (over 22,000 casualties). A Union "victory" — Confederate forces withdrew — that gave Lincoln the political standing to issue the Emancipation Proclamation.

**The Emancipation Proclamation (1863)**: Lincoln declared enslaved people in Confederate states "forever free." This transformed the war's meaning — from a war for union to a war for union and freedom. It also prevented European recognition of the Confederacy (Britain and France would not ally with a slaveholding nation in a war against freedom).

**Gettysburg and Vicksburg (July 4, 1863)**: Confederate General Robert E. Lee's invasion of Pennsylvania was repulsed at enormous cost (50,000+ casualties over three days). The same day, Vicksburg fell, giving the Union control of the Mississippi.

**Sherman's March to the Sea (1864)**: Union General William Sherman's army cut through Georgia, destroying railroads and supplies, targeting Confederate civilian infrastructure as a deliberate war strategy.

**Appomattox (April 9, 1865)**: Lee surrendered to General Ulysses Grant. Other Confederate forces followed over the next weeks. The war was over.

---

## 5. The Confederacy's Defeat

The Confederacy was ultimately outproduced, outmanned, and outled. Enslaved people themselves contributed to Confederate defeat: by running to Union lines (where Lincoln eventually enlisted them as soldiers — 180,000 Black men served in the Union Army), by slowing work, and by providing intelligence.

Lincoln's leadership — holding the Union together through devastating losses, finding Grant and Sherman, winning reelection in 1864 despite war weariness — was decisive.

---

## 6. Lincoln's Assassination and the 13th Amendment

On April 14, 1865 — five days after Lee's surrender — Lincoln was shot by Confederate sympathizer John Wilkes Booth at Ford's Theatre in Washington. He died the next morning.

The **13th Amendment**, ratified in December 1865, formally abolished slavery throughout the United States — completing what the Emancipation Proclamation had begun.

---

## 7. What the War Settled (and What It Didn't)

The Civil War settled: the union was permanent; the federal government was supreme over states; slavery was abolished.

It did not settle: the question of what freedom would mean for four million formerly enslaved people and their descendants. That struggle continued through Reconstruction, Jim Crow, and the Civil Rights movement — and in many ways continues today.

---

## Summary

The Civil War was fought over slavery — the Confederate states said so explicitly in their secession declarations. The Union's material advantages — population, industry, railroads, and navy — eventually prevailed over the Confederacy's defensive advantages and early military leadership. The Emancipation Proclamation transformed the war's moral meaning and shaped its international context. The war ended slavery through military defeat and the 13th Amendment, preserved the Union, and established federal supremacy. But the fundamental question of what Black freedom would mean in American life remained violently contested in the decades that followed.
