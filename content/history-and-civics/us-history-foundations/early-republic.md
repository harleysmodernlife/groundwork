The period from ratification of the Constitution to the eve of the Civil War — roughly 1789–1860 — was the United States' critical first test as a nation. The founders had created a constitutional framework, but everything else remained to be worked out: how power would actually be exercised, what relationship the federal government would have to the states and to the economy, who would be included in the political community, and whether the republic could survive the tensions built into its founding.

---

## 1. The First Presidency: Washington and Precedent

George Washington's presidency (1789–1797) was pivotal in ways he understood clearly — everything he did would set precedents. He established the cabinet, the practice of presidential addresses to Congress, and the norm of limiting presidents to two terms (formalized only by the 22nd Amendment in 1951).

His administration dealt immediately with economic crisis. **Alexander Hamilton**, Treasury Secretary, proposed a financial program: assume all state debts, create a national bank, and protect American manufacturing. **Thomas Jefferson** and **James Madison** opposed this as unconstitutional federal overreach and a system that favored financial elites over farmers.

This division became the first American political party system: **Federalists** (Hamilton) vs. **Democratic-Republicans** (Jefferson and Madison).

Washington warned in his **Farewell Address** against "permanent alliances" with foreign nations and against the "baneful effects of the spirit of party." Both warnings were largely ignored.

---

## 2. The Alien and Sedition Acts

During the **XYZ Affair** (1797–98) — a near-war with France — the Federalist-controlled Congress passed the **Alien and Sedition Acts**, which among other things made it illegal to "publish false, scandalous, and malicious" writings against the government.

Jefferson and Madison responded with the **Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions**, asserting that states could "nullify" unconstitutional federal laws. This doctrine of nullification would resurface repeatedly, most fatally before the Civil War.

---

## 3. The Election of 1800 and the "Revolution of 1800"

When Jefferson defeated Adams in the 1800 election — the first peaceful transfer of power between opposing political parties in a republic — it demonstrated that the constitutional system could survive partisan competition. Jefferson called it a "revolution" because it reversed Federalist policies without violence or constitutional rupture.

The election revealed a flaw: electors voted separately for president and vice president, and Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr received equal votes. Congress had to choose between them. The 12th Amendment (1804) corrected this, establishing separate electoral votes for president and vice president.

---

## 4. Marbury v. Madison and Judicial Review

In his final days, outgoing President Adams appointed dozens of Federalist judges — "midnight judges." When Jefferson took office, he ordered his Secretary of State James Madison not to deliver the commissions. William Marbury sued.

Chief Justice John Marshall used the case to establish **judicial review** — the Supreme Court's power to declare laws unconstitutional. The ruling in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) denied Marbury his commission on technical grounds (avoiding a confrontation Jefferson could simply ignore), but established the principle that the Court is the final arbiter of constitutional meaning. This decision fundamentally shaped American governance.

---

## 5. The Louisiana Purchase (1803)

Napoleon, needing money for European wars, offered to sell France's vast North American territory to the United States. Jefferson, despite doubting his constitutional authority to do so (nowhere does the Constitution authorize purchasing foreign territory), agreed. The **Louisiana Purchase** doubled the size of the United States for $15 million.

Jefferson sent Meriwether Lewis and William Clark to explore the new territory (1804–06). The purchase intensified questions about the expansion of slavery into new territories — questions that would dominate American politics for the next six decades.

---

## 6. War of 1812

The young republic's relationship with European powers remained volatile. Britain, at war with Napoleon, was impressing American sailors into the Royal Navy and supporting Indigenous resistance to American expansion.

Congress declared war in 1812. The war was inconclusive militarily — the British burned Washington, D.C.; the Americans failed to conquer Canada; Andrew Jackson won a famous victory at the Battle of New Orleans (after the peace treaty had already been signed). The **Treaty of Ghent** (1814) essentially restored the status quo ante.

The war did produce outcomes: a surge of American nationalism, the death of the Federalist Party, and the negotiation of the northern border with Britain.

---

## 7. Jacksonian Democracy and Its Contradictions

Andrew Jackson's presidency (1829–1837) represented a new democratic era — expanding voting rights for white men (removing property requirements in most states), positioning the president as the direct representative of the people, and attacking the Bank of the United States as an elite institution.

But Jacksonian democracy had severe limits:

- **Indian Removal Act (1830)**: Authorized the forced removal of tens of thousands of Indigenous people from the Southeast. The **Trail of Tears** (1838–39) killed thousands of Cherokee.
- Jacksonian democracy was explicitly democracy for white men; Black Americans, women, and Indigenous peoples were excluded.

---

## Summary

The early republic was defined by the practical challenges of making a new constitutional system work: building institutions, managing partisan conflict, navigating European wars, expanding territory, and confronting the tensions between the republic's ideals and its realities. Judicial review, the Louisiana Purchase, and the two-party system all emerged in this period. Jacksonian democracy expanded political participation for white men while intensifying exclusion and dispossession for everyone else — setting the stage for the fundamental conflict over slavery that would tear the republic apart.
