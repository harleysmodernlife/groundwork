The American Revolution was both a war for independence and an experiment in self-governance. Fighting the most powerful military in the world with a fragile coalition of colonies, limited resources, and no established government, the revolutionaries created something genuinely new — a republic governed by a written constitution and based, at least in principle, on popular sovereignty.

---

## 1. The Revolutionary War (1775–1783)

The Continental Army, commanded by General George Washington, faced an enormous disadvantage against the British. The British had a professional army, a powerful navy, loyalist support within the colonies (perhaps a third of colonists remained loyal to Britain), and vastly superior resources.

The Americans had advantages too: fighting on familiar terrain, the motivation of defending their homes, and the support (passive or active) of much of the population. Washington's strategy — avoid decisive engagement, keep the army intact, wear down British resolve — proved crucial.

**Key turning points**:

- **Bunker Hill (1775)**: Bloody British victory that demonstrated colonial willingness to fight
- **Trenton (1776)**: Washington's surprise crossing of the Delaware River on Christmas night; a desperately needed morale boost after a string of defeats
- **Saratoga (1777)**: The decisive American victory that convinced France to enter the war as an American ally — providing the money, troops, and naval power that ultimately tipped the balance
- **Valley Forge (1777–78)**: A brutal winter encampment that tested the Continental Army to its limits; survivors emerged more disciplined and committed
- **Yorktown (1781)**: British General Cornwallis, trapped by French naval forces and besieged by Washington's combined American-French force, surrendered. The last major battle of the war.

The **Treaty of Paris (1783)** formally ended the war. Britain recognized American independence and ceded all territory east of the Mississippi River.

## 2. The Question of Who the Revolution Was For

The revolution's ideals — "all men are created equal," natural rights, popular sovereignty — sat uncomfortably alongside its realities.

**Slavery**: The Declaration of Independence's assertion of equality was written largely by enslavers. An estimated 450,000 enslaved people were held in the colonies at the revolution's start. Some founders hoped slavery would gradually die out; others actively benefited from it and had no intention of ending it. The Constitution's compromises on slavery — the Three-Fifths Compromise, the protection of the slave trade until 1808, the Fugitive Slave Clause — embedded the contradiction into the republic's foundation.

**Women**: Women were excluded from formal political participation. Abigail Adams famously wrote to her husband John to "remember the ladies" in forming the new government; he responded that men would not give up "the masculine systems." Women's legal rights were severely limited.

**Indigenous peoples**: The revolution expanded settler encroachment on Indigenous lands. The new republic's westward ambitions accelerated displacement and conflict.

**Non-property-owning white men**: Most states initially restricted voting to property owners. Gradual expansion of suffrage followed over subsequent decades.

The revolution created a framework — natural rights, popular sovereignty, representative government — that proved generative for subsequent demands for inclusion, even when the founders themselves failed to extend it.

## 3. The Articles of Confederation

The first national government was created by the **Articles of Confederation** (1781). Its design reflected colonial suspicion of centralized power:

- No executive
- No independent federal taxation
- Decisions requiring supermajorities
- Each state with one vote regardless of size

The Articles failed in practice. The government couldn't pay its debts, couldn't regulate commerce between states, couldn't enforce its own laws, and couldn't respond to events like Shays' Rebellion (1786–87) — a debtor uprising in Massachusetts that revealed the central government's impotence.

---

## 4. The Constitutional Convention (1787)

Delegates gathered in Philadelphia — ostensibly to revise the Articles, in practice to scrap them entirely and write a new constitution.

Key compromises:
- **The Great Compromise**: Bicameral Congress — proportional representation in the House (satisfying large states), equal representation in the Senate (satisfying small states)
- **The Three-Fifths Compromise**: Enslaved people counted as three-fifths of a person for apportionment purposes — giving slaveholding states more representation while denying enslaved people any political rights
- **The Electoral College**: Presidents chosen indirectly through state-appointed electors, not direct popular vote

The resulting Constitution created a stronger federal government with separated powers, checks and balances, and — crucially — direct authority over individual citizens, not just states.

---

## 5. Ratification and the Bill of Rights

Ratification required nine of thirteen states. Fierce debate followed — **Federalists** (favoring ratification, including Hamilton, Madison, and Jay in *The Federalist Papers*) against **Anti-Federalists** (fearing a too-powerful central government and the absence of explicit rights protections).

The compromise: the first Congress would add a Bill of Rights. Ratification succeeded (1788–89); the first ten amendments were ratified in 1791.

---

## Summary

The American Revolution succeeded militarily through tenacity, French alliance, and British strategic overextension. The Declaration of Independence articulated universal principles of natural rights and popular sovereignty while leaving fundamental contradictions — especially slavery — unresolved. The Articles of Confederation proved unworkable; the Constitution created a stronger federal government through a series of significant compromises. The founding generation established genuinely new political forms, even as they failed to live up to their own professed ideals.
