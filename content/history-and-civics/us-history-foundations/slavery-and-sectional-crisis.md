Slavery was the defining contradiction of the American republic. A nation founded on ideals of liberty and equality built its economy — especially in the South — on the forced labor of enslaved people. For decades, political leaders sought compromises that would hold the union together while slavery's expansion intensified the moral and political crisis. By 1860, the compromises had failed.

---

## 1. The Scale and Nature of Slavery

By 1860, nearly four million people were enslaved in the United States. Enslaved people were legally property — they could be bought, sold, separated from their families, and subjected to violence without legal recourse.

Slavery was not simply labor exploitation — it was a total system of domination:
- Enslaved people were denied literacy (teaching enslaved people to read was illegal in most southern states)
- Marriage between enslaved people had no legal recognition; families were routinely separated through sale
- Enslaved people who resisted faced brutal punishment
- Sexual exploitation of enslaved women was pervasive

Resistance took many forms: slowing work, feigning illness, running away, and occasionally armed revolt (Nat Turner's rebellion, 1831; others). The Underground Railroad — a network of routes and safe houses — helped thousands escape to the North or Canada.

---

## 2. The Economics of Slavery

Southern plantation agriculture — tobacco, cotton, rice, sugar — was extraordinarily profitable, and profitability depended on enslaved labor. By 1860, the South produced most of the world's cotton, which supplied the textile mills of both England and the American North.

The value of enslaved people as property exceeded the value of all banks, railroads, and factories in the antebellum United States combined. Southern political and economic elites had enormous financial incentive to defend and expand slavery.

Northern manufacturing interests and small farmers had different economic interests — but Northern economy was also intertwined with Southern slavery through textile mills, banking, and shipping.

---

## 3. The Missouri Compromise (1820)

As the nation expanded, every new territory raised the question: would slavery be permitted? The balance of slave and free states in the Senate meant that each new state would shift power.

The **Missouri Compromise** (1820) admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state (preserving the balance), and drew the line 36°30' — slavery prohibited in new territories north of that line, permitted south of it.

This held the crisis at bay for thirty years.

---

## 4. Abolition and the Moral Argument

The abolitionist movement argued that slavery was a moral evil that must be immediately ended — not gradually or with compensation to enslavers.

**William Lloyd Garrison** founded The Liberator (1831) and the American Anti-Slavery Society. **Frederick Douglass**, himself escaped from slavery, became the movement's most powerful voice — his autobiography (*Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass*, 1845) exposed slavery's brutality to a mass audience. **Harriet Beecher Stowe's** *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852) reached millions more.

The abolitionist movement was controversial even in the North — many Northerners opposed slavery's expansion but also opposed racial equality or disruption of the union.

---

## 5. The Compromise of 1850 and "Bleeding Kansas"

The Mexican-American War opened vast new territory, reviving the question of slavery's expansion.

**The Compromise of 1850**: California entered as a free state; the rest of the new territory would be decided by "popular sovereignty" (the inhabitants would vote); a strengthened Fugitive Slave Act required Northerners to return escaped enslaved people.

The Fugitive Slave Act outraged Northerners — it made them complicit in slavery on their own soil. Resistance to the act and its enforcement radicalized many.

The **Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854)** repealed the Missouri Compromise line and opened both territories to popular sovereignty. Pro-slavery and anti-slavery settlers rushed in; violence erupted. "**Bleeding Kansas**" was a preview of the civil war to come.

---

## 6. Dred Scott (1857) and John Brown (1859)

**Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)**: The Supreme Court ruled that enslaved people were not citizens and had no standing to sue; that Congress had no power to ban slavery from territories (voiding the Missouri Compromise entirely); and that Black Americans, enslaved or free, had no rights the government was bound to respect. The decision was celebrated in the South and outraged in the North.

**John Brown's Raid (1859)**: Abolitionist John Brown led a raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, hoping to arm enslaved people for a revolt. He was captured, tried, and hanged. Brown was considered a martyr by many Northerners and a terrorist by Southerners.

These events made compromise seem impossible.

---

## 7. The Election of 1860

The Democratic Party split in 1860 — Northern Democrats nominated Stephen Douglas; Southern Democrats nominated John C. Breckinridge. The newly formed **Republican Party** — committed to preventing slavery's expansion but not immediately abolishing it — nominated Abraham Lincoln.

Lincoln won without carrying a single Southern state. Before his inauguration, seven Southern states seceded. The failure of decades of compromise had brought the nation to the edge of civil war.

---

## Summary

Slavery was embedded in American economics, law, and politics from the founding. The question of slavery's expansion into new territories drove a series of crises — the Missouri Compromise, the Compromise of 1850, Bleeding Kansas — that each paper over but deepened the fundamental conflict. The abolitionist movement built the moral case for immediate emancipation; the Dred Scott decision demonstrated that the judiciary would not protect it; John Brown's raid showed that the conflict was approaching violence. Lincoln's election in 1860 convinced Southern states that the union no longer protected their interests. Secession and war followed.
