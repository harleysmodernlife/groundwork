Between 1775 and 1848, a series of revolutions — in America, France, Haiti, and across Latin America and Europe — challenged traditional authority and attempted to establish governments based on popular sovereignty, rights, and constitutions. These Atlantic Revolutions shared Enlightenment ideas but produced very different outcomes.

---

## 1. The Enlightenment Foundation

The revolutions were rooted in **Enlightenment** philosophy — the 18th century intellectual movement that applied reason to human society, politics, and morality.

Key ideas:
- **Natural rights**: People possess inherent rights — to life, liberty, and property — that governments cannot legitimately violate (John Locke)
- **Social contract**: Government derives its legitimate authority from the consent of the governed; if it violates rights, the people may replace it
- **Popular sovereignty**: Political authority belongs to the people, not to monarchs or aristocrats by divine right
- **Separation of powers**: Montesquieu argued governments should have separate legislative, executive, and judicial branches

These ideas circulated through books, coffeehouses, salons, and pamphlets across the Atlantic world, read by educated elites who would lead the revolutions.

---

## 2. The American Revolution

As covered in more depth in the U.S. History section: American colonists, drawing on Enlightenment ideas and English constitutional traditions, revolted against British taxation without representation (1775–1783). The Declaration of Independence articulated natural rights philosophy; the Constitution created a republic with separated powers and checks and balances.

The American Revolution was conservative relative to what followed in France — it changed who governed, not the fundamental social structure. Slavery survived; property qualifications for voting remained; Indigenous rights were ignored. But it demonstrated that a republic based on Enlightenment principles could function.

---

## 3. The French Revolution (1789–1799)

France's revolution was far more radical. By 1789, France was in financial crisis (from wars, including support for the American Revolution), facing food shortages, and seething with resentment of an aristocracy that paid no taxes while commoners bore the burden.

**The Estates-General (1789)**: The king summoned this assembly (dormant since 1614) to address the financial crisis. The Third Estate (commoners) demanded more representation; when refused, they declared themselves a **National Assembly** and pledged to write a constitution.

**The Tennis Court Oath**: The Third Estate found themselves locked out of their meeting hall and gathered on a nearby tennis court, vowing not to disband until a constitution was written.

**Bastille (July 14, 1789)**: A Paris crowd stormed the Bastille fortress, a symbol of royal tyranny. This date — Bastille Day — is now France's national holiday.

**Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen (1789)**: Asserted liberty, equality, and popular sovereignty.

**The Terror (1793–1794)**: The revolution radicalized. The monarchy was abolished; Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette were guillotined. The Committee of Public Safety, led by **Maximilien Robespierre**, executed tens of thousands as counter-revolutionary enemies. Eventually Robespierre himself was guillotined.

**Napoleon Bonaparte** ended the revolutionary period, seizing power in 1799 and eventually declaring himself Emperor (1804). Napoleon exported both revolutionary ideals (the Code Napoléon unified law, abolished serfdom) and military conquest across Europe.

---

## 4. The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)

The Haitian Revolution was the most radical of the Atlantic Revolutions and the only successful slave revolt in history to produce an independent nation.

Saint-Domingue (modern Haiti) was France's most profitable colony — its enslaved population (500,000) produced enormous quantities of sugar and coffee. In 1791, enslaved people rose in revolt. The rebellion grew into a complex war involving France, Spain, Britain, and the colony's own free Black and mixed-race populations.

**Toussaint Louverture**, a formerly enslaved man of extraordinary military and political skill, emerged as the revolution's leader. He successfully expelled British and Spanish forces.

After Napoleon attempted to reimpose slavery in 1802 — and had Toussaint arrested, where he died in prison — the revolution continued under Jean-Jacques Dessalines. On January 1, 1804, Haiti declared independence — the first Black republic in history.

France imposed massive reparations on Haiti (for lost "property" — enslaved people — that France had "lost") that Haiti paid until 1947, hobbling its development.

---

## 5. Latin American Independence (1810–1825)

Inspired by the American and French Revolutions and by Napoleon's conquest of Spain (which undermined Spanish authority in the colonies), Latin American colonies won independence through a series of wars:

- **Simón Bolívar** led liberation movements in Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia
- **José de San Martín** liberated Argentina, Chile, and Peru
- **Miguel Hidalgo** initiated Mexican independence (1810); it was achieved in 1821

These revolutions were primarily led by the **Creole elite** (American-born people of European descent) — who wanted independence from Spain but generally preserved the existing social hierarchy, including slavery (where it existed) and Indigenous subordination.

---

## 6. The European Revolutions of 1848

A wave of revolutions swept Europe in 1848 — France, the German states, the Austrian Empire, Italy — demanding constitutions, national self-determination, and liberal reforms. They were partly inspired by economic crisis (poor harvests, early industrialization) and the accumulated influence of Enlightenment and Romantic nationalism.

Most of the 1848 revolutions failed — conservative forces reasserted control within a year or two. But they showed the power of nationalist and liberal ideas, and many of their demands would eventually be achieved in subsequent decades.

---

## Summary

The Atlantic Revolutions of 1775–1848 applied Enlightenment ideas — natural rights, popular sovereignty, constitutionalism — to challenge monarchical authority. The American Revolution created a stable republic while preserving existing hierarchies. The French Revolution was more radical, producing the Declaration of the Rights of Man, the Terror, and Napoleon. The Haitian Revolution — the only successful slave revolution in history — produced the world's first Black republic. Latin American independence replaced European with local Creole rule. The 1848 European revolutions mostly failed but demonstrated the durability of liberal and nationalist demands.
