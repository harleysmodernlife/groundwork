The Scientific Revolution (roughly 1543–1687) transformed how Europeans understood the natural world. Through observation, experimentation, and mathematical reasoning — rather than reliance on ancient authority — scientists developed a new method for producing knowledge and arrived at discoveries that overturned fundamental assumptions about the universe.

---

## 1. The Context: Challenging Ancient Authority

Medieval European intellectual life was dominated by the authority of ancient texts — primarily Aristotle (for natural philosophy) and Ptolemy (for astronomy) — as interpreted through the Catholic Church. The idea that careful empirical investigation might contradict these authorities was intellectually dangerous.

The Renaissance had already begun questioning this deference to tradition; the Reformation challenged the Church's authority in religion; the Scientific Revolution challenged it in natural philosophy.

---

## 2. The Copernican Revolution

**Nicolaus Copernicus** (1473–1543), a Polish mathematician and astronomer, proposed in *On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres* (1543) that the Earth revolves around the Sun — the **heliocentric model**. This contradicted the **geocentric** (Earth-centered) model of Ptolemy, which placed Earth at the universe's center.

Copernicus's model had problems (it still used circular orbits, which didn't fit observation perfectly), but it launched a revolution in thinking. The heliocentric model was not merely an astronomical adjustment — it displaced humanity from the center of the universe.

---

## 3. Tycho Brahe and Johannes Kepler

**Tycho Brahe** (1546–1601), a Danish astronomer, made the most precise naked-eye astronomical observations in history — meticulous records of planetary motion that would be crucial for what came next.

**Johannes Kepler** (1571–1630) used Brahe's data to derive three laws of planetary motion, most famously that planets move in **ellipses** (not circles) around the Sun. This resolved the inaccuracies in Copernicus's model and provided the mathematical foundation for later work.

---

## 4. Galileo and the Telescope

**Galileo Galilei** (1564–1642) turned the newly invented telescope toward the sky and discovered:
- Mountains on the Moon (disproving the idea that celestial bodies were perfect spheres)
- Jupiter's moons (disproving that all celestial bodies orbit Earth)
- The phases of Venus (consistent with heliocentrism, inconsistent with geocentrism)

Galileo was a brilliant experimenter who also challenged Aristotelian physics. He showed that objects fall at the same rate regardless of weight (contradicting Aristotle), and laid groundwork for what Newton would later formalize.

The **Inquisition** tried Galileo in 1633 for heresy — for teaching heliocentrism after being ordered not to. He was forced to recant and spent the rest of his life under house arrest. The case became the symbol of conflict between scientific inquiry and religious authority.

---

## 5. Francis Bacon and the Scientific Method

**Francis Bacon** (1561–1626), an English philosopher, argued for an empirical, inductive approach to knowledge: observe the world, collect data, generalize from observation. He criticized reliance on ancient authority and logical deduction from unexamined premises.

Bacon's philosophy — though he made no major scientific discoveries himself — influenced how subsequent generations thought about the proper method for acquiring knowledge. His vision of organized, systematic empirical research anticipated modern scientific institutions.

---

## 6. René Descartes and Mathematical Reasoning

**René Descartes** (1596–1650), a French philosopher and mathematician, took a different but complementary approach — emphasizing **rational deduction from first principles**. "I think, therefore I am" (*cogito ergo sum*) was his starting point. Descartes developed **analytical geometry**, connecting algebra and geometry.

Descartes's **mechanical philosophy** imagined the natural world as a machine operating by mathematical laws — a framework that unified physics and mathematics.

---

## 7. Isaac Newton: The Synthesis

**Isaac Newton** (1643–1727) synthesized the work of his predecessors into a unified mathematical framework.

**Principia Mathematica** (1687) presented:
- **Laws of motion** (three laws describing how objects move and interact)
- **Universal gravitation**: Every body attracts every other body with a force proportional to their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their distance. This one law explained both the fall of an apple and the orbits of planets.

Newton also developed **calculus** (simultaneously with Leibniz), a mathematical tool for describing continuous change.

Newton's achievement represented a new way of understanding the world: universal mathematical laws governing all matter, discoverable through reason and observation.

---

## 8. Broader Impacts

**The Scientific Method**: The combination of empirical observation, controlled experiment, mathematical analysis, and peer review became the dominant framework for producing reliable knowledge.

**Institutions**: Scientific societies — the Royal Society in London (1660), the Académie des Sciences in Paris (1666) — institutionalized science as a collective, peer-reviewed enterprise.

**The Enlightenment**: The Scientific Revolution's success inspired philosophers to apply similar methods to human society, politics, and morality — leading to the Enlightenment.

**Theology and science**: The relationship was complex — many scientists were devout Christians; Newton wrote more on theology than on science. But the methodological shift placed natural explanation at the center of science, gradually marginalizing supernatural explanation.

---

## Summary

The Scientific Revolution replaced reliance on ancient authority with empirical observation, mathematical reasoning, and systematic experimentation. Copernicus proposed heliocentrism; Kepler provided its mathematical foundations; Galileo provided observational evidence; Newton unified everything into a mathematical framework explaining motion and gravity. The new scientific method — empirical, quantitative, collective — transformed humanity's ability to understand and eventually control the natural world, and inspired the Enlightenment's application of similar reasoning to human society.
