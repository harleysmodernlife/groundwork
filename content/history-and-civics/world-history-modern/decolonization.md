Decolonization — the process by which colonized peoples won independence from European empires — was the defining political process of the mid-20th century. Between 1945 and 1975, dozens of new nations emerged from colonial rule across Asia, Africa, and the Caribbean. This process was rarely smooth; independence often came through prolonged struggle, and the legacies of colonialism shaped the challenges the new nations faced.

---

## 1. Why Decolonization Happened When It Did

**The World Wars weakened European powers**: Britain and France emerged from WWII economically exhausted and dependent on American support. They could no longer afford to maintain overseas empires or suppress large-scale independence movements.

**The wars delegitimized colonial ideology**: Fighting fascism — a doctrine of racial hierarchy and imperial domination — made it difficult to maintain rhetoric about the civilizing mission of European colonialism. Colonial subjects who had fought for the Allies asked why they had fought for freedoms denied to them.

**The United States opposed formal colonialism**: American ideology was rhetorically anti-colonial (despite its own imperial history). Cold War competition meant the U.S. wanted newly independent nations to choose the Western side — which required supporting decolonization.

**Independence movements had matured**: A generation of educated colonial leaders — trained often at European universities — organized mass movements demanding independence. The Indian independence movement, led by Gandhi, was the most influential model.

**The United Nations**: The new UN system provided a forum for anti-colonial arguments and a framework of self-determination and human rights that colonial powers found difficult to argue against.

---

## 2. South Asia

**India (1947)**: The Indian independence movement, led by **Mahatma Gandhi** (nonviolent civil disobedience) and **Jawaharlal Nehru** (institutional politics), forced British withdrawal. **Partition** — the division of the subcontinent into India and Pakistan along religious lines — produced one of history's largest forced migrations (10–20 million people) and massive communal violence that killed hundreds of thousands to over a million.

**Pakistan** (1947) was created as a Muslim-majority state; its eastern wing became **Bangladesh** through a brutal war in 1971.

**Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia**: French Indochina — French colonies since the 19th century — saw independence movements suppressed by France before American involvement took over (see Cold War section). Independence came for Cambodia and Laos in 1953, for Vietnam through the fall of Saigon in 1975.

---

## 3. Africa

The decolonization of Africa was dramatic: in 1950, almost the entire continent was under European rule; by 1975, almost none of it was.

**Ghana (1957)**: Under **Kwame Nkrumah**, the first African country south of the Sahara to win independence, setting off a wave.

**French West Africa**: France's approach was to seek integration of colonies into France, then under pressure transitioned most of its African colonies to independence in 1960 — the "Year of Africa" when 17 new nations were born.

**Belgian Congo (1960)**: Achieved independence with little preparation; descended almost immediately into civil war and military coup. **Patrice Lumumba**, the first Prime Minister, was assassinated — with CIA involvement — within months. The country's instability (eventually ruled by Mobutu Sese Seko for 32 years under Western support) exemplified Cold War interference in decolonization.

**Algeria (1962)**: France's war to maintain its settler colony cost an estimated 1.5 million Algerian lives. Independence came after eight years of brutal conflict.

**Zimbabwe (1980), Namibia (1990)**: Among the last African nations to win independence from white minority rule.

**South Africa**: The apartheid regime — a system of racial segregation imposed by the white minority — maintained through severe repression until 1994, when **Nelson Mandela** led the African National Congress to democratic victory.

---

## 4. The Palestinian-Israeli Conflict

The British Mandate of Palestine — controlled by Britain since WWI — became a focal point of competing nationalisms. Jewish immigration, including Holocaust survivors, intensified after WWII; Arab Palestinians resisted. Britain withdrew; Israel declared independence in 1948; the first Arab-Israeli war immediately followed. Hundreds of thousands of Palestinians became refugees (**the Nakba**, "the catastrophe" in Arabic). The conflict remains unresolved and continues to shape the Middle East.

---

## 5. The Non-Aligned Movement

Many newly independent nations, led by figures like Nehru (India), Nasser (Egypt), Tito (Yugoslavia), and Sukarno (Indonesia), attempted to chart a path between the U.S. and Soviet spheres — the **Non-Aligned Movement** (1961). The Cold War superpowers routinely interfered with these aspirations, supporting client governments and undermining or overthrowing independent ones that seemed too close to the opposing side.

---

## 6. The Legacies of Colonialism

Decolonization created independent nations but didn't erase colonialism's effects:

**Economic dependence**: Colonial economies had been organized to export raw materials; independent nations inherited structures that made development difficult.

**Arbitrary borders**: Colonial-era borders cut across ethnic and cultural lines, creating multiethnic states with built-in tensions. Many post-independence conflicts — in Nigeria, Congo, Rwanda, Sudan — grew partly from these divisions.

**Educated elites vs. broad empowerment**: Decolonization was often led by a small Western-educated elite; genuine democratic participation was harder to achieve.

**Neo-colonialism**: Economic dependence on former colonial powers and international institutions often constrained sovereignty even after formal independence.

---

## Summary

Decolonization was driven by the weakening of European powers by the World Wars, the delegitimization of colonial ideology, maturing independence movements, and Cold War competition. South Asian independence (1947) was accompanied by devastating partition; African decolonization was rapid (1960s–70s) but left colonial-era borders and economic structures intact. Cold War interference complicated the independence of many new nations. The legacies of colonialism — arbitrary borders, export-oriented economies, suppressed institutions — continue to shape the postcolonial world.
