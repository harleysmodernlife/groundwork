Between 1400 and 1600, European sailors explored and mapped the coastlines of Africa, Asia, and the Americas — connecting the world in ways that had never happened before. The "Age of Exploration" (also called the Age of Discovery) transformed global trade, reshaped human populations, and initiated European colonial empires that would dominate much of the world for four centuries.

---

## 1. Why European Exploration?

Several factors drove European maritime exploration:

**The desire for direct trade with Asia**: The spice trade was enormously profitable — spices like pepper, cinnamon, and nutmeg were worth fortunes in Europe. But the overland Silk Road routes were controlled by Muslim intermediaries; European merchants wanted direct access.

**Technology**: Better ships (the Portuguese caravel), more accurate navigation tools (the astrolabe, compass), improved maps, and the development of maritime navigation techniques made long ocean voyages possible.

**Portuguese royal sponsorship**: Portugal's **Prince Henry the Navigator** (1394–1460) systematically sponsored exploration along the African coast — establishing a model of crown-sponsored exploration.

**Religious motivation**: Spreading Christianity was an explicit goal for many explorers and their sponsors.

**Competition between states**: Spain and Portugal competed intensely; France, England, and the Netherlands would follow.

---

## 2. The Portuguese Route to Asia

Portugal led European expansion along the African coast:

**Bartolomeu Dias** rounded the Cape of Good Hope (1488) — proving it was possible to sail around Africa to the Indian Ocean.

**Vasco da Gama** reached India (1498) — opening a direct sea route. The Portuguese established trading posts along the African coast, in the Persian Gulf, and in India, creating a maritime trading empire.

---

## 3. Columbus and the Americas

**Christopher Columbus** (Genoese, sailing for Spain) believed — incorrectly — that Asia could be reached by sailing west across the Atlantic. He encountered the Caribbean islands in 1492, thinking he had reached the "Indies" (hence the term "West Indies" and the misnomer "Indians" for Indigenous Americans).

Columbus made four voyages. He never reached mainland North America and died believing he had found a route to Asia. What he had done was initiate sustained contact between Europe and the Americas — a momentous event Columbus himself didn't fully understand.

**The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)**: Spain and Portugal, mediated by the Pope, divided the non-Christian world between themselves — Portugal controlling the east route to Asia, Spain controlling the west. This is why Brazil (which Portugal claimed) speaks Portuguese while the rest of Latin America speaks Spanish.

---

## 4. The Spanish Conquest of the Americas

The most consequential immediate impact of Columbus's voyages was the Spanish conquest of the Americas:

**Hernán Cortés** defeated the **Aztec Empire** in Mexico (1519–1521) with a small force of Spanish soldiers augmented by thousands of Indigenous allies who resented Aztec domination. Disease (smallpox), which swept through the Aztec population, was decisive.

**Francisco Pizarro** conquered the **Inca Empire** in Peru (1532–1533) through similar methods — deception, violence, and disease.

Spanish America was organized as a colonial system:
- **Encomienda**: Spanish colonizers were granted rights to Indigenous labor
- **Silver mining**: Enormous quantities of silver were extracted, primarily from Potosí (in modern Bolivia), using Indigenous forced labor
- **The Catholic Church**: Missionaries accompanied conquest, converting (often forcibly) Indigenous populations

---

## 5. The Columbian Exchange

The encounter between the Old World and the New World produced the most significant biological exchange in history:

**From the Americas to Europe (and beyond)**:
- **Food crops**: Potatoes, tomatoes, maize, chili peppers, cacao, sweet potatoes — these transformed diets and enabled population growth in Europe, Africa, and Asia
- **Tobacco**

**From Europe to the Americas**:
- **Disease**: Smallpox, measles, typhus, and other diseases killed an estimated 50–90% of the Indigenous population of the Americas — the most catastrophic demographic collapse in human history
- **Livestock**: Horses, cattle, pigs, sheep — transforming Indigenous economies and environments
- **Wheat, sugar cane**

The potato, introduced to Europe from South America, enabled population growth that would not otherwise have been possible. The demographic collapse of the Americas enabled European settlement.

---

## 6. The Atlantic Slave Trade

The labor demands of American plantations — growing sugar (particularly), tobacco, and cotton — combined with the demographic collapse of Indigenous populations led European colonizers to the African slave trade.

Over approximately four centuries (roughly 1500–1900), an estimated 12–15 million Africans were forcibly transported to the Americas. Millions more died during capture and the horrific Middle Passage (the ocean crossing). The slave trade devastated parts of West Africa and built much of the wealth of Atlantic-facing European nations.

---

## Summary

The Age of Exploration connected the world for the first time through European maritime technology and state sponsorship. Portugal pioneered the African route to Asia; Spain colonized the Americas after Columbus's 1492 voyage. The Columbian Exchange transformed global agriculture and ecology — bringing transformative food crops to the Old World while bringing catastrophic disease to the Americas. The demographic collapse of Indigenous Americans facilitated European colonization and created the labor demand that drove the transatlantic slave trade. European exploration began a process of global integration that was simultaneously a story of commercial and technological achievement and of conquest, disease, enslavement, and cultural destruction.
