The two World Wars of the 20th century killed an estimated 85–100 million people combined, destroyed empires, reshaped global borders, and transformed international relations. Together they represent the most catastrophic violence in human history and the foundational events of the modern world order.

---

## 1. World War I: Origins and Course

*See also the U.S. History section on World War I for American perspectives.*

**Origins**: World War I emerged from the collision of militarism, alliance systems, imperial rivalry, and nationalism in Europe. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo (June 1914) triggered the alliance system — Austria-Hungary's conflict with Serbia drew in Russia, then Germany, then France and Britain.

**The Western Front**: Four years of trench warfare produced staggering casualties with minimal territorial change. New weapons — poison gas, machine guns, artillery barrages — made offensive action suicidal. Major battles like Verdun and the Somme killed hundreds of thousands for minimal gain.

**Other Theaters**: The Eastern Front saw more mobile warfare; Russia suffered catastrophic losses before collapsing in revolution (1917), producing the Bolshevik Revolution and the Soviet Union. The Ottoman Empire, allied with Germany, committed genocide against Armenian Christians (1915–1916), killing an estimated 600,000–1.5 million people.

**U.S. Entry (1917)**: American entry provided fresh troops and resources that helped tip the balance.

**Armistice (November 11, 1918)**: Germany and its allies surrendered.

**The Treaty of Versailles (1919)**: Imposed heavy reparations and sole "war guilt" on Germany; stripped it of territory and colonies; created new states from the collapsed empires. German resentment of the treaty's terms would fuel Adolf Hitler's rise.

**Map redrawn**: Four empires (German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, Russian) collapsed. New states — Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia — were created, often with borders that ignored ethnic realities.

---

## 2. The Interwar Period

**The Russian Revolution (1917)**: The Bolsheviks, led by Vladimir Lenin, seized power and created the world's first communist state — the Soviet Union. Lenin and then Stalin built a totalitarian system; Stalin's forced collectivization and purges killed millions.

**The Great Depression (1929–1939)**: The global economic crisis created conditions for political radicalization across Europe. Established governments seemed unable to respond; fascist movements offering order and national renewal gained support.

**Fascism**: A radical authoritarian ideology that emerged in Italy (Mussolini, 1922) and Germany (Hitler, 1933). Fascism was characterized by extreme nationalism, rejection of democracy and communism, glorification of violence, racial hierarchy, and totalitarian control.

**Appeasement**: Britain and France, exhausted from WWI and hoping to avoid another war, acquiesced to Hitler's demands — annexing Austria (1938), then the Sudetenland (Czechoslovakia) at Munich (1938). Hitler took the rest of Czechoslovakia in 1939; Britain and France pledged to defend Poland.

---

## 3. World War II: The European Theater

*See also the U.S. History section on World War II for American perspectives and the Pacific theater.*

**Poland and the fall of France**: Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939; Britain and France declared war. Germany also invaded the Soviet Union (June 1941) — the war's most destructive front.

**The Holocaust**: Nazi Germany systematically murdered approximately 6 million Jews and millions of others — Roma, disabled people, LGBTQ+ individuals, Slavic peoples, political prisoners — in an industrialized genocide unlike anything in history.

**The Eastern Front**: Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union penetrated deep before stalling. The **Battle of Stalingrad** (1942–43) — the bloodiest single battle in history — marked the turning point. Soviet forces counterattacked, pushing Germany back to Berlin.

**D-Day (June 6, 1944)**: Allied amphibious assault on Normandy opened the Western Front. By May 1945, Germany was caught between East and West; Hitler died by suicide; Germany surrendered.

---

## 4. Global Dimensions

World War II was genuinely global:

**China**: Japan had been at war with China since 1937 — the **Nanjing Massacre** (1937) killed an estimated 200,000+ Chinese civilians. China's war was enormous in scale but less central to Western narratives.

**North Africa and Italy**: Allied forces fought Germans and Italians across North Africa before invading Italy (1943).

**Colonial subjects**: Millions of Indian, African, and other colonial subjects served in Allied armies — often fighting for freedoms denied to them in their home countries. The war accelerated the decolonization movement.

---

## 5. The World the Wars Made

The two World Wars produced the world we live in:

**The United Nations (1945)**: Created to promote international peace and cooperation — a successor to the failed League of Nations, this time with American participation.

**Decolonization**: The wars devastated European powers and delegitimized colonial authority; a wave of independence movements followed.

**The Cold War**: The U.S. and Soviet Union, wartime allies, became rivals; nuclear arsenals made direct war existential.

**Israel**: The Holocaust's revelation created moral pressure for Jewish homeland; Israel declared independence in 1948.

**The Nuremberg and Tokyo Trials**: War crimes tribunals established that individuals could be held criminally responsible for atrocities committed under state orders — laying the foundation for international human rights law.

**Nuclear weapons**: The bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki ended the war and inaugurated the nuclear age, permanently changing the character of warfare and international competition.

---

## Summary

World War I emerged from alliance systems, nationalism, and imperial competition; its devastating stalemate and punitive peace created the conditions for World War II. The interwar period saw the Soviet Union, fascism, and the Great Depression reshape the political landscape. World War II was driven by Nazi aggression and ideology — including the systematic murder of 6 million Jews and millions of others in the Holocaust. Together the wars killed tens of millions, destroyed Europe's global dominance, created the Cold War, and produced the international institutions and norms that govern the contemporary world.
