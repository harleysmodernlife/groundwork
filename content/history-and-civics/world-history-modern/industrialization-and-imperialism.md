The Industrial Revolution and European imperialism were the defining forces of the 19th century. Industrialization transformed how goods were made, how people lived and worked, and how economies functioned. The military and economic advantages it created enabled European powers to impose their rule over most of the world — a process with consequences that echo into the present.

---

## 1. The Industrial Revolution (Britain, c. 1760–1840)

**Why Britain first?** Several factors converged: abundant coal and iron deposits; a culture that valued practical innovation; investment capital from colonial trade and agriculture; enclosure of common lands (pushing rural people into cities); a political system that protected property rights; and access to colonial markets for manufactured goods and colonial raw materials.

**The key technologies**:
- **Steam engine** (James Watt, 1769): The transformative technology — using burning coal to produce steam to drive machinery
- **Textile machinery**: The spinning jenny, water frame, and power loom mechanized cloth production
- **Railways**: From the 1820s onward, railways transformed transportation of goods and people; by 1850, Britain had 6,000 miles of track
- **Iron and steel production**: New techniques enabled mass production of structural metal

---

## 2. Social Consequences

Industrialization produced:

**Urbanization**: Factory workers moved to cities. Manchester's population grew from 25,000 to 340,000 between 1772 and 1850. Urban conditions were appalling — overcrowded, with inadequate sanitation, no public health infrastructure, and dangerous factory work.

**The working class**: Factory workers — including children — worked 12–16 hour days in dangerous conditions for subsistence wages. Child labor in mines and factories was common.

**The middle class**: Factory owners, merchants, managers, and professionals formed a new wealthy middle class distinct from the aristocracy — and increasingly demanding political power to match their economic power.

**New economic thought**: **Adam Smith's** *Wealth of Nations* (1776) articulated the case for free markets and the "invisible hand." Later, **Karl Marx** analyzed capitalism's contradictions and predicted its overthrow by the working class (*The Communist Manifesto*, 1848; *Das Kapital*, 1867).

---

## 3. The Spread of Industrialization

By the mid-19th century, industrialization had spread to Belgium, France, Germany, and the United States. The U.S. railroads connected the continent and drove industrial growth. By 1900, Germany and the United States were challenging Britain's industrial dominance.

Japan industrialized rapidly after the Meiji Restoration (1868) — deliberately adopting Western technology and organizational methods to maintain independence.

---

## 4. The New Imperialism (1870–1914)

The "New Imperialism" of the late 19th century differed from earlier European colonialism in its speed and scope. Between 1870 and 1914, European powers colonized most of Africa and much of Asia.

**Why the acceleration?**:
- **Industrial technology** provided decisive military advantage: steam-powered gunboats could penetrate river systems; the Maxim gun gave Europeans overwhelming firepower. "Whatever happens, we have got / The Maxim gun, and they have not" — Hilaire Belloc
- **Raw materials**: Industrializing economies needed cotton, rubber, oil, minerals
- **Markets**: Export markets for manufactured goods
- **Investment opportunities**: Surplus capital sought overseas returns
- **Strategic competition**: European powers competed with each other for colonies as prestige and strategic position

---

## 5. The Scramble for Africa

The **Berlin Conference (1884–85)**: European powers gathered to partition Africa — without inviting any Africans. They drew borders based on European convenience, cutting across ethnic, cultural, and political lines that had defined African societies for centuries.

By 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent. European powers controlled the rest through direct rule or protectorates.

**Colonial systems** varied: direct administration, indirect rule through existing chiefs, and settler colonialism (in South Africa, Algeria, Kenya). All extracted resources for European benefit; all imposed European culture and authority while denying Africans political rights.

---

## 6. Asia Under Imperialism

China, already weakened by internal conflicts, was forced to cede territory and accept humiliating conditions through a series of "unequal treaties" following the Opium Wars (1839–42, 1856–60). European powers carved out "spheres of influence." The **Boxer Rebellion** (1900) — a Chinese uprising against foreign presence — was suppressed by an international military force.

India, controlled by the British East India Company since the 18th century, came under direct British Crown rule after the **Indian Rebellion** (1857) — which the British called the "Indian Mutiny."

**Southeast Asia** was largely colonized: French Indochina (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia), British Burma and Malaya, Dutch Indonesia.

---

## 7. Legacies of Imperialism

The colonial period's legacies persist:
- **Economic extraction** left colonies with raw material-focused economies poorly positioned for development after independence
- **Arbitrary borders** created multiethnic states without historical or cultural coherence — source of many post-independence conflicts
- **Cultural disruption**: Missionary education, forced assimilation, suppression of local cultures and governance
- **Infrastructure**: Colonial powers built railroads, ports, and other infrastructure — primarily to extract resources, not to benefit local populations, though some had lasting utility
- **Racial ideologies**: Scientific racism, developed to justify imperialism, had long afterlives

---

## Summary

The Industrial Revolution transformed production, created the working class and urban poverty, and gave European powers decisive military and economic advantages. The New Imperialism translated those advantages into direct political control over most of Africa and Asia by 1914. Colonial systems extracted resources, disrupted existing political structures, and imposed European authority through violence when necessary. The borders, economies, and political problems of many postcolonial nations were shaped by decisions made in European capitals, with consequences that are still being reckoned with.
