Islam emerged in the 7th century CE in the Arabian Peninsula and within a century had spread across an enormous swath of the world — from Spain to Central Asia. The Islamic world preserved ancient learning, advanced mathematics and science, and created one of history's most sophisticated civilizations.

---

## 1. The Origins of Islam

**Muhammad** (c. 570–632 CE) was born in Mecca, a trading city in the Arabian Peninsula. Around 610 CE, he began receiving revelations he believed were from God (Allah) through the angel Gabriel. These revelations became the **Quran** — Islam's holy text, which Muslims consider the direct word of God.

Muhammad's message was simple and radical: there is one God (monotheism); all people are equal before God; the wealthy and powerful must care for the poor. This was challenging to Mecca's polytheistic merchant elite, who relied on pilgrimage to the Kaaba (a pre-Islamic holy site) for their economy.

Muhammad and his followers were persecuted; in 622 CE, they migrated to Medina — the **Hijra** (migration), which marks the beginning of the Islamic calendar.

Muhammad became both a religious and political leader in Medina. By his death in 632, he had united most of the Arabian Peninsula under Islam.

---

## 2. The Five Pillars of Islam

Muslims are united by five foundational obligations:

1. **Shahada**: Declaration of faith — "There is no god but God, and Muhammad is His messenger"
2. **Salat**: Prayer five times daily, facing Mecca
3. **Zakat**: Almsgiving — required charity to the poor (typically 2.5% of wealth annually)
4. **Sawm**: Fasting during the month of Ramadan (dawn to sunset)
5. **Hajj**: Pilgrimage to Mecca at least once in a lifetime, if able

---

## 3. The Rashidun and Umayyad Caliphates: The Arab Conquests

After Muhammad's death, Muslim leaders disagreed on succession — a dispute that eventually produced the **Sunni-Shia split** (Shia Muslims believed leadership should remain in Muhammad's family; Sunni Muslims accepted the community's chosen leaders).

The first four **Caliphs** (successors) — Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali — are called the Rashidun (rightly guided). Under them and the subsequent **Umayyad Caliphate** (661–750 CE), Arab armies expanded at extraordinary speed:

- **637 CE**: Conquest of Iraq and Persia
- **641 CE**: Conquest of Egypt
- **711 CE**: Invasion of Spain and the Iberian Peninsula
- **732 CE**: Arab advance into France halted by Charles Martel at the Battle of Tours

Within a century of Muhammad's death, the Islamic world stretched from Spain to the borders of China.

---

## 4. The Abbasid Golden Age (750–1258 CE)

The **Abbasid Caliphate** overthrew the Umayyads in 750 CE and shifted the empire's center from Damascus to the newly built city of **Baghdad**. Under the Abbasids, Islamic civilization reached its cultural and intellectual height — the Islamic Golden Age.

**The House of Wisdom** (Bayt al-Hikma) in Baghdad became the world's leading center of learning. Scholars translated Greek, Persian, Indian, and other classical works into Arabic, preserving texts that might otherwise have been lost. Then they advanced beyond them:

- **Mathematics**: Al-Khwarizmi developed algebra (the word comes from his book *Al-Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi Hisab al-Jabr*); the numerals we use today (Arabic numerals) came through the Islamic world
- **Astronomy**: Precise star catalogs; many star names still used today are Arabic
- **Medicine**: Ibn Sina (Avicenna), whose *Canon of Medicine* was used in European universities for centuries; Ibn al-Haytham (optics)
- **Philosophy**: Islamic philosophers preserved, commented on, and extended Aristotle; this work was crucial to the European scholastic tradition
- **Geography**: Al-Idrisi created one of the most accurate maps of the medieval world

---

## 5. The Mongol Invasion and Aftermath

In 1258, Mongol forces under Hulagu Khan sacked Baghdad, killing the Caliph and destroying the Abbasid Caliphate. The House of Wisdom was destroyed; countless texts were burned; the irrigation systems of Iraq were demolished.

The Mongol destruction ended the Abbasid Golden Age, though Islamic civilization continued in other centers — Cairo, Córdoba, Samarkand, Delhi.

---

## 6. Islam's Cultural and Geographic Reach

By the 13th century, Islam had spread through trade and missionary activity well beyond the original Arab conquests:

- **Sub-Saharan Africa**: Mali, Songhai, and other kingdoms converted
- **South Asia**: Muslim traders and conquerors brought Islam to the Indian subcontinent; the **Delhi Sultanate** governed much of northern India
- **Central and Southeast Asia**: Turkic peoples converted; Islam spread through trading networks to Southeast Asia

Islam is today practiced by approximately 1.9 billion people worldwide — the world's second-largest religion.

---

## Summary

Islam emerged in 7th century Arabia through Muhammad's revelations and spread with extraordinary speed through conquest and trade across three continents. The Islamic Golden Age — centered at Baghdad under the Abbasids — preserved ancient learning and advanced mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and philosophy. The Mongol destruction of Baghdad ended this era, but Islamic civilization continued through diverse successor states and continued its geographic spread through trade and conversion. Understanding Islamic history is essential for understanding the medieval world, the origins of the modern Middle East, and the global present.
