Ancient Greece — a civilization of city-states scattered across the Aegean world from roughly 800 to 150 BCE — produced enduring contributions to philosophy, democracy, science, drama, and art that shaped Western civilization profoundly. Understanding ancient Greece means understanding not a unified nation but a competitive, fragmented, and brilliant world of independent poleis.

---

## 1. The Polis: City-State as the Greek World

The fundamental political unit of ancient Greece was the **polis** (plural: *poleis*) — the city-state. Athens, Sparta, Corinth, Thebes, and hundreds of others were each self-governing, with their own laws, currency, religion, and identity. Greeks thought of themselves first as Athenians or Spartans, only secondarily as Hellenes (Greeks).

The poleis competed constantly — militarily, culturally, economically. This competition drove innovation. It also made Greece chronically difficult to unify.

---

## 2. Athens and Democracy

**Athens** (population perhaps 250,000 at its peak, with 30,000–50,000 male citizens eligible to vote) developed the world's first democracy — though by contemporary standards it was profoundly limited.

**Direct democracy**: Athenian male citizens participated directly in governance through the **Assembly** (Ekklesia), where they debated and voted on laws and policy. There were no representatives — citizens were the legislature.

Women, slaves (who comprised perhaps 30% of Athens' population), and resident foreigners (metics) were excluded from political participation entirely.

**Key institutions**:
- **Assembly**: All male citizens could speak and vote
- **Council of 500 (Boule)**: Prepared legislation for the Assembly; selected by lot
- **Courts**: Large citizen juries decided cases
- **Ostracism**: Citizens could vote to exile a political figure for 10 years — a check on dangerous popularity

**Pericles** (c. 495–429 BCE): The dominant Athenian statesman of the Classical era, who oversaw the building of the Parthenon and articulated Athenian democracy's ideals in the famous **Funeral Oration** (recorded by Thucydides).

---

## 3. Sparta: The Warrior State

**Sparta** took a radically different path. The Spartans conquered and enslaved the neighboring population (the **Helots**), who vastly outnumbered them. Permanent readiness to suppress Helot revolt shaped every aspect of Spartan society:

- Male citizens underwent the **agoge** — an intensive military training program beginning at age 7, lasting until 30
- Individual development was suppressed in favor of collective military strength
- Women had unusual freedoms by Greek standards — property rights and physical training — because they needed to produce and raise warriors
- Sparta had no significant literature, philosophy, or art — its "contribution" to Greek civilization was military

The **Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE)**: Athens and Sparta, the two dominant powers, led competing alliances into a prolonged, devastating war that ultimately exhausted Athens.

---

## 4. The Persian Wars

The **Persian Wars (490–479 BCE)** were the defining military crucible of classical Greece.

**Battle of Marathon (490 BCE)**: Athens defeated the first Persian invasion. The Athenian messenger who reportedly ran 26 miles to announce the victory gave us the marathon.

**Thermopylae (480 BCE)**: A small Greek force, including the famous 300 Spartans under Leonidas, held a mountain pass for three days against Xerxes' massive army — allowing Greek forces to regroup. The Spartans died to the last man.

**Salamis (480 BCE)**: Athens' navy, on Themistocles' strategy, destroyed the Persian fleet in a narrow strait.

The Persian Wars produced a surge of Greek confidence and cultural achievement — and Athens' naval power, which Salamis demonstrated, enabled its subsequent empire.

---

## 5. The Golden Age of Athens

The period from roughly 480–404 BCE — the Classical Age — produced extraordinary cultural achievements in Athens:

**Philosophy**:
- **Socrates**: Developed the dialectical method — questioning assumptions to expose contradictions; executed by Athens for "corrupting the youth" (399 BCE)
- **Plato**: Socrates' student; authored dialogues exploring justice, knowledge, the ideal state (*The Republic*)
- **Aristotle**: Plato's student; systematic works on logic, science, ethics, and politics that shaped thought for two millennia

**Drama**:
- **Tragedy**: Aeschylus, Sophocles (*Oedipus Rex, Antigone*), and Euripides explored human fate, ethics, and the gods
- **Comedy**: Aristophanes satirized politics and society

**Architecture**: The **Parthenon** (448–432 BCE), built on the Acropolis under Pericles, remains one of the finest examples of Doric architecture.

**Historiography**: **Thucydides** (*History of the Peloponnesian War*) introduced historical writing based on evidence and human causation rather than divine intervention.

---

## 6. The Hellenistic World

After the Peloponnesian War exhausted Athens and Sparta, **Macedonia** — a northern kingdom — rose to dominance under **Philip II** and then his son **Alexander the Great**.

Alexander's campaigns (334–323 BCE) conquered the Persian Empire, Egypt, and extended Greek power to the borders of India. He died at 32. His successors divided his empire, creating the **Hellenistic kingdoms** — spreading Greek culture (mixed with local traditions) across a vast area from Egypt to Central Asia.

---

## Summary

Ancient Greece — organized around competing city-states rather than a unified nation — produced democracy, Western philosophy, drama, and foundational mathematics and science. Athens developed the first democracy (limited but genuine) and produced the Socratic philosophical tradition. Sparta prioritized military excellence. The Persian Wars demonstrated Greek military capability and cultural confidence. The Classical Age of Athens produced enduring contributions to philosophy, drama, and architecture. Alexander the Great spread Greek culture across an enormous swath of Eurasia.
