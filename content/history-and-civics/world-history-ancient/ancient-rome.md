Rome transformed from a small city on the Tiber River into an empire that encompassed the entire Mediterranean world and endured for centuries. Roman contributions to law, government, engineering, and language shaped Western civilization more durably than perhaps any other ancient society.

---

## 1. The Republic (509–27 BCE)

According to Roman tradition, the Republic began when the Romans expelled their last king in 509 BCE. The Republic created political institutions to prevent any individual from gaining too much power:

**The Senate**: An assembly of aristocrats (patricians) that dominated foreign policy, finances, and legislation. Senators served for life.

**Consuls**: Two elected annually, with equal power and veto over each other (the first checks and balances). They commanded armies and held executive power.

**The Assembly**: Citizens (initially only patricians, then also plebeians) voted on laws and elected officials.

**The Struggle of the Orders**: Conflict between patricians (the hereditary aristocracy) and plebeians (commoners) over political rights. By the 3rd century BCE, plebeians had won the right to hold all major offices and to pass binding legislation (the Lex Hortensia, 287 BCE).

**Expansion**: Rome methodically conquered Italy, then the western Mediterranean (the Punic Wars against Carthage, 264–146 BCE), then the eastern Mediterranean. By the end of the Republic, Rome controlled an empire from Spain to Anatolia.

---

## 2. The Punic Wars

The three **Punic Wars** against Carthage (the great trading empire in North Africa) were the pivotal conflicts of the Roman Republic.

**First Punic War (264–241 BCE)**: Rome built a navy and defeated Carthage, winning Sicily — Rome's first overseas territory.

**Second Punic War (218–201 BCE)**: **Hannibal Barca** led Carthaginian forces across the Alps into Italy with war elephants — one of history's great military feats. He won major victories (Trebia, Lake Trasimene, Cannae) but could not take Rome. **Scipio Africanus** eventually defeated him at Zama in 202 BCE.

**Third Punic War (149–146 BCE)**: Rome destroyed Carthage utterly — killing its population, salting the earth (according to legend). Rome now controlled the western Mediterranean.

---

## 3. The Fall of the Republic

Expansion created new problems. Conquered lands concentrated wealth among the elite; small farmers, unable to compete with slave-worked plantations, lost their land and drifted to cities. Military commanders grew more powerful than the civilian state.

**The Gracchi brothers** (133–121 BCE) attempted land reform; both were killed by conservative senators.

**Marius and Sulla**: Generals turned their armies against each other and against the Roman state. Sulla marched on Rome twice — an act previously unthinkable.

**Julius Caesar**: Brilliant general and politician, Caesar crossed the Rubicon (49 BCE) — an act of treason, bringing his army into Italy. He won the civil war, made himself dictator for life, and was assassinated by senators on March 15, 44 BCE (the Ides of March).

Civil war resumed. **Augustus Caesar** (formerly Octavian) defeated Mark Antony and Cleopatra, emerging as Rome's first emperor (27 BCE).

---

## 4. The Roman Empire

Augustus maintained the forms of the Republic — the Senate, elections, Roman law — while exercising real power as emperor (*princeps*, first citizen). This arrangement was politically skillful: he ruled like a king while letting Romans pretend they were still a republic.

**Pax Romana ("Roman Peace")**, 27 BCE–180 CE: Roughly two centuries of relative stability, prosperity, and expansion. The empire stretched from Britain to Mesopotamia, from the Rhine to the Sahara.

**Roman achievements during the Empire**:
- **Law**: Roman law — codified, systematized, and applied consistently across vast territories — is the foundation of most European legal systems today
- **Engineering**: Aqueducts, roads (over 50,000 miles), concrete, the arch, amphitheaters (the Colosseum)
- **Infrastructure**: Roman roads connected the empire; still visible in parts of Europe
- **Language**: Latin evolved into Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, and Romanian; heavily influenced English through the Normans and church Latin

**The Colosseum** (completed 80 CE): Held 50,000 spectators; hosted gladiatorial combat, animal hunts, and public executions — entertainment as display of imperial power.

---

## 5. Christianity and Rome

Jesus of Nazareth was executed by Roman authorities in Judea around 30 CE. The religion founded in his name spread through Roman trade networks and the Greek-speaking world.

Romans initially persecuted Christians — their refusal to worship Roman gods was seen as dangerous to social order. But the religion spread. **Emperor Constantine** converted to Christianity (312 CE) and issued the Edict of Milan (313 CE) granting religious tolerance. Christianity became the official religion of the Empire under **Theodosius** (380 CE).

The **Council of Nicaea** (325 CE) standardized Christian doctrine — an example of imperial power shaping religion.

---

## 6. The Decline and Fall of the Western Empire

The Western Roman Empire declined gradually from the 3rd century onward. Causes debated by historians include:
- Political instability (constant civil war, "crisis of the third century")
- Economic stagnation and overextension
- Military pressure from Germanic peoples on the frontier
- Epidemic disease

In 476 CE, the last Western Roman emperor was deposed by a Germanic chieftain — traditionally the date of Rome's fall. The **Eastern Roman Empire** (Byzantine Empire), centered in Constantinople, continued until 1453 CE.

---

## Summary

Rome evolved from city-state to republic to empire over roughly a thousand years. The Republic's political institutions — balanced power, law, representative assemblies — provided a model for later governments. The Empire unified the Mediterranean under Roman law, engineering, and culture. Roman contributions to law, language, architecture, and political thought are among the most durable legacies of the ancient world. Christianity, transformed by its relationship to the empire, became the West's dominant religion through Roman channels.
