The United States federal government is divided into three branches: legislative, executive, and judicial. This separation of powers was a deliberate design choice — the framers of the Constitution feared concentrated power and built a system where each branch limits the others.

---

## 1. The Legislative Branch: Congress

**Article I** of the Constitution establishes Congress as the lawmaking body. Congress is **bicameral** — two chambers.

### The House of Representatives

- 435 members, apportioned by state population (larger states have more Representatives)
- 2-year terms (all seats up for election every two years)
- Members must be 25, a citizen for 7 years, and a resident of the state they represent
- The House has the **power to originate revenue bills** and the **power of impeachment**
- The Speaker of the House leads the chamber

### The Senate

- 100 members — two senators per state regardless of population
- 6-year terms, staggered so about one-third are elected every two years
- Members must be 30, a citizen for 9 years, and a resident of their state
- The Senate confirms presidential appointments (judges, cabinet members, ambassadors) and ratifies treaties
- The Senate tries impeachment cases
- The Vice President is the president of the Senate (votes only in case of a tie); day-to-day leadership falls to the Senate Majority Leader

### Congressional Powers

Congress can:
- Pass legislation (both chambers must pass identical bills)
- Levy taxes
- Borrow money
- Regulate interstate and foreign commerce
- Declare war
- Maintain the military
- Override presidential vetoes (two-thirds vote of both chambers)
- Propose constitutional amendments (two-thirds of both chambers)

**The "necessary and proper" clause** (Article I, Section 8) allows Congress to make laws needed to carry out its enumerated powers — source of much debate about the scope of congressional authority.

---

## 2. The Executive Branch: The President

**Article II** establishes the executive branch, headed by the President.

The President:
- Is elected to 4-year terms (limited to two terms by the 22nd Amendment)
- Must be 35, a natural-born citizen, and a resident for 14 years
- Is elected through the **Electoral College**, not direct popular vote
- Appoints federal judges, cabinet members, and ambassadors (with Senate confirmation)
- Commands the military as **Commander in Chief**
- Signs or vetoes legislation
- Issues **executive orders** (directives to the executive branch, with force of law, but can be overturned by Congress or courts)
- Conducts foreign policy and negotiates treaties (requiring Senate ratification)
- Grants pardons for federal crimes

The executive branch also includes the **Vice President**, the **Cabinet** (heads of 15 executive departments), and a vast array of federal agencies.

---

## 3. The Judicial Branch: The Courts

**Article III** establishes the Supreme Court and authorizes Congress to create lower federal courts.

### The Supreme Court

- 9 justices (not specified in the Constitution — set by Congress; hasn't changed since 1869)
- Nominated by the President, confirmed by the Senate
- **Life tenure** ("during good behavior") — designed to insulate justices from political pressure
- Has **original jurisdiction** in cases involving states and foreign diplomats; **appellate jurisdiction** over lower court decisions
- Issues opinions that interpret federal law and the Constitution

### Lower Federal Courts

- **94 U.S. District Courts**: Trial-level federal courts
- **13 U.S. Courts of Appeals (Circuit Courts)**: Hear appeals from district courts
- Specialized courts: tax court, bankruptcy courts, court of international trade

### Judicial Review

Not explicitly written in the Constitution, **judicial review** — the power to strike down laws that violate the Constitution — was established in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803). It is now the Supreme Court's most significant power.

---

## 4. Why Three Branches?

The framers were influenced by Montesquieu's principle of separation of powers and by their own experience under British rule and under the Articles of Confederation. They had seen what happened when legislative power was unchecked (Parliament's authority over the colonies) and when executive power was unchecked (royal prerogative).

The solution was to give each branch distinct powers and give each branch tools to limit the others — checks and balances.

---

## Summary

The legislative branch (Congress) makes laws; the executive branch (the President) enforces and administers them; the judicial branch (the courts) interprets them. Each branch has powers the others don't, and each has tools to constrain the others. This system was designed to prevent tyranny by distributing power and building in mutual accountability. Understanding the three branches is foundational to understanding how American government actually works.
