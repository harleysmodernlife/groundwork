Elections are how Americans choose their representatives and hold government accountable. The mechanics of voting, the structure of elections, and the ways participation is encouraged or discouraged are central to whether democracy actually works.

---

## 1. Who Can Vote?

**Eligibility requirements** (federal):
- U.S. citizen
- At least 18 years old (26th Amendment, 1971)
- Meet state residency requirements

**History of voting rights expansion**:
- **1870 (15th Amendment)**: Race cannot be used to deny the right to vote
- **1920 (19th Amendment)**: Women gain the right to vote
- **1964 (24th Amendment)**: Poll taxes banned in federal elections
- **1965 (Voting Rights Act)**: Prohibited discriminatory voting practices; required federal pre-clearance for certain states with history of discrimination
- **1971 (26th Amendment)**: Voting age lowered to 18 (during Vietnam War debate about drafting non-voters)

Despite these expansions, access to voting has been contested continuously — through Jim Crow laws, voter suppression, and ongoing debates about voter ID requirements, polling place availability, and felony disenfranchisement.

---

## 2. Voter Registration

Unlike most democracies, the U.S. requires voters to register in advance. Requirements vary by state:

- **Deadline**: Some states allow same-day registration; most require registration weeks before election day
- **Method**: In-person, by mail, or online (state-dependent)
- **Automatic registration**: Some states automatically register eligible citizens through government transactions (DMV, etc.)

Registration is often cited as a barrier to participation. The U.S. has lower turnout than many comparable democracies, in part because of the registration requirement.

---

## 3. Types of Elections

**Primary elections**: Held within parties to select candidates for the general election. Different types:
- **Open primary**: Any registered voter can vote in either party's primary
- **Closed primary**: Only registered party members can vote in that party's primary
- **Runoff primary**: When no candidate reaches a required threshold, the top two compete again

**General elections**: The final election between candidates from different parties (or independents).

**Special elections**: Held to fill vacancies outside the regular election cycle.

**Local elections**: Often held on different dates from federal elections; tend to have much lower turnout.

---

## 4. The Electoral College

Presidents are not elected by direct popular vote. Instead:

1. Each state receives electoral votes equal to its total congressional delegation (House + Senate seats) — at minimum 3, up to 54 for California
2. Washington D.C. receives 3 electoral votes (23rd Amendment)
3. **Total: 538 electoral votes**; a majority of 270 is needed to win

In all but two states (Maine and Nebraska use congressional district methods), it's **winner-take-all**: the candidate who wins the state's popular vote gets all that state's electoral votes.

**Faithless electors** (those who don't vote as pledged) are rare and legally restricted in many states.

The Electoral College means candidates focus on competitive "swing states" rather than all states. It has produced presidents who lost the popular vote (2000: Gore vs. Bush; 2016: Clinton vs. Trump).

---

## 5. Congressional Elections

Members of Congress are elected by their districts or states:

- **House**: 435 single-member districts, each choosing one representative by **plurality vote** (most votes wins, not necessarily a majority). **Redistricting** — redrawing district lines — happens after each census and is controlled by state legislatures, creating opportunities for **gerrymandering** (drawing districts to favor one party).

- **Senate**: Statewide elections. Senators represent the whole state.

**Midterm elections** (two years into a presidential term) typically see lower turnout but often shift the balance of power in Congress.

---

## 6. Campaign Finance

Money in politics is extensive and contested:

- **Citizens United v. FEC (2010)**: Supreme Court ruled corporations and unions can spend unlimited amounts on political advertising (not coordinated with campaigns)
- **Super PACs**: Organizations that can raise and spend unlimited money but (in theory) must operate independently of candidates
- **Disclosure**: Rules about what must be disclosed and by whom are frequently contested

Campaign finance is a major ongoing debate about the relationship between money, speech, and democratic equality.

---

## 7. Voting Methods

- **In-person on election day**: Traditional; requires appearing at a designated polling place
- **Early voting**: In-person voting before election day; available in most states
- **Absentee / mail voting**: Voting by mail; all states offer it under some conditions; some conduct elections entirely by mail

After COVID-19, mail voting expanded significantly; debates about its security and accessibility continue.

---

## Summary

Voting is the primary mechanism of democratic accountability. Eligibility has expanded substantially since the founding but remains contested. The Electoral College — not the popular vote — determines the president. Congressional elections are shaped by single-member districts and gerrymandering. Registration requirements, polling access, and campaign finance rules all affect who participates and how. Participation in elections — at local, state, and federal levels — is one of the most consequential civic acts available to eligible voters.
