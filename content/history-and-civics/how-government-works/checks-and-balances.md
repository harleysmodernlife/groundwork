Separation of powers divides authority among three branches of government. But separation alone isn't enough — a branch with unchecked power could still become tyrannical even if it didn't share authority with others. **Checks and balances** ensure that each branch has specific tools to limit the others, making it difficult for any one branch to accumulate or abuse power.

---

## 1. The Logic of Checks and Balances

James Madison wrote in *Federalist No. 51*: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." The framers didn't assume those in power would always act virtuously. They built a system in which the institutional interests of each branch would motivate it to resist overreach by the others.

The result is a system that is deliberately inefficient. Making law requires agreement between the House, the Senate, and the President (or enough of Congress to override a veto). This friction was intentional — it prevents hasty or minority-driven action.

---

## 2. Legislative Checks

Congress has tools to limit both the executive and judicial branches:

**On the Executive**:
- **Override veto**: A presidential veto can be overridden by two-thirds of both chambers
- **Control of the purse**: Congress appropriates all money; the President cannot spend what Congress hasn't authorized
- **Impeachment**: The House can impeach (formally charge) the President, Vice President, and civil officers; the Senate tries impeachment cases and can remove by two-thirds vote
- **Senate confirmation**: Presidential appointments to the Supreme Court, cabinet, and ambassadorial positions require Senate approval
- **Treaty ratification**: Two-thirds of the Senate must approve all treaties

**On the Judiciary**:
- Congress sets the number of Supreme Court justices (currently 9)
- Congress establishes lower federal courts
- Congress can propose constitutional amendments to override judicial interpretations
- Senate confirms all federal judges

---

## 3. Executive Checks

The President has tools to limit Congress and the courts:

**On Congress**:
- **Veto**: The President can reject legislation; Congress must achieve a two-thirds supermajority to override
- **Agenda-setting**: The President's annual address to Congress and budget proposals shape legislative priorities
- **Executive orders**: Presidents can direct the executive branch without legislation, though Congress can pass countervailing legislation

**On the Judiciary**:
- Nominates all federal judges, including Supreme Court justices
- Can pardon individuals convicted of federal crimes (though not for state crimes or future crimes)

---

## 4. Judicial Checks

The courts have tools to limit both Congress and the executive:

**On Congress**:
- **Judicial review**: Courts can strike down legislation that violates the Constitution (*Marbury v. Madison*, 1803)

**On the Executive**:
- Can rule presidential actions unconstitutional (*Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*, 1952, limiting President Truman's seizure of steel mills)
- Can rule executive orders unlawful

---

## 5. Checks in Practice: Key Examples

**Watergate (1972–1974)**: After the Nixon administration's abuses of power, Congress investigated under its oversight authority, courts ruled against claims of executive privilege, and Nixon resigned before facing near-certain impeachment.

**DACA (2012–)**: President Obama issued an executive order protecting "Dreamers" (undocumented immigrants brought to the US as children). Subsequent presidents attempted to rescind it; courts limited those efforts; Congress has repeatedly failed to pass legislation to settle the question — the three-branch tension continues.

**Supreme Court appointments**: Senate refusal to hold hearings for Merrick Garland (2016) and the confirmation of three Trump-appointed justices (2017–2020) reshaped the Court — demonstrating how the confirmation process is itself a political check.

**Presidential impeachments**: Andrew Johnson (1868), Bill Clinton (1998), and Donald Trump (2019, 2021) were all impeached by the House but acquitted by the Senate.

---

## 6. Tensions and Critiques

The system produces gridlock — which is sometimes a feature (preventing bad laws) and sometimes a bug (preventing necessary ones). Critics from across the political spectrum argue:

- Executive power has expanded dramatically over time, through executive orders, administrative agencies, and war powers, beyond what the framers intended
- Judicial review gives unelected judges immense power — some argue too much
- Partisan polarization has weakened some checks; a Senate unwilling to challenge a president of its own party provides less effective oversight

These tensions are ongoing features of American government, not anomalies.

---

## Summary

Checks and balances give each branch specific tools to limit the others — not just separate spheres, but mechanisms of mutual constraint. Congress can override vetoes, impeach officials, and control spending. The President can veto legislation, nominate judges, and issue executive orders. Courts can strike down laws and executive actions as unconstitutional. The system creates friction by design, requiring cooperation across institutions to govern effectively. How effectively these checks function in practice depends on political will, public attention, and the willingness of institutions to use the tools they have.
