The federal judiciary is the third branch of government — tasked with interpreting the law and the Constitution. At its apex is the Supreme Court, whose decisions shape American law and life in ways that extend far beyond any individual case.

---

## 1. The Structure of Federal Courts

The federal court system has three main levels:

**U.S. District Courts (94)**
The trial-level federal courts. Most federal cases begin here. District courts hear evidence, conduct trials, and make initial rulings. There is at least one district court in every state.

**U.S. Courts of Appeals (13 circuits)**
The intermediate appellate courts. They review district court decisions but do not hold new trials — they examine whether the law was applied correctly. Decisions are usually made by panels of three judges. The Supreme Court hears far fewer cases than the courts of appeals; the circuit courts are effectively final for most federal cases.

**U.S. Supreme Court**
Nine justices, one Chief Justice and eight Associate Justices. The Supreme Court has:
- **Original jurisdiction** in a narrow set of cases (cases involving states, or foreign ambassadors)
- **Appellate jurisdiction** over lower court decisions

The Supreme Court receives around 8,000–10,000 petitions per year and agrees to hear roughly 60–80. It grants review by **writ of certiorari** — and requires 4 justices to agree to hear a case (the "rule of four").

---

## 2. Federal Judges

All federal judges — including Supreme Court justices — are:
- Nominated by the President
- Confirmed by the Senate (simple majority vote)
- Appointed for **life tenure** ("during good behavior"), removable only by impeachment

Life tenure insulates judges from political pressure and public opinion. The tradeoff: there is no direct democratic accountability.

The confirmation process has become intensely political. Nominees' judicial philosophies, past rulings, and views on contested legal questions are scrutinized extensively.

---

## 3. How Cases Reach Federal Courts

Federal courts have **subject matter jurisdiction** over:
- Cases arising under the Constitution or federal law
- Cases involving the United States as a party
- Cases between states
- Cases between citizens of different states (if the amount exceeds $75,000)

State courts handle most law in the United States — criminal law, contracts, property, family law. Federal courts handle cases with federal legal questions.

---

## 4. Judicial Review

The most significant power of the federal courts is **judicial review** — the authority to strike down laws that violate the Constitution. This power is not explicitly stated in the Constitution; it was established in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) by Chief Justice John Marshall.

The logic: the Constitution is supreme law. If a legislative act conflicts with the Constitution, courts must give effect to the Constitution and disregard the act. Otherwise the Constitution is just another law, not supreme law.

Judicial review makes the courts a check on both Congress and the executive branch. Laws passed by overwhelming majorities can be struck down if they violate constitutional rights or limits.

---

## 5. The Supreme Court's Significance

Supreme Court decisions shape American life permanently. Some landmark decisions:

- **McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)**: Established implied powers; federal supremacy
- **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**: Established judicial review
- **Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)**: Denied citizenship to Black Americans; helped precipitate the Civil War
- **Plessy v. Ferguson (1896)**: Upheld "separate but equal" racial segregation
- **Brown v. Board of Education (1954)**: Overturned *Plessy*; declared school segregation unconstitutional
- **Miranda v. Arizona (1966)**: Required warnings about rights upon arrest
- **Roe v. Wade (1973)**: Recognized right to abortion; overturned by *Dobbs v. Jackson* (2022)
- **Obergefell v. Hodges (2015)**: Recognized right to same-sex marriage

---

## 6. Constitutional Interpretation

Justices interpret the Constitution using different approaches:

**Originalism**: The text means what it meant when written. Judges should not go beyond the original public meaning.

**Living constitutionalism**: Constitutional principles evolve with society. The 14th Amendment's "equal protection," for example, should be read in light of contemporary understandings of equality.

These are not always as distinct in practice as in theory, and most justices employ elements of both approaches.

---

## Summary

The federal judiciary — district courts, courts of appeals, and the Supreme Court — interprets federal law and the Constitution. Life-tenured judges, nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate, are meant to be insulated from political pressure. Judicial review — the power to strike down unconstitutional laws — is the judiciary's most significant check on the other branches. Supreme Court decisions are final and nationwide, making the Court's membership and philosophy consequential far beyond any single legal dispute.
