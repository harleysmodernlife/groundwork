Congress is the national legislature — the branch of government that makes federal law. Understanding how Congress is structured and how legislation actually moves from an idea to a signed law is essential for civic literacy.

---

## 1. The Structure of Congress

Congress is **bicameral** — two chambers that must both agree for a bill to become law.

**The House of Representatives** (435 members) represents population. Each state has representatives in proportion to its population, with at least one regardless of size. Representatives serve two-year terms, so the entire House faces election every two years.

**The Senate** (100 members, 2 per state) represents states equally. Senators serve six-year terms, with about one-third elected every two years.

This structure was the **Great Compromise** of the Constitutional Convention — small states insisted on equal representation; large states insisted on proportional representation. The Senate satisfies the first; the House satisfies the second.

---

## 2. Congressional Leadership

**House of Representatives**:
- **Speaker of the House**: Elected by House members; most powerful position in Congress; third in presidential succession
- **Majority and Minority Leaders**: Manage floor strategy for each party
- **Whips**: Mobilize votes

**Senate**:
- **Vice President**: Constitutional president of the Senate (votes only in ties)
- **President Pro Tempore**: Presides in the VP's absence (usually the most senior majority senator)
- **Senate Majority Leader**: Most powerful Senate position; sets the legislative agenda
- **Minority Leader** and party whips

---

## 3. Congressional Committees

Most legislative work happens in **committees** — smaller groups that specialize in specific areas (judiciary, finance, foreign relations, armed services). Committees:
- Hold hearings on proposed legislation
- Invite expert testimony
- Mark up (amend) bills
- Decide whether to send bills to the full chamber for a vote

**Standing committees** are permanent; **select committees** are created for specific purposes; **joint committees** involve both chambers; **conference committees** reconcile differences between House and Senate versions of a bill.

Committee chairs are powerful gatekeepers — a bill that doesn't pass committee usually goes nowhere.

---

## 4. How a Bill Becomes Law

The process is deliberately complex, with many points where legislation can fail.

**Step 1 — Introduction**: Any member can introduce a bill (in their respective chamber). It receives a number (H.R. for House bills, S. for Senate).

**Step 2 — Committee referral**: The bill is sent to the relevant committee (or committees).

**Step 3 — Committee action**: The committee studies the bill, holds hearings, marks it up, and votes. If approved, it moves to the full chamber. Many bills die in committee.

**Step 4 — Floor debate and amendment**: The full chamber debates and can amend the bill. The House uses more structured rules (the Rules Committee sets terms for debate); the Senate allows more open debate.

**Step 5 — Vote**: Both chambers must pass the bill. If they pass different versions, a **conference committee** reconciles the differences, and both chambers must approve the reconciled version.

**Step 6 — Presidential action**: The President has 10 days (Sundays excluded) to:
- **Sign** the bill into law
- **Veto** it (returns to Congress, which can override with two-thirds of both chambers)
- Do nothing (the bill becomes law if Congress is in session; if Congress adjourns, a **pocket veto** kills the bill)

---

## 5. The Filibuster

In the Senate, a single senator can extend debate indefinitely to prevent a vote — this is the **filibuster**. To end a filibuster requires **cloture**: a vote of 60 senators. This means most significant Senate legislation effectively requires 60 votes, not a simple majority, giving the minority significant blocking power.

The filibuster is not in the Constitution; it's a Senate rule. Major exceptions:
- Budget reconciliation bills require only 51 votes
- Supreme Court and federal judicial confirmations were changed to 51 votes (2013 for lower courts, 2017 for Supreme Court)

---

## 6. Congressional Powers Beyond Legislation

Congress does much beyond passing laws:

- **Oversight**: Monitoring executive branch agencies, holding hearings, investigating
- **Appropriations**: All federal spending requires congressional approval; Congress controls the budget
- **Confirmation**: Senate confirms presidential nominees to major positions
- **Ratification**: Senate ratifies treaties by two-thirds vote
- **Impeachment and removal**: House impeaches; Senate tries

---

## Summary

Congress creates federal law through a bicameral structure that requires both the House and Senate to agree. Bills go through committee review, floor debate, and vote in both chambers before reaching the President, who can sign or veto. The filibuster in the Senate effectively raises the threshold for most legislation to 60 votes. Beyond legislation, Congress holds broad powers of oversight, appropriation, confirmation, and impeachment — making it the most powerful branch of government, though one that often struggles to act decisively.
