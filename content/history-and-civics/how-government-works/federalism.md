Federalism is the division of power between a central (federal) government and regional (state) governments. In the United States, both levels of government have their own authority, and neither can simply absorb the other. This structure was designed to allow unity on national issues while preserving local variation and serving as an additional check on concentrated power.

---

## 1. Why Federalism?

The framers had two concerns pulling in opposite directions. On one side: the federal government under the Articles of Confederation was too weak to function — it couldn't tax, couldn't enforce its decisions, and couldn't regulate interstate commerce. On the other: giving the federal government too much power risked recreating the tyranny of a distant central authority (as they had experienced under British rule).

Federalism was the compromise: a strong federal government for national purposes, with states retaining significant autonomous authority.

---

## 2. Constitutional Basis

### Enumerated Powers (Article I, Section 8)

The federal government has the powers explicitly listed in the Constitution: levying taxes, regulating interstate and foreign commerce, coining money, declaring war, establishing post offices, and others.

### The Necessary and Proper Clause

Also in Article I, Section 8: Congress may make laws "necessary and proper" for carrying out its enumerated powers. This clause has been broadly interpreted, significantly expanding federal authority over time.

### The Supremacy Clause (Article VI)

The Constitution, federal laws, and treaties are the "supreme law of the land." When federal and state law conflict, federal law prevails.

### The 10th Amendment

"The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." This is the constitutional statement of state authority.

### The Commerce Clause

The federal government's power to regulate interstate commerce has been interpreted expansively since the New Deal, allowing federal regulation of everything from labor standards to civil rights.

---

## 3. Types of Powers

- **Enumerated (expressed) powers**: Specifically listed in the Constitution — belonging to the federal government
- **Implied powers**: Not listed, but reasonably inferred from enumerated powers (*McCulloch v. Maryland*, 1819 — establishing the national bank)
- **Concurrent powers**: Held by both federal and state governments — taxing, building roads, establishing courts
- **Reserved powers**: Retained by the states under the 10th Amendment — education, criminal law, marriage law, property law

---

## 4. How Federalism Has Evolved

**Layer Cake vs. Marble Cake**: Early federalism was more "layer cake" — clear separation of federal and state authority. Modern federalism resembles "marble cake" — federal and state functions are deeply intertwined.

**The Civil War**: The war settled (by force) the question of whether states could leave the Union. It also established federal supremacy on the question of slavery.

**New Deal expansion**: Roosevelt's New Deal programs dramatically expanded federal authority, particularly through broad interpretation of the Commerce Clause. The Supreme Court initially resisted, then acquiesced.

**Post-New Deal**: Federal authority continued to expand — Medicare, Medicaid, the Civil Rights Act (overriding state segregation laws), the Voting Rights Act. Federal grants to states (with conditions attached) became a major mechanism of federal influence.

**Recent federalism tensions**: States have asserted authority on marijuana (some legalizing despite federal prohibition), immigration enforcement, environmental regulations, and other issues, while the federal government continues to use funding conditions to influence state policy.

---

## 5. Federal Tools to Influence States

States are sovereign in their reserved powers — but the federal government uses several indirect tools:

- **Grants-in-aid**: Federal money given to states, often with conditions (comply with federal requirements or lose funding)
- **Unfunded mandates**: Federal requirements states must comply with, without corresponding federal funding
- **Preemption**: Federal law displacing state law in the same area

---

## 6. Advantages and Disadvantages of Federalism

**Advantages**:
- Allows policy experimentation ("laboratories of democracy") — states can try different approaches
- Preserves local variation and local responsiveness
- Provides an additional check on federal power
- Easier to participate in state government (closer to citizens)

**Disadvantages**:
- Creates complexity and inconsistency (50 different laws on the same issue)
- Has historically been used to maintain inequality (state sovereignty arguments used to defend slavery and segregation)
- Can produce a "race to the bottom" in taxation and regulation
- Diffuses responsibility — who is accountable for what?

---

## Summary

Federalism divides power between the federal government and the states, with each level retaining its own sphere of authority. The federal government has enumerated powers, implied powers, and supremacy over conflicting state law; states retain reserved powers under the 10th Amendment. The system has evolved significantly over two centuries, with federal authority expanding substantially since the New Deal. Federalism creates genuine benefits — experimentation, local variation, additional checks — and genuine problems, including complexity, inconsistency, and a history of being used to shield injustice.
