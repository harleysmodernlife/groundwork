Variation is the raw material of evolution. Without differences between individuals, natural selection would have nothing to act on. Mutations — heritable changes in DNA — are the ultimate source of all genetic variation, and their effects range from invisible to devastating to occasionally beneficial.

---

## 1. What Is a Mutation?

A **mutation** is any permanent change in the nucleotide sequence of DNA. Mutations can occur in:
- **Somatic cells** (body cells): affect only the individual; not passed to offspring. Can cause cancer.
- **Germ cells** (sperm and egg): potentially passed to offspring and may become part of the heritable gene pool.

---

## 2. Types of Mutations

### Point Mutations (Single-Base Changes)

- **Silent (synonymous) mutation**: the base change codes for the same amino acid due to redundancy in the genetic code. No effect on protein.
- **Missense mutation**: codes for a different amino acid. Effect depends on which amino acid changed and where in the protein. May be neutral, deleterious, or (rarely) beneficial. Example: sickle cell disease is caused by a single missense mutation — one amino acid changed in hemoglobin.
- **Nonsense mutation**: creates a premature stop codon, truncating the protein. Usually severely disruptive.

### Insertions and Deletions (Indels)

Adding or removing one or more bases. If the number is not a multiple of three, it causes a **frameshift mutation** — every codon from that point forward is misread, usually producing a completely nonfunctional protein. Frameshift mutations are generally more disruptive than point mutations.

### Chromosomal Mutations

Large-scale changes involving segments of chromosomes:
- **Deletion**: loss of a chromosome segment
- **Duplication**: a segment is copied and added to the chromosome
- **Inversion**: a segment is flipped 180° and reinserted
- **Translocation**: a segment moves from one chromosome to a non-homologous chromosome

Chromosomal mutations often have severe effects because they alter the dosage or arrangement of many genes simultaneously.

### Changes in Chromosome Number

**Polyploidy**: having extra sets of chromosomes (3n, 4n, etc.). Lethal in most animals but common in plants — many crop species including wheat, cotton, and strawberries are polyploids.

**Aneuploidy**: having extra or missing individual chromosomes.
- **Trisomy**: three copies of a chromosome instead of two. Down syndrome = trisomy 21; caused by nondisjunction during meiosis.
- **Monosomy**: only one copy of a chromosome. Most monosomies are lethal in humans; Turner syndrome (45, X0) is a rare exception.

---

## 3. Causes of Mutations

**Spontaneous mutations** arise from errors during DNA replication (the polymerase occasionally inserts the wrong base) or from the inherent chemical instability of DNA.

**Induced mutations** result from exposure to **mutagens**:
- **Physical mutagens**: UV radiation (causes pyrimidine dimers in DNA), ionizing radiation (X-rays, gamma rays — causes double-strand breaks and oxidative damage)
- **Chemical mutagens**: base analogs (mimic normal bases and cause errors), alkylating agents (modify bases chemically), intercalating agents (insert between bases and cause frameshifts)
- **Biological mutagens**: transposons ("jumping genes" that insert into other genes), viruses that integrate into host DNA

---

## 4. DNA Repair

Cells have multiple mechanisms to detect and repair DNA damage before mutations become permanent:

- **Proofreading**: DNA polymerase checks each newly added base and removes errors; reduces error rate to ~1 per billion
- **Mismatch repair**: enzymes scan newly synthesized DNA for mismatched base pairs and correct them
- **Nucleotide excision repair**: removes bulky lesions (like UV-induced dimers) by cutting out a segment of the damaged strand and resynthesizing it
- **Double-strand break repair**: broken chromosomes are repaired through homologous recombination (using the sister chromatid as a template) or non-homologous end joining (error-prone)

Inherited defects in DNA repair genes dramatically increase cancer risk. BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations impair double-strand break repair and substantially raise the risk of breast and ovarian cancer.

---

## 5. Sources of Genetic Variation

Mutations are the ultimate source of new genetic variants, but most variation in sexually reproducing populations arises from three processes during meiosis and fertilization:

- **Crossing over**: exchanges segments between homologous chromosomes during meiosis I, creating new combinations of alleles on each chromosome
- **Independent assortment**: random orientation of chromosome pairs at meiosis I produces gametes with different combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes (2²³ ≈ 8 million possible combinations in humans)
- **Random fertilization**: any sperm can fertilize any egg, multiplying the potential combinations further

The result is that every human (except identical twins) has a unique genotype — a combination of alleles never before seen in the history of life.

---

## 6. Effects of Mutations on Fitness

The effects of mutations on an organism's survival and reproduction (its **fitness**) span a spectrum:

- **Neutral**: no effect on fitness. Most mutations in non-coding DNA or that produce synonymous codons fall here.
- **Deleterious**: reduce fitness. Most mutations in protein-coding regions fall here — evolution has already optimized proteins, so random changes tend to disrupt function.
- **Lethal**: cause death before reproduction. These are rapidly eliminated from the gene pool.
- **Beneficial**: improve fitness in a given environment. Rare, but these are the raw material for adaptive evolution. Example: mutations conferring antibiotic resistance in bacteria.

The same mutation can be beneficial, neutral, or deleterious depending on the environment. The sickle cell allele is deleterious in two copies but protective against malaria in one copy — a clear example of environment determining the fitness effect of a mutation.

---

## Summary

Mutations are heritable DNA sequence changes — point mutations, indels, chromosomal rearrangements, and changes in chromosome number. They are caused by replication errors, radiation, and chemical mutagens, and are largely corrected by DNA repair systems. Most mutations are neutral or deleterious; beneficial mutations are rare but essential for evolution. Genetic variation in sexually reproducing populations also arises from crossing over, independent assortment, and random fertilization during meiosis.
