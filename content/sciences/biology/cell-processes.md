Cells are not passive containers — they are chemical factories operating continuously, converting energy, building molecules, responding to signals, and maintaining the conditions necessary for life. The core processes of cellular metabolism — photosynthesis and cellular respiration — are the engines that power virtually all life on Earth.

---

## 1. Energy and ATP

Cells run on **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate) — a small, energy-carrying molecule. When ATP releases energy, it loses a phosphate group and becomes **ADP** (adenosine diphosphate). ATP is regenerated from ADP by coupling it to energy-releasing reactions, primarily cellular respiration.

ATP is not stored in large quantities. A cell continually produces and consumes it. A human body turns over roughly its own body weight in ATP every day.

---

## 2. Cellular Respiration

**Cellular respiration** is the process by which cells extract energy from glucose and store it as ATP. The overall equation:

**C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ~36–38 ATP**

It occurs in three stages:

### Stage 1: Glycolysis (Cytoplasm)

Glucose (6 carbons) is split into two molecules of **pyruvate** (3 carbons each). This produces a net gain of **2 ATP** and 2 NADH (electron carriers). Glycolysis requires no oxygen — it is **anaerobic** and occurs in all cells.

### Stage 2: Citric Acid Cycle / Krebs Cycle (Mitochondrial Matrix)

Each pyruvate is converted to **acetyl-CoA** (releasing CO₂) and then fed into the citric acid cycle, which extracts more electrons (stored in NADH and FADH₂) and releases the remaining carbon as CO₂. Produces 2 more ATP per glucose.

### Stage 3: Electron Transport Chain and Oxidative Phosphorylation (Inner Mitochondrial Membrane)

NADH and FADH₂ deliver electrons to the electron transport chain — a series of protein complexes in the inner mitochondrial membrane. As electrons move down the chain, they pump protons (H⁺) across the membrane, building a concentration gradient. Protons flow back through **ATP synthase**, generating ATP. This stage produces ~32–34 ATP per glucose.

Oxygen is the final **electron acceptor** at the end of the chain — it combines with electrons and protons to form water. Without oxygen, the chain stops.

### Anaerobic Respiration and Fermentation

When oxygen is unavailable, cells cannot use the electron transport chain. They must regenerate NAD⁺ (needed for glycolysis to continue) through **fermentation**:
- **Lactic acid fermentation**: pyruvate is reduced to lactate; used by muscle cells during intense exercise and by bacteria (used to make yogurt, cheese)
- **Alcoholic fermentation**: pyruvate is converted to ethanol and CO₂; used by yeast (used in brewing and baking)

Fermentation yields only 2 ATP per glucose — far less efficient than aerobic respiration.

## 3. Photosynthesis

**Photosynthesis** is the process by which plants, algae, and cyanobacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. The overall equation is the reverse of cellular respiration:

**6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂**

Photosynthesis occurs in **chloroplasts** and proceeds in two stages:

### Light Reactions (Thylakoid Membranes)

Chlorophyll and other pigments absorb sunlight. This energy is used to:
- Split water molecules, releasing oxygen as a byproduct (the O₂ we breathe)
- Generate ATP and NADPH (energy carriers for the next stage)

### Calvin Cycle (Stroma)

ATP and NADPH from the light reactions power the **carbon fixation** reactions: CO₂ from the air is incorporated into organic molecules (via the enzyme RuBisCO), ultimately producing **G3P** (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate), which is used to build glucose.

The Calvin cycle does not require light directly, but it depends on the ATP and NADPH produced by the light reactions.

---

## 4. Relationship Between Photosynthesis and Respiration

These two processes are complementary and interdependent at a planetary scale:

- Photosynthesis consumes CO₂ and water; produces glucose and oxygen
- Cellular respiration consumes glucose and oxygen; produces CO₂ and water

The oxygen in Earth's atmosphere is almost entirely the product of billions of years of photosynthesis. The carbon locked in fossil fuels represents ancient photosynthetic products.

---

## 5. Enzyme-Catalyzed Reactions

Nearly every chemical reaction in a cell is catalyzed by **enzymes** — protein molecules that speed up reactions by lowering the energy required to start them (**activation energy**).

- Enzymes are specific: each enzyme catalyzes a particular reaction or type of reaction
- The molecule an enzyme acts on is its **substrate**, which fits into the enzyme's **active site** (the "lock and key" model, or more accurately, the **induced fit** model where the enzyme changes shape slightly to accommodate the substrate)
- Enzymes are not consumed by the reactions they catalyze — they are reused
- **Temperature and pH** affect enzyme function: each enzyme has an optimal range; outside it, the enzyme may denature (lose its shape and function)
- **Inhibitors** can block enzyme activity: **competitive inhibitors** compete with the substrate for the active site; **noncompetitive inhibitors** bind elsewhere and change the enzyme's shape

---

## Summary

Cells run on ATP, regenerated primarily by cellular respiration. Respiration converts glucose to CO₂, water, and up to ~38 ATP through glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. Without oxygen, fermentation provides a limited ATP supply. Photosynthesis reverses this process, capturing light energy to fix CO₂ into glucose and release oxygen. Enzymes catalyze virtually every cellular reaction with high specificity, and their activity is sensitive to temperature, pH, and regulatory molecules.
