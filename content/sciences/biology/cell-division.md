Every cell in your body — all 37 trillion of them — descended from a single fertilized egg through successive rounds of cell division. Cell division is how organisms grow, heal, and reproduce. Understanding it means understanding both the remarkable precision of normal cell replication and the consequences when that precision fails.

---

## 1. The Cell Cycle

The **cell cycle** is the ordered sequence of events a cell undergoes as it grows and divides. It has two major phases:

### Interphase

The cell spends most of its life in **interphase** — actively growing, carrying out its functions, and preparing to divide. It has three sub-phases:

- **G₁ (Gap 1)**: cell grows, synthesizes proteins, performs its normal functions; a key checkpoint determines whether conditions are favorable for division
- **S (Synthesis)**: DNA replication occurs — every chromosome is copied so the cell has a complete second set
- **G₂ (Gap 2)**: cell continues growing; prepares division machinery; second checkpoint confirms DNA was copied correctly

### Mitotic Phase (M Phase)

The cell actually divides — first the nucleus (**mitosis**), then the cytoplasm (**cytokinesis**).

---

## 2. DNA Replication

Before a cell can divide, it must copy its entire genome. **DNA replication** is semiconservative: each strand of the double helix serves as a template for a new complementary strand.

Key steps:
1. **Helicase** unwinds and separates the two strands at the **origin of replication**
2. **Primase** lays down short RNA primers (starting points)
3. **DNA polymerase** reads each template strand and assembles a new complementary strand (always in the 5'→3' direction)
4. **Leading strand** is synthesized continuously; **lagging strand** is synthesized in fragments (**Okazaki fragments**) that are later joined
5. Proofreading mechanisms correct errors; the error rate is about 1 per billion base pairs

## 3. Mitosis

**Mitosis** produces two genetically identical daughter cells with the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell. It is used for growth, tissue repair, and asexual reproduction. Mitosis has four phases:

### Prophase

- Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes (each consisting of two identical **sister chromatids** joined at the **centromere**)
- The **mitotic spindle** (made of microtubules) begins to form
- The nuclear envelope breaks down

### Metaphase

- Chromosomes line up along the cell's equator (**metaphase plate**)
- Spindle fibers attach to the centromere of each chromosome at structures called **kinetochores**
- A checkpoint ensures all chromosomes are attached before proceeding

### Anaphase

- Sister chromatids are pulled apart by shortening spindle fibers, moving toward opposite poles of the cell
- Each pole now has a complete set of chromosomes

### Telophase + Cytokinesis

- Chromosomes arrive at the poles and decondense
- Nuclear envelopes reform around each set
- **Cytokinesis**: the cytoplasm divides — in animal cells, a cleavage furrow pinches the cell in two; in plant cells, a cell plate forms and becomes the new cell wall

Result: two identical daughter cells, each with the same chromosome number as the parent.

---

## 4. Meiosis

**Meiosis** is specialized cell division for sexual reproduction. It produces **four genetically unique daughter cells** (gametes — sperm or eggs) with **half the chromosome number** of the parent cell.

In humans, body cells contain 46 chromosomes (23 pairs). Meiosis produces gametes with 23 chromosomes each. When two gametes fuse at fertilization, the resulting zygote has 46 again.

Meiosis involves two rounds of division:

### Meiosis I (Reductive Division)

**Prophase I**: homologous chromosomes pair up and exchange segments in **crossing over** (recombination) — shuffling genetic information to create new combinations. This is the primary source of genetic variation in sexually reproducing species.

**Metaphase I → Anaphase I → Telophase I**: homologous chromosome pairs are separated, producing two cells with half the original chromosome number. (Sister chromatids stay together in this round.)

### Meiosis II

Similar to mitosis: sister chromatids of each chromosome are separated, producing four haploid cells. No further DNA replication occurs between Meiosis I and II.

### Sources of Genetic Variation in Meiosis

- **Crossing over** (prophase I): new combinations of maternal/paternal alleles on each chromosome
- **Independent assortment**: each pair of homologous chromosomes lines up randomly at the metaphase plate, so each gamete gets a random mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes (2²³ = ~8 million possible combinations in humans, just from this mechanism alone)
- **Random fertilization**: any sperm can fertilize any egg

## 5. Cancer: Cell Division Gone Wrong

Cancer occurs when the cell cycle's control systems break down and cells divide uncontrollably.

**Proto-oncogenes** are normal genes that promote cell division. Mutations that make them permanently active produce **oncogenes** — they act like a stuck accelerator.

**Tumor suppressor genes** normally put the brakes on cell division or trigger apoptosis (programmed cell death) in damaged cells. Mutations that inactivate them remove the brakes.

Cancer typically requires multiple mutations in both types of genes — acquired over a lifetime of cell division or induced by mutagens (UV radiation, carcinogens, certain viruses).

---

## Summary

The cell cycle includes interphase (growth and DNA replication) and the mitotic phase (division). Mitosis produces two identical daughter cells with a full chromosome set — for growth and repair. Meiosis produces four genetically unique daughter cells with half the chromosome set — for sexual reproduction. Crossing over and independent assortment during meiosis generate genetic diversity. Cancer results from mutations that disrupt cell cycle regulation, causing uncontrolled proliferation.
