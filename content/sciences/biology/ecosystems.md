An ecosystem is the sum of all living organisms in an area and all the ways they interact with each other and with their physical environment. Understanding ecosystems means understanding energy flow, nutrient cycling, and the intricate web of dependencies that makes life on Earth possible.

---

## 1. Components of an Ecosystem

**Biotic components**: all living organisms — plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, protists.

**Abiotic components**: all non-living physical and chemical factors — sunlight, temperature, water, soil, wind, pH, salinity, nutrients.

The interplay between biotic and abiotic components defines what organisms can survive in an ecosystem and shapes the entire community of life.

---

## 2. Energy Flow

All energy in most ecosystems originates from the **sun**. (Exceptions: hydrothermal vent ecosystems powered by chemolithotrophs that derive energy from inorganic chemicals.)

### Trophic Levels

Organisms are organized into **trophic levels** based on how they obtain energy:

- **Producers (autotrophs)**: make their own food through photosynthesis or chemosynthesis. Plants, algae, and cyanobacteria. The energy base of nearly all ecosystems.
- **Primary consumers (herbivores)**: eat producers. Deer, caterpillars, zooplankton.
- **Secondary consumers**: eat primary consumers. Foxes, frogs, small fish.
- **Tertiary consumers**: eat secondary consumers. Eagles, large predatory fish.
- **Decomposers**: break down dead organic matter and return nutrients to the soil/water. Bacteria and fungi. Essential to all ecosystems — without them, nutrients would be locked in dead tissue.

### The 10% Rule

When energy passes from one trophic level to the next, only about **10% is transferred** as usable energy. The rest (90%) is lost as heat during metabolism or in feces. This is why:
- Food chains are rarely longer than 4–5 levels (not enough energy to support higher levels)
- The world can support far more herbivores than carnivores
- Eating lower on the food chain is more energy-efficient (one of the reasons plant-based diets have smaller ecological footprints)

### Food Webs

A **food chain** traces a single path of energy transfer: grass → grasshopper → frog → hawk. A **food web** shows the complex, interconnected feeding relationships among many species in an ecosystem. Real ecosystems have food webs, not simple food chains — most predators eat multiple prey, and most prey are eaten by multiple predators.

## 3. Nutrient Cycling (Biogeochemical Cycles)

Unlike energy (which flows through an ecosystem and is lost as heat), **nutrients cycle** continuously between living organisms and the abiotic environment.

### Carbon Cycle

Carbon is the backbone of all organic molecules. It moves between the atmosphere (CO₂), living organisms, soil, ocean, and rock:
- Photosynthesis: removes CO₂ from atmosphere, incorporates it into organic matter
- Respiration: releases CO₂ back to atmosphere
- Decomposition: releases CO₂ from dead organic matter
- Combustion of fossil fuels: releases ancient carbon stored in coal and oil; this is driving atmospheric CO₂ increases and climate change

### Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen (N₂) makes up 78% of the atmosphere but most organisms can't use it directly. **Nitrogen fixation** by bacteria converts N₂ into ammonium (NH₄⁺) — usable by plants. **Nitrification** converts ammonium to nitrate (NO₃⁻). **Denitrification** returns nitrogen gas to the atmosphere. Decomposers release nitrogen from dead organic matter (ammonification). Synthetic fertilizers bypass natural fixation but can cause runoff and eutrophication.

### Water Cycle

Water evaporates from surfaces (evaporation) and plants (transpiration), rises as water vapor, condenses into clouds, and returns as precipitation. Runoff and groundwater flow return water to oceans and lakes. The water cycle drives weather and distributes fresh water globally.

## 4. Ecological Relationships

Species in an ecosystem interact in multiple ways:

- **Predation**: one species (predator) kills and eats another (prey). Drives coevolution of attack and defense adaptations.
- **Competition**: two species compete for the same limited resource. Can lead to **competitive exclusion** (one species eliminates the other) or **resource partitioning** (species evolve to use slightly different resources, reducing overlap).
- **Mutualism**: both species benefit. Bees and flowers, mycorrhizal fungi and plant roots, bacteria in the human gut.
- **Commensalism**: one species benefits, the other is unaffected. Barnacles on whales, egrets following cattle.
- **Parasitism**: one species (parasite) benefits at the expense of the host. Tapeworms, ticks, mistletoe.

---

## 5. Ecological Succession

After a disturbance — a fire, glacier retreat, volcanic eruption — ecosystems change over time in a predictable sequence called **ecological succession**.

**Primary succession** begins on bare rock or fresh lava with no soil. Pioneer species (lichens, mosses) break down rock and accumulate organic matter, enabling others to colonize.

**Secondary succession** occurs where soil already exists after a disturbance (fire, logging, farmland abandonment). Faster than primary succession because soil is intact.

Over decades or centuries, communities of species shift toward a **climax community** — a relatively stable assemblage characteristic of the region's climate. However, "climax" is not static; disturbances continually reset parts of ecosystems to earlier successional stages.

---

## 6. Ecosystem Services

Ecosystems provide services that human economies depend on but rarely price:

- **Provisioning**: food, fresh water, timber, fiber, medicines
- **Regulating**: climate regulation, flood control, water purification, pollination
- **Supporting**: nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production
- **Cultural**: recreation, aesthetics, spiritual values

Biodiversity underpins ecosystem stability and services. Ecosystems with more species are generally more productive, more resilient to disturbance, and more capable of recovering from stress.

---

## Summary

Ecosystems consist of biotic organisms and abiotic factors interacting together. Energy flows from producers through consumers at each trophic level, with ~10% transferred at each step. Nutrients cycle through organisms and the environment via biogeochemical cycles (carbon, nitrogen, water). Species interact through predation, competition, mutualism, and parasitism. Ecosystems change over time through succession and provide essential services that sustain human life.
