DNA is the molecule of heredity — the archive of instructions that directs the development, function, and reproduction of every living organism on Earth. Understanding DNA means understanding how biological information is stored, copied, expressed, and passed from generation to generation.

---

## 1. The Structure of DNA

**DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)** is a polymer made of units called **nucleotides**. Each nucleotide has three components:
- A **deoxyribose sugar**
- A **phosphate group**
- One of four **nitrogenous bases**: Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Cytosine (C), or Guanine (G)

Nucleotides are linked in a chain through their sugar-phosphate backbones. Two such chains wind around each other in the iconic **double helix** — discovered by Watson and Crick in 1953, based on X-ray crystallography data by Rosalind Franklin.

The two strands are held together by **hydrogen bonds** between complementary base pairs:
- **A pairs with T** (2 hydrogen bonds)
- **C pairs with G** (3 hydrogen bonds)

This **complementary base pairing** is fundamental to everything DNA does — replication, transcription, and repair. The strands are antiparallel: they run in opposite directions (5'→3' and 3'→5').

---

## 2. Genes and the Genome

A **gene** is a specific sequence of DNA nucleotides that encodes a functional product — usually a protein, sometimes a functional RNA molecule.

The **genome** is the complete set of DNA in an organism — all genes plus non-coding regions. The human genome contains approximately 3 billion base pairs and encodes about 20,000–25,000 protein-coding genes. Remarkably, only about 1.5% of human DNA directly codes for proteins. The rest includes regulatory sequences, introns, repetitive elements, and regions whose functions are still being studied.

DNA in eukaryotic cells is organized into **chromosomes** — long DNA molecules wrapped around **histone** proteins and condensed into structures visible under a microscope during cell division. Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs) in most body cells.

## 3. From DNA to Protein: Gene Expression

The flow of genetic information is described by the **Central Dogma of Molecular Biology**:

**DNA → RNA → Protein**

Gene expression occurs in two stages: **transcription** and **translation**.

### Transcription (Nucleus)

A gene is "read" by the enzyme **RNA polymerase**, which unwinds a section of the double helix and synthesizes a complementary **messenger RNA (mRNA)** strand using the DNA template.

The mRNA is a working copy of the gene. In eukaryotes, it is processed before leaving the nucleus:
- **Introns** (non-coding sequences) are spliced out
- **Exons** (coding sequences) are joined together
- A protective 5' cap and poly-A tail are added

### Translation (Ribosome)

The processed mRNA travels to a ribosome, where it is read in **codons** — triplets of bases, each specifying one amino acid. The **genetic code** is the correspondence between codons and amino acids. It is nearly universal across all life.

**Transfer RNA (tRNA)** molecules bring amino acids to the ribosome. Each tRNA has an **anticodon** complementary to the mRNA codon and carries the corresponding amino acid. As codons are read, amino acids are linked by peptide bonds, building the polypeptide chain.

The process begins at a **start codon** (AUG, encoding methionine) and ends when a **stop codon** (UAA, UAG, or UGA) is reached.

---

## 4. Mutations

A **mutation** is a heritable change in the DNA sequence. Mutations can be:

- **Point mutations**: a single base change
  - **Silent**: codes for the same amino acid (the code is redundant); no effect
  - **Missense**: codes for a different amino acid; may alter protein function
  - **Nonsense**: creates a premature stop codon; produces a truncated, usually nonfunctional protein
- **Insertions and deletions**: adding or removing bases. If the number is not a multiple of three, the reading frame shifts (**frameshift mutation**), altering every amino acid downstream
- **Chromosomal mutations**: large-scale changes — deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations of chromosome segments

Causes: errors during DNA replication, radiation (UV, X-rays), chemical mutagens, viral insertion.

Not all mutations are harmful — many are neutral, and some provide beneficial variation that natural selection can act upon.

---

## 5. DNA Replication

Before cell division, DNA is copied with remarkable fidelity:

1. **Helicase** unwinds the double helix
2. **DNA polymerase** reads each strand and builds a new complementary strand
3. The result is two identical double helices, each containing one original and one new strand (**semiconservative replication**)
4. Proofreading mechanisms catch and correct errors; the final error rate is ~1 per billion base pairs

---

## 6. Regulation of Gene Expression

Having a gene doesn't mean it's always active. **Gene regulation** controls which genes are expressed, when, and how much — allowing a single genome to produce hundreds of different cell types.

Key mechanisms:
- **Transcription factors**: proteins that bind to DNA near a gene and activate or repress transcription
- **Promoters**: DNA sequences upstream of a gene where RNA polymerase binds; their sequence affects how efficiently transcription begins
- **Enhancers and silencers**: regulatory sequences that can be far from the gene; bound by activator or repressor proteins
- **Epigenetics**: chemical modifications to histones or the DNA itself (methylation) that alter gene accessibility without changing the DNA sequence; heritable and influenced by environment

---

## Summary

DNA is a double helix of complementary base pairs (A-T and C-G), encoding genes as sequences of nucleotides. Gene expression converts DNA to protein via transcription (DNA → mRNA) and translation (mRNA → protein) following the genetic code. Mutations alter DNA sequences and may change protein function. Gene regulation determines which genes are active in which cells and when — allowing a single genome to produce an entire organism's complexity.
