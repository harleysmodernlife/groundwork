Heredity is the transmission of traits from parents to offspring. The rules governing this transmission — first worked out by Gregor Mendel in the 1860s and later understood in terms of chromosomes and DNA — explain why children resemble their parents, why genetic diseases run in families, and how the same two parents can produce children who look quite different from one another.

---

## 1. Mendel's Experiments

Gregor Mendel, an Austrian monk, spent eight years crossbreeding pea plants and tracking traits across generations. His careful quantitative approach produced the foundational principles of genetics.

He chose traits with two clear variants (yellow vs. green seeds, tall vs. short plants) and performed **monohybrid crosses** (one trait at a time) and **dihybrid crosses** (two traits simultaneously).

Key observations:
- When he crossed true-breeding yellow-seed plants with true-breeding green-seed plants, all offspring (F₁) had yellow seeds
- When F₁ plants were self-fertilized (F₂), approximately 3/4 had yellow seeds and 1/4 had green seeds — a 3:1 ratio
- The "hidden" trait (green) didn't disappear — it was masked in F₁ but reappeared in F₂

---

## 2. Mendel's Laws

### Law of Segregation

Each individual has two copies of each gene (one from each parent). When gametes (sperm and egg) are formed, the two copies **segregate** — each gamete receives only one copy. The two alleles in an offspring come from the union of two gametes.

### Law of Independent Assortment

Genes on different chromosomes (or far apart on the same chromosome) are inherited independently of each other. In a dihybrid cross, the combinations of traits in offspring approach a 9:3:3:1 ratio. This law applies when genes are on different chromosomes (not linked).

---

## 3. Key Terminology

- **Gene**: a hereditary unit that controls a specific trait
- **Allele**: a variant form of a gene (e.g., yellow vs. green seed color)
- **Dominant allele**: masks the expression of the recessive allele when present (written in uppercase: Y)
- **Recessive allele**: expressed only when two copies are present (written lowercase: y)
- **Homozygous**: two identical alleles for a gene (YY or yy)
- **Heterozygous**: two different alleles (Yy)
- **Genotype**: the actual allele combination an organism carries (YY, Yy, or yy)
- **Phenotype**: the physical expression of the genotype (yellow or green seed)

---

## 4. Punnett Squares

A **Punnett square** is a grid used to predict the probability of offspring genotypes from a cross.

For a monohybrid cross between two heterozygous parents (Yy × Yy):

```
      Y        y
Y    YY       Yy
y    Yy       yy
```

Genotype ratios: 1 YY : 2 Yy : 1 yy (25% homozygous dominant, 50% heterozygous, 25% homozygous recessive)
Phenotype ratios: 3 yellow : 1 green (75% dominant phenotype, 25% recessive phenotype)

---

## 5. Patterns of Inheritance

### Complete Dominance

One allele completely masks the other (classic Mendelian ratios above).

### Incomplete Dominance

Neither allele is fully dominant. Heterozygotes show an intermediate phenotype. Example: red (RR) × white (WW) flowers → pink (RW) in snapdragons. F₂ cross gives 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.

### Codominance

Both alleles are expressed simultaneously. Example: AB blood type — both A and B antigens are present on red blood cells.

### Multiple Alleles

Some genes have more than two alleles in the population. The ABO blood type gene has three alleles (Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i), producing four possible blood types.

### Polygenic Inheritance

Many traits (height, skin color, intelligence) are controlled by multiple genes, each contributing a small effect. These traits show continuous variation (a bell curve distribution) rather than discrete categories.

### Sex-Linked Traits

Genes on the sex chromosomes (X and Y) follow different inheritance patterns. The Y chromosome is much smaller and carries few genes. Traits encoded on the X chromosome with no corresponding allele on Y are **X-linked**; males (XY) express any allele on their single X, while females can be carriers.

Examples: red-green color blindness and hemophilia are X-linked recessive traits — far more common in males because they have only one X chromosome to carry the allele.

---

## 6. Chromosomal Basis of Heredity

Mendel's laws have a direct physical basis in chromosomes. Chromosomes come in pairs (homologs), one from each parent. Alleles are located at the same position (**locus**) on homologous chromosomes.

During meiosis, homologs segregate (Law of Segregation) and pairs of homologs sort independently (Law of Independent Assortment). Crossing over further shuffles allele combinations.

**Linked genes** — located on the same chromosome — tend to be inherited together, violating independent assortment. However, crossing over can separate them.

---

## 7. Genetic Disorders

Recessive disorders: only expressed when two copies of the disease allele are present.
- **Cystic fibrosis**: defective chloride ion channel; affects lung and digestive function
- **Sickle cell disease**: abnormal hemoglobin causes red blood cells to deform under low oxygen

Dominant disorders: one copy of the disease allele causes disease.
- **Huntington's disease**: progressive neurodegeneration; onset typically in midlife

Chromosomal abnormalities: errors in chromosome number or structure.
- **Down syndrome (Trisomy 21)**: an extra copy of chromosome 21; caused by nondisjunction during meiosis

---

## Summary

Heredity follows rules first discovered by Mendel: traits are controlled by discrete gene pairs that segregate into gametes and assort independently. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones; heterozygotes for a recessive allele are carriers. Punnett squares predict offspring ratios. Incomplete dominance, codominance, sex linkage, and polygenic inheritance extend beyond simple Mendelian patterns. Genetic disorders arise from mutations or chromosomal abnormalities in these inheritance systems.
