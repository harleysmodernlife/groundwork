With an estimated 8.7 million species on Earth, biology needs a system for organizing life that reflects both practical identification and evolutionary relationships. The science of **taxonomy** — classifying and naming organisms — has evolved from simple observation-based groupings into a sophisticated system grounded in genetics and evolutionary history.

---

## 1. The Linnaean System

Carl Linnaeus developed the foundations of modern classification in the 18th century. His system, still in use today (with modifications), organizes life into a nested hierarchy of increasingly specific groups:

**Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species**

A useful mnemonic: **D**ear **K**ing **P**hilip **C**ame **O**ver **F**or **G**ood **S**oup

Each level is called a **taxon** (plural: taxa). As you move down the hierarchy, organisms share more characteristics.

### Binomial Nomenclature

Every species is given a two-part Latin scientific name: **Genus species**. The genus is capitalized; the species epithet is lowercase; both are italicized (or underlined when handwritten).

- *Homo sapiens* — modern humans
- *Panthera leo* — lion
- *Canis lupus* — gray wolf

Binomial nomenclature provides a universal system that transcends language barriers and prevents confusion from common names (a "starfish" is neither a star nor a fish — its scientific name, *Asterias rubens*, is unambiguous).

## 2. The Three Domains of Life

Modern classification recognizes three fundamental domains based on cellular structure and molecular phylogenetics (comparing DNA and rRNA sequences):

### Domain Bacteria

Prokaryotes (no membrane-bound nucleus). The most abundant and diverse organisms on Earth. Include the bacteria responsible for nitrogen fixation, decomposition, disease, fermentation, and photosynthesis. Some of the most important organisms for ecosystems and human health.

### Domain Archaea

Also prokaryotes, but genetically and biochemically distinct from bacteria. Many are **extremophiles** — adapted to extreme environments:
- **Thermophiles**: hot springs and hydrothermal vents (temperatures above 100°C)
- **Halophiles**: extremely salty environments (Dead Sea, salt flats)
- **Methanogens**: anaerobic environments; produce methane as a metabolic byproduct (found in animal guts and swamps)

Archaea are more closely related to eukaryotes than to bacteria, which has implications for the origin of complex life.

### Domain Eukarya

Organisms whose cells have a membrane-bound nucleus. Includes four kingdoms:

- **Kingdom Protista**: mostly unicellular eukaryotes; a diverse, paraphyletic grouping (not all share a single common ancestor). Includes amoebas, paramecia, algae, slime molds.
- **Kingdom Fungi**: heterotrophs that absorb nutrients from their surroundings (decomposers); include mushrooms, yeasts, molds. Cell walls made of chitin.
- **Kingdom Plantae**: multicellular, photosynthetic autotrophs. Cell walls of cellulose. Include mosses, ferns, conifers, and flowering plants.
- **Kingdom Animalia**: multicellular heterotrophs that ingest food. No cell walls. Include invertebrates and vertebrates.

## 3. Phylogenetics and Cladistics

Traditional Linnaean taxonomy was based on observable characteristics — morphology, anatomy, physiology. Modern taxonomy increasingly relies on **phylogenetics** — the study of evolutionary relationships based on molecular data (DNA sequences, protein sequences).

A **phylogenetic tree** (or cladogram) depicts hypothesized evolutionary relationships among organisms. Groups that share a common ancestor are more closely related. Each branch point represents a common ancestor.

**Cladistics** groups organisms based on **shared derived characters** — traits that appeared in a common ancestor and are shared by all its descendants. A **clade** (or monophyletic group) includes an ancestor and all of its descendants.

Modern molecular phylogenetics has sometimes overturned traditional groupings:
- Birds are now classified as a subgroup of **reptiles** (they share a more recent common ancestor with crocodiles than crocodiles share with lizards)
- Fungi are more closely related to animals than to plants
- The classification of "fish" as a group is paraphyletic — it excludes some descendants of fish ancestors (i.e., tetrapods, including us)

---

## 4. Species Concepts

Defining a "species" is harder than it seems. The most widely used definition is the **biological species concept**: a species is a group of populations whose members can interbreed and produce fertile offspring, and are reproductively isolated from other groups.

Problems:
- Doesn't apply to asexually reproducing organisms (bacteria, many plants)
- Some species hybridize in nature (wolves and coyotes; grizzlies and polar bears)
- Doesn't work for fossils

Alternative concepts include the morphological species concept (based on physical similarity) and the phylogenetic species concept (based on unique evolutionary lineage).

---

## 5. Viruses: Life or Not?

Viruses are not classified within the three domains because they are not cellular. They lack ribosomes, cannot carry out metabolism independently, and only "reproduce" by hijacking host cell machinery.

Whether viruses are "alive" depends on how you define life. They contain genetic information (DNA or RNA), evolve by natural selection, and interact intimately with living cells — but they cannot reproduce without a host. They are best understood as genetic parasites.

---

## Summary

Life is classified using a hierarchical system: Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. Three domains — Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya — represent the deepest divisions in life. Modern taxonomy uses molecular phylogenetics to reconstruct evolutionary relationships and build classification systems that reflect actual ancestry. Binomial nomenclature gives every species a unique, universally recognized two-part scientific name.
