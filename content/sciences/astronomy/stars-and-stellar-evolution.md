Stars are the engines of the universe — forging heavy elements in their cores, seeding space with those elements when they die, and providing the energy that enables planets and life to exist. Every atom in your body heavier than hydrogen was forged in a star. Understanding stellar evolution is understanding where matter itself comes from.

---

## 1. What Is a Star?

A **star** is a massive, self-luminous ball of plasma held together by gravity and powered by nuclear fusion in its core. The balance between the inward pull of gravity and the outward pressure of radiation and hot gas (**hydrostatic equilibrium**) determines a star's size and stability throughout most of its life.

Stars range enormously in mass — from about 0.08 solar masses (below which a body cannot sustain hydrogen fusion) to over 100 solar masses. Mass is the single most important factor determining a star's luminosity, temperature, color, and lifespan.

---

## 2. Stellar Classification

Stars are classified by their **surface temperature** using a spectral sequence:

**O B A F G K M** (hottest to coolest)

Mnemonic: "Oh, Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me"

| Class | Temperature | Color | Example |
|-------|------------|-------|---------|
| O | >30,000 K | Blue | Mintaka (Orion's belt) |
| B | 10,000–30,000 K | Blue-white | Rigel |
| A | 7,500–10,000 K | White | Sirius |
| F | 6,000–7,500 K | Yellow-white | Procyon |
| G | 5,200–6,000 K | Yellow | Sun |
| K | 3,700–5,200 K | Orange | Arcturus |
| M | <3,700 K | Red | Proxima Centauri, Betelgeuse |

O-type stars are the most massive, brightest, hottest, and shortest-lived. M-type stars are the least massive, dimmest, coolest, and longest-lived (up to trillions of years for the smallest).

---

## 3. The Hertzsprung-Russell Diagram

The **H-R diagram** plots stars by luminosity (vertical axis) vs. surface temperature (horizontal axis, with temperature increasing to the left). Most stars (including our Sun) fall along the **main sequence** — a diagonal band from hot/luminous to cool/dim.

- **Main sequence**: stars fusing hydrogen to helium in their cores
- **Red giants/supergiants**: evolved stars with expanded outer layers; upper right of diagram
- **White dwarfs**: stellar remnants; lower left (hot but dim)
- **Neutron stars**: too dim to appear prominently

The position of a star on the main sequence is almost entirely determined by its mass. High-mass stars are at the top-left (hot and luminous); low-mass stars are at the bottom-right.

---

## 4. Star Formation

Stars form in **molecular clouds** — dense, cold regions of gas (mostly hydrogen) and dust in interstellar space. Turbulence or a shockwave from a nearby supernova can trigger gravitational collapse.

1. A clump of gas begins to collapse under gravity (**protostellar cloud**)
2. As it contracts, gravitational potential energy converts to heat. The center becomes a glowing **protostar**
3. The protostar is surrounded by a rotating disk of gas and dust — material that may form planets
4. When core temperatures reach ~10 million K, hydrogen fusion ignites. Radiation pressure halts further collapse. The star joins the main sequence

A protostar the mass of the Sun takes about 50 million years to reach the main sequence. The most massive stars form in as little as 100,000 years.

---

## 5. Main Sequence Life

During the **main sequence** stage, a star fuses hydrogen into helium in its core. The Sun has been on the main sequence for 4.6 billion years and will remain there for another ~5 billion.

A star's lifespan is inversely proportional to its mass:
- A 10 solar mass star burns 10,000× more fuel but only has 10× more mass → ~10 million year lifespan
- A 0.1 solar mass red dwarf has a ~10 trillion year lifespan

**Mass-luminosity relation**: L ∝ M³·⁵ (approximately)
A star 10× as massive as the Sun is ~3,162× as luminous — explaining the enormous lifespan differences.

---

## 6. Red Giant Phase

When a star exhausts hydrogen in its core, fusion stops. The core contracts and heats. A **hydrogen-burning shell** around the core reignites, dumping energy into the outer layers. The star expands enormously, cooling its surface — becoming a **red giant** (or **red supergiant** for massive stars).

The Sun's red giant phase will begin in ~5 billion years. It will expand to perhaps 150–200× its current radius, potentially engulfing Mercury, Venus, and possibly Earth.

In more massive stars, the hotter core ignites **helium fusion** (helium → carbon, oxygen) and eventually carbon fusion and beyond, producing heavier elements up to iron.

---

## 7. Stellar Death: Three Fates

### Low to Medium Mass Stars (< ~8 solar masses, including the Sun)

1. After the red giant phase, the outer layers are expelled as a beautiful **planetary nebula** (despite the name, nothing to do with planets — named historically for their appearance)
2. The exposed core — a hot, Earth-sized ball of carbon-oxygen — becomes a **white dwarf**, gradually cooling over billions to trillions of years
3. A white dwarf that reaches 1.4 solar masses (the Chandrasekhar limit) by accreting mass from a companion star explodes as a **Type Ia supernova** — a standard candle used to measure cosmological distances

### High Mass Stars (> ~8 solar masses)

1. Fusion proceeds up the periodic table — helium → carbon → neon → oxygen → silicon → iron
2. Iron cannot release energy through fusion; the core collapses in milliseconds
3. The collapse triggers a **core-collapse supernova** — one of the most energetic events in the universe, briefly outshining entire galaxies
4. Elements heavier than iron are forged in the explosion itself through rapid neutron capture (the **r-process**)
5. The remaining core becomes a **neutron star** (if the remnant is 1.4–3 solar masses) or a **black hole** (if above ~3 solar masses)

### Neutron Stars

Incredibly dense (a teaspoon weighs ~10 billion tons), typically 20 km in diameter, spinning rapidly. **Pulsars** are neutron stars beaming radio waves along their magnetic axis — detected as regular pulses as the star rotates.

Two merging neutron stars produce **kilonovae** — detected both in gravitational waves and light. These events produce gold, platinum, and other heavy elements through the r-process.

---

## 8. Nucleosynthesis: Where Elements Come From

- **Hydrogen and Helium**: formed in the Big Bang (Big Bang nucleosynthesis)
- **Helium through Iron**: fused in stellar cores during main sequence and evolved stages
- **Elements heavier than iron**: formed in supernova explosions and neutron star mergers (r-process), and in some stars through slow neutron capture (s-process)

When stars die, they expel these elements into interstellar space. The next generation of stars and planets forms from this enriched material. Earth — and everything on it — is made of stellar ejecta. We are, literally, stardust.

---

## Summary

Stars range from tiny red dwarfs to massive supergiants, classified by temperature (O through M). The H-R diagram maps their luminosity vs. temperature. Stars form in molecular clouds by gravitational collapse and spend most of their lives fusing hydrogen on the main sequence. When core hydrogen is exhausted, stars expand to red giants. Low-mass stars end as white dwarfs within planetary nebulae; high-mass stars explode as supernovae and leave neutron stars or black holes. Stellar nucleosynthesis produces most elements heavier than hydrogen — including all carbon, oxygen, and iron in our bodies.
