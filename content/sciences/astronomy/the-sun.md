The Sun is the star at the center of our solar system — a blazing sphere of plasma so massive that its gravity holds eight planets, thousands of dwarf planets, and billions of smaller bodies in orbit. It is the source of virtually all energy on Earth, and understanding it is the key to understanding stars in general.

---

## 1. Basic Facts

- **Type**: G-type main sequence star ("yellow dwarf")
- **Age**: ~4.6 billion years
- **Distance from Earth**: 1 AU (astronomical unit) = ~150 million km; light takes 8 minutes 20 seconds to travel from the Sun to Earth
- **Diameter**: ~1.39 million km (109 × Earth's diameter)
- **Mass**: 1.989 × 10³⁰ kg (330,000 × Earth's mass); contains 99.86% of the solar system's mass
- **Composition**: ~73% hydrogen, ~25% helium by mass, plus trace heavier elements
- **Surface temperature**: ~5,500°C (photosphere)
- **Core temperature**: ~15 million°C

---

## 2. Structure of the Sun

The Sun is not a solid body — it has a layered structure defined by how energy is generated and transported.

### Core

The innermost ~25% of the Sun's radius. Temperatures (~15 million°C) and pressures (~250 billion atmospheres) are sufficient for **nuclear fusion** — the process that powers the Sun. Hydrogen nuclei (protons) fuse to form helium nuclei in the **proton-proton chain**, releasing enormous energy in the form of gamma rays and neutrinos.

About 600 million tons of hydrogen are fused into helium every second. The mass difference (~4 million tons per second) is converted to energy according to E = mc².

### Radiative Zone

Energy produced in the core travels outward through the **radiative zone** via photons — but the zone is so dense that photons are constantly absorbed and re-emitted. A single photon may take 100,000–170,000 years to traverse this layer, following a random walk.

### Convective Zone

In the outer ~30% of the Sun's radius, energy is transported by **convection** — hot plasma rises to the surface, cools, and sinks back down. This convective motion creates the granular texture visible on the Sun's surface.

### Photosphere

The visible surface — the "bottom" of the Sun's atmosphere. The photosphere is about 500 km thick and glows at ~5,500°C. It shows:
- **Granules**: convection cells (~1,000 km across) that appear bright at centers (rising hot plasma) and dark at edges (cooling, sinking plasma)
- **Sunspots**: cooler (~3,700°C), darker regions where strong magnetic fields suppress convection

### Chromosphere

A thin layer above the photosphere (~2,000 km thick). Visible during solar eclipses as a pinkish-red ring. Contains **solar prominences** — loops of plasma following magnetic field lines — and **spicules** (jets of plasma).

### Corona

The Sun's outer atmosphere, extending millions of kilometers into space. Paradoxically hotter than the photosphere (~1–3 million°C) — an unsolved problem in solar physics (the coronal heating problem). The corona is visible as the pearly halo during a total solar eclipse. It is the source of the **solar wind**.

---

## 3. Nuclear Fusion: The Sun's Energy Source

The **proton-proton chain** is the dominant fusion reaction in stars like the Sun:

1. Two protons fuse to form deuterium (heavy hydrogen nucleus), releasing a positron and neutrino
2. Deuterium + proton → helium-3
3. Two helium-3 nuclei fuse → helium-4 + two protons

Net: 4 protons → 1 helium-4 nucleus + energy

The energy released per reaction is tiny, but the Sun performs this reaction ~9.2 × 10³⁷ times per second. The total luminosity (power output) is 3.8 × 10²⁶ watts — equivalent to converting ~4 million tons of mass to energy every second.

The Sun has enough hydrogen to sustain fusion for another ~5 billion years.

---

## 4. Solar Activity

The Sun's magnetic field drives a cycle of activity with an ~11-year period:

**Sunspots**: temporary dark spots where intense magnetic fields inhibit convection. More sunspots indicate higher solar activity. The solar cycle (sunspot minimum to maximum and back) lasts about 11 years.

**Solar flares**: sudden intense eruptions of radiation (X-rays, UV) from the chromosphere, associated with magnetic field reconnection.

**Coronal Mass Ejections (CMEs)**: massive eruptions of plasma and magnetic field from the corona, travelling at 100–3,000 km/s. When directed at Earth, they can cause geomagnetic storms that disrupt satellites, radio communications, and power grids, and produce brilliant auroras.

**Solar wind**: a continuous stream of charged particles (mostly protons and electrons) flowing outward from the corona at ~400–800 km/s. It shapes the magnetospheres of all planets.

---

## 5. The Sun's Fate

The Sun is about halfway through its ~10 billion year main sequence life. In approximately 5 billion years:

1. Hydrogen in the core will be exhausted
2. The core will contract and heat up; the outer layers will expand enormously — the Sun will become a **red giant**, potentially engulfing Earth's orbit
3. The Sun will pulse and shed its outer layers as a **planetary nebula**
4. The remaining core will become a **white dwarf** — an Earth-sized, extremely dense remnant that slowly cools over billions of years

---

## Summary

The Sun is a G-type star powered by hydrogen fusion in its core at 15 million°C. Energy travels through the radiative zone and convective zone before emerging from the photosphere at ~5,500°C. The corona extends millions of km and produces the solar wind. Solar activity — sunspots, flares, and CMEs — follows an ~11-year cycle driven by the Sun's magnetic field. In ~5 billion years, the Sun will exhaust its core hydrogen, expand into a red giant, and ultimately leave a white dwarf remnant.
