A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system of stars, stellar remnants, interstellar gas, dust, and dark matter. The observable universe contains an estimated 2 trillion galaxies. Our own galaxy, the Milky Way, contains 100–400 billion stars — and we live in the suburbs, two-thirds of the way from the center to the edge, in an unremarkable corner of one of its spiral arms.

---

## 1. The Milky Way

The **Milky Way** is a **barred spiral galaxy** — a flattened disk with a central bar-shaped structure from which two major and several minor spiral arms extend. It spans approximately 100,000 light-years in diameter (with a diffuse halo of stars and dark matter extending much farther) and is about 1,000 light-years thick at the disk.

**Our location**: The Sun is located in the **Orion Arm** (Orion Spur), about 26,000 light-years from the **galactic center** (Sagittarius A*). We orbit the galactic center at roughly 220 km/s, completing one orbit every ~225–250 million years — one **galactic year** (also called a cosmic year).

**Sagittarius A*** (**Sgr A***): the supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way. Mass: ~4 million solar masses. The first direct image of Sgr A* was produced in 2022 by the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration.

**The Milky Way's satellite galaxies**: the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds (LMC and SMC) are irregular dwarf galaxies visible to the naked eye from the Southern Hemisphere, gravitationally bound to the Milky Way and slowly being absorbed by it.

## 2. Types of Galaxies

Galaxies are classified by shape — the **Hubble sequence** (or "Hubble tuning fork"):

### Elliptical Galaxies

Range from nearly spherical (E0) to highly elongated (E7). Composed mostly of old, red/orange stars; little gas or dust; very little new star formation. Can range from dwarf ellipticals (a few million stars) to giant ellipticals (trillions of stars). Form through mergers of other galaxies.

### Spiral Galaxies

Have a central bulge surrounded by a flattened disk with spiral arms. Young, blue stars and active star formation in the arms (where gas and dust are compressed); older stars in the bulge.
- **Normal spirals (S)**: central bulge + spiral arms
- **Barred spirals (SB)**: a linear bar of stars through the center, with arms emanating from the bar's ends. The Milky Way is type SBbc (barred spiral with moderately wound arms).

### Lenticular Galaxies (S0)

Between elliptical and spiral — a disk and central bulge like a spiral, but no prominent spiral arms and little star formation. "Used up" their gas.

### Irregular Galaxies

No regular shape. Often the result of gravitational interactions or collisions. Tend to be gas-rich with active star formation. The Magellanic Clouds are examples.

## 3. Galaxy Formation and Evolution

Galaxies formed from slight density variations in the early universe. Regions of slightly higher density gravitationally attracted more matter, eventually forming the first stars within the first billion years.

Galaxies grow through:
- **Accretion**: gathering gas from the intergalactic medium
- **Mergers**: galaxies collide and merge (galaxies are mostly empty space, so individual stars rarely collide, but gravity reshapes both galaxies into new structures)

The Milky Way is currently in the process of absorbing the Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy — pulling stars from it into our own galactic disk. In ~4.5 billion years, the Milky Way will collide and merge with the **Andromeda Galaxy** (M31), eventually forming a large elliptical galaxy (sometimes called "Milkomeda").

---

## 4. Supermassive Black Holes

Nearly all large galaxies appear to contain a **supermassive black hole** (SMBH) at their center, ranging from millions to billions of solar masses.

The mass of an SMBH correlates tightly with the properties of the galaxy's central bulge — suggesting coevolution of black holes and their host galaxies, though the mechanism is debated.

When material falls into a SMBH, it forms an **accretion disk** that heats to extreme temperatures and radiates intensely. These active galactic nuclei (AGN) can be:
- **Quasars** (quasi-stellar objects): extremely luminous AGN at cosmological distances; some outshine their entire host galaxy by factors of thousands
- **Seyfert galaxies**: active galactic nuclei in nearby spiral galaxies
- **Blazars**: AGN with jets pointed nearly directly at Earth

The Milky Way's Sgr A* is currently relatively quiet — it accretes little material compared to quasars.

## 5. Large-Scale Structure of the Universe

Galaxies are not uniformly distributed. They cluster into:

- **Galaxy groups**: small clusters of a few to dozens of galaxies. The **Local Group** contains the Milky Way, Andromeda (M31), Triangulum (M33), and ~80 smaller galaxies.
- **Galaxy clusters**: hundreds to thousands of galaxies bound by gravity. The **Virgo Cluster** (the nearest large cluster, ~54 million light-years) is the center of the **Local Supercluster** (Virgo Supercluster).
- **Superclusters**: vast collections of clusters, the largest known structures in the universe
- **Filaments, walls, and voids**: the large-scale structure of the universe resembles a cosmic web — filaments of galaxies surrounding enormous voids of nearly empty space

The **Observable Universe** spans about 93 billion light-years in diameter (accounting for cosmic expansion since the Big Bang). Within it, ~2 trillion galaxies are estimated to exist.

## 6. The Distance Ladder

Measuring distances in astronomy requires a hierarchy of methods — the **cosmic distance ladder**:

1. **Parallax**: the apparent shift of nearby stars against distant background stars as Earth orbits the Sun. Works up to a few thousand light-years.
2. **Standard candles**: objects with known intrinsic luminosity; distance is inferred from how dim they appear.
   - **Cepheid variables**: pulsating stars with a period-luminosity relationship; used by Hubble to establish that Andromeda is a separate galaxy, not a nebula in the Milky Way
   - **Type Ia supernovae**: uniform peak luminosity; used to measure distances to billions of light-years; the observations that revealed the accelerating expansion of the universe
3. **Redshift (Hubble's Law)**: distant galaxies have their light shifted toward the red end of the spectrum by cosmic expansion. The **Hubble constant** (H₀ ≈ 70 km/s/Mpc) relates recession velocity to distance.

---

## Summary

Galaxies are gravitationally bound systems containing billions to trillions of stars. They are classified as elliptical, spiral (including barred spirals like the Milky Way), lenticular, or irregular. The Milky Way is a barred spiral ~100,000 light-years across, with Sagittarius A* — a 4-million solar mass black hole — at its center. Galaxies form through accretion and mergers, cluster into groups and superclusters, and are arranged in a cosmic web of filaments and voids. Supermassive black holes reside in the centers of most large galaxies and power quasars and other active galactic nuclei.
