Beyond the planets, the solar system is filled with an enormous variety of smaller bodies — moons orbiting planets, rocky asteroids, icy comets, and countless fragments left over from the solar system's formation. Far from being empty, interplanetary space is a complex zoo of objects that hold clues to the solar system's history and occasionally make headlines when they come close to Earth.

---

## 1. Moons

A **natural satellite** (moon) is any natural body that orbits a planet or other body. The solar system contains over 290 known moons orbiting planets alone — and our understanding keeps growing as telescopes improve.

### Earth's Moon

The **Moon** is the fifth largest in the solar system and unusually large relative to its host planet. It formed ~4.5 billion years ago, probably from debris ejected when a Mars-sized body (**Theia**) collided with early Earth — the **giant impact hypothesis**. Evidence: the Moon has a composition similar to Earth's mantle; it lacks a substantial iron core despite forming near a large iron-rich body.

- **Diameter**: 3,474 km (about 1/4 of Earth's)
- **Distance**: average 384,400 km (varies — Moon's orbit is elliptical)
- **Orbital period** = **rotation period**: 27.3 days — this synchronous rotation (tidally locked) explains why we always see the same face
- **Surface**: heavily cratered highlands (ancient), dark basaltic plains (**maria** — ancient lava flows), and the far side (no maria; thicker crust)
- **No atmosphere**: no magnetic field to retain gas; solar wind strips it away
- **Tides**: the Moon's gravity causes tidal bulges in Earth's oceans; friction from tides is slowly transferring angular momentum from Earth to the Moon, causing Earth's rotation to slow (~1.4 ms/century) and the Moon to recede (~3.8 cm/year)

### Notable Moons of Other Planets

**Io (Jupiter)**: the most volcanically active body in the solar system. Over 400 active volcanoes continuously resurface it. Extreme volcanism is powered by **tidal flexing** — Jupiter's gravity and the gravitational pull of other Galilean moons knead Io's interior, generating heat.

**Europa (Jupiter)**: covered by a smooth, cracked ice shell ~10–30 km thick over a global subsurface liquid water ocean. The ocean contains more water than all of Earth's oceans combined. Considered one of the most promising places to search for life in the solar system. The Hubble Space Telescope has detected water vapor plumes erupting from its surface.

**Ganymede (Jupiter)**: largest moon in the solar system (larger than Mercury). Has its own magnetic field — the only moon known to have one.

**Callisto (Jupiter)**: the most heavily cratered body in the solar system; geologically dead for billions of years. May also have a subsurface ocean.

**Titan (Saturn)**: the only moon with a dense atmosphere (~1.5× Earth's pressure; mostly nitrogen). Has stable liquid methane and ethane lakes and rivers — the only surface liquids known outside Earth. The Huygens probe landed on Titan in 2005.

**Enceladus (Saturn)**: small (500 km diameter) but extremely active. A global subsurface ocean vents through the south polar region in geysers of water vapor, ice, and organic molecules. The Cassini spacecraft flew through these plumes and detected hydrogen — potentially indicating hydrothermal vents on the ocean floor. Another top candidate for life.

**Triton (Neptune)**: orbits Neptune retrograde (opposite Neptune's rotation), strongly suggesting it's a captured Kuiper Belt Object. Has geysers of nitrogen. Its retrograde orbit means tidal forces are decelerating it — it will eventually be torn apart by Neptune's gravity in ~3.6 billion years.

## 2. Asteroids

**Asteroids** are rocky or metallic bodies, mostly leftover from the solar system's formation, ranging from dust to ~940 km in diameter (Ceres, the largest — now classified as a dwarf planet).

### The Asteroid Belt

Located between Mars and Jupiter (2.2–3.2 AU), the main **asteroid belt** contains millions of objects, though their total mass is less than 4% of the Moon's mass. Jupiter's gravity prevented this material from accreting into a planet, and collisions have ground much of it to dust.

**Composition types:**
- **C-type (carbonaceous)**: most common (~75%); dark, carbon-rich, similar in composition to the Sun minus volatile elements; found in the outer belt
- **S-type (silicaceous)**: ~17%; stony, containing silicate minerals and some nickel-iron; found in the inner belt
- **M-type (metallic)**: metallic iron-nickel; may be remnants of the cores of differentiated asteroids that were shattered in collisions

### Near-Earth Asteroids (NEAs)

Asteroids whose orbits bring them within 1.3 AU of the Sun. Several categories based on orbit:
- **Atens**: orbit mostly inside Earth's orbit
- **Apollos**: Earth-crossing orbits with periods > 1 year
- **Amors**: orbits outside Earth's, but within Mars's

**Potentially Hazardous Asteroids (PHAs)**: NEAs that come within 0.05 AU of Earth's orbit and are large enough (>140 m) to cause regional damage if they impacted.

The Chicxulub impactor that ended the Cretaceous (~66 million years ago) was ~10 km in diameter — large enough to cause global extinction. NASA and other agencies actively track NEAs; no known object currently poses a significant impact threat in the next century.

**Planetary defense**: the DART mission (2022) successfully changed the orbit of the asteroid Dimorphos by impacting it — demonstrating that kinetic impactor technology can deflect potentially threatening asteroids.

## 3. Comets

**Comets** are icy bodies that develop luminous **comas** (atmospheres) and tails when they approach the Sun. Heat vaporizes volatile ices (water, CO₂, CO, methane), releasing gas and dust.

**Structure of an active comet:**
- **Nucleus**: solid, irregular body of ice and rock (typically 1–50 km)
- **Coma**: expanding cloud of gas and dust surrounding the nucleus; can be larger than Earth
- **Ion tail**: always points directly away from the Sun (pushed by solar wind); blue, straight
- **Dust tail**: curves along the comet's orbital path (affected by radiation pressure); white/yellow

### Origins

- **Short-period comets** (periods < 200 years): originate in the **Kuiper Belt** (beyond Neptune), perturbed into the inner solar system by gravitational interactions
- **Long-period comets** (periods > 200 years, up to millions of years): originate in the **Oort Cloud**, a spherical shell of icy bodies extending 1,000–100,000 AU; perturbed by passing stars or the galactic tide

### Notable Comets

- **Halley's Comet**: famous short-period comet (~76-year period); last visible from Earth in 1986; returns ~2061
- **Comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko**: visited by the ESA Rosetta spacecraft (2014–2016); the Philae lander made the first soft landing on a comet
- **Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9**: broke into fragments and impacted Jupiter in 1994 — witnessed directly

Comets are time capsules of early solar system material; studying their composition gives insight into conditions 4.6 billion years ago. They may have delivered water and organic molecules to early Earth.

---

## Summary

The solar system contains over 290 natural satellites, with notable examples including Europa (global subsurface ocean), Titan (dense atmosphere and liquid hydrocarbon lakes), and Enceladus (active geysers and potential hydrothermal vents). The asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter contains millions of rocky/metallic bodies that never coalesced into a planet. Near-Earth asteroids are tracked for impact risk. Comets are icy bodies from the Kuiper Belt and Oort Cloud that develop tails as they approach the Sun, and they carry primordial material from the solar system's formation.
