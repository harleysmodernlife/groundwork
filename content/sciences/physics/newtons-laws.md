In 1687, Isaac Newton published three laws of motion that unified the physics of everyday objects and celestial bodies under a single framework. These laws remain among the most useful and well-tested ideas in all of science. They describe not just how objects move, but *why* they move the way they do.

---

## 1. First Law: The Law of Inertia

**An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force.**

**Inertia** is the tendency of objects to resist changes to their state of motion. It is not a force — it is a property of mass. The more mass an object has, the more inertia it has, and the harder it is to change its motion.

This law overturned the Aristotelian view that objects naturally tend to stop. In the absence of friction and air resistance — forces that act on moving objects in everyday life — there is no reason for motion to stop. A hockey puck sliding on ice barely slows; a spacecraft in deep space, with no friction at all, continues indefinitely.

**Net force** matters here. Multiple forces can act on an object simultaneously. If they balance out (net force = zero), the object behaves as if no force is acting — it remains at rest or continues at constant velocity. This is called **equilibrium**.

---

## 2. Second Law: Force, Mass, and Acceleration

**The net force acting on an object equals the object's mass times its acceleration.**

**F_net = ma**

- Force (F) is measured in **newtons (N)**. One newton = 1 kg·m/s².
- Mass (m) is measured in kilograms.
- Acceleration (a) is measured in m/s².

This law encodes two relationships:
- **More force → more acceleration** (direct relationship): push harder and the object accelerates faster.
- **More mass → less acceleration** (inverse relationship): push the same force on a heavier object and it accelerates less.

This is why it's harder to stop a truck than a bicycle moving at the same speed — the truck has far more mass (and thus more inertia) requiring a much larger force to change its motion.

**Weight** is the force gravity exerts on an object: W = mg, where g = 9.8 m/s². Weight is a force; mass is not. Your mass is the same on the moon; your weight is one-sixth as much because the moon's gravitational acceleration is ~1.6 m/s².

---

## 3. Third Law: Action and Reaction

**For every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force.**

Forces always come in pairs. When you push on a wall, the wall pushes back on you with the same magnitude of force in the opposite direction. When a rocket ejects exhaust gases downward, the gases push the rocket upward. When Earth's gravity pulls you down, you pull Earth upward with the same force.

**Critical clarification**: action-reaction pairs act on *different objects*. They never cancel each other out. The wall doesn't accelerate much when you push it because of its enormous mass (F = ma; same F, huge m → tiny a). You might accelerate backward because your mass is much smaller.

### Why You Don't Fall Through the Floor

When you stand on the floor, two forces act on you: gravity pulls you down (weight = mg), and the floor pushes you up (**normal force**). These two forces acting on *you* are equal and opposite — they cancel, and you are in equilibrium (zero net force, zero acceleration). This is not Newton's Third Law; it is Newton's First Law (equilibrium). The Third Law pairs are: Earth pulling you down ↔ you pulling Earth up; floor pushing you up ↔ you pushing floor down.

## 4. Common Applications

### Friction

**Friction** is a force opposing relative motion between surfaces in contact. **Static friction** prevents objects from starting to slide; **kinetic friction** acts on objects already sliding. Friction = μN, where μ is the coefficient of friction and N is the normal force.

### Free Body Diagrams

A **free body diagram** isolates one object and shows all forces acting on it as arrows (vectors). It is the essential tool for applying Newton's laws to solve problems. Each arrow represents a real force with direction and approximate magnitude.

### Atwood Machine, Inclined Planes

Newton's Second Law applied to systems of connected objects (like blocks on ramps or over pulleys) requires identifying all forces on each object and writing F = ma for each, then solving the resulting equations simultaneously.

---

## Summary

Newton's First Law says objects resist changes to their motion — a net force is required to change velocity. The Second Law quantifies this: F = ma. The Third Law says forces come in equal, opposite pairs acting on different objects. Together these three laws explain virtually all motion we encounter in everyday life and formed the foundation of classical mechanics for over 200 years.
