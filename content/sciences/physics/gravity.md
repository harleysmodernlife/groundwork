Gravity is the weakest of the four fundamental forces, yet it dominates the universe at large scales — shaping the orbits of planets, the life cycles of stars, and the large-scale structure of the cosmos. Understanding gravity means understanding both its familiar, everyday effects and the deeper picture revealed by general relativity.

---

## 1. Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

In 1687, Newton proposed that every mass in the universe attracts every other mass with a gravitational force. The **Law of Universal Gravitation** states:

**F = G · (m₁ · m₂) / r²**

Where:
- F = gravitational force between the two objects (newtons)
- G = gravitational constant = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²
- m₁ and m₂ = the masses of the two objects (kg)
- r = distance between their centers (m)

### What This Means

- **Force increases with mass**: double either mass and the force doubles.
- **Force decreases with distance squared** (inverse-square law): double the distance and the force drops to one-quarter. Triple it and force drops to one-ninth.

Gravity is a mutual force — Earth pulls on you with the same force you pull on Earth. Earth doesn't move because its mass is so enormous that even a large force produces negligible acceleration (F = ma; enormous m → tiny a).

## 2. Gravitational Acceleration

Near Earth's surface, the acceleration due to gravity is approximately:

**g = 9.8 m/s²** (often rounded to 10 m/s² for quick estimates)

This value comes directly from Newton's law: g = G·M_Earth / R_Earth². It varies slightly with location — g is slightly smaller at the equator (larger radius) and at altitude.

The weight of an object is the gravitational force on it:

**W = mg**

Weight is a force; mass is not. A 70 kg person weighs about 686 N on Earth and about 115 N on the moon (where g ≈ 1.6 m/s²), but their mass is 70 kg in both places.

## 3. Orbital Motion

Orbital motion is gravity in action. The moon orbits Earth because Earth's gravity continuously pulls it inward — without this force, the moon would travel in a straight line into space.

An orbit is essentially continuous free fall: the object falls toward Earth, but is also moving sideways fast enough that Earth curves away as fast as the object falls. The result is a circular (or elliptical) path.

**Orbital speed** at a given altitude: v = √(G·M/r)

The **International Space Station** orbits at ~400 km altitude and travels at ~7.7 km/s (~27,700 km/h) to maintain orbit. Astronauts experience **weightlessness** not because there is no gravity in orbit — gravity there is about 90% as strong as at the surface — but because they and their spacecraft are both in free fall together. There is no contact force between them.

### Kepler's Laws

Before Newton, Johannes Kepler described planetary orbits empirically:
1. **Planets move in ellipses** with the sun at one focus.
2. **A line from the sun to a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times** (planets move faster when closer to the sun).
3. **The square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its average orbital radius** (T² ∝ r³).

Newton's gravitational law *derives* all three of Kepler's laws mathematically — one of the most powerful unifications in physics.

## 4. Tides

Earth's tides are caused by the difference in gravitational pull across Earth's diameter. The side of Earth closest to the moon is pulled more strongly than the far side. The water on the near side bulges toward the moon; water on the far side bulges away (left behind as Earth is pulled toward the moon more than the far-side water is). This creates **two tidal bulges**, producing two high tides and two low tides per day as Earth rotates through them.

The sun also exerts a tidal force on Earth. When the sun, moon, and Earth align (new and full moons), tidal forces reinforce — producing stronger **spring tides**. When they are at right angles (quarter moons), they partially cancel — producing weaker **neap tides**.

## 5. General Relativity: A Deeper Picture

Newton's gravity worked extraordinarily well for 200 years, but Einstein's **General Theory of Relativity** (1915) revealed a deeper truth: gravity is not a force in the usual sense. It is the **curvature of spacetime** caused by mass and energy.

Massive objects like stars and planets warp the fabric of spacetime around them. Other objects follow the straightest possible paths through this curved spacetime — and those paths appear to us as gravitational attraction.

General relativity predicts effects that Newtonian gravity cannot:
- **Gravitational time dilation**: time runs slower in stronger gravitational fields (GPS satellites must account for this)
- **Gravitational lensing**: light bends around massive objects, allowing astronomers to observe objects behind stars and galaxy clusters
- **Black holes**: regions where spacetime curvature is so extreme that not even light can escape
- **Gravitational waves**: ripples in spacetime caused by accelerating massive objects (detected directly for the first time in 2015)

---

## Summary

Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation describes gravity as an attractive force between all masses, proportional to mass and inversely proportional to distance squared. It explains orbital motion, tides, and the acceleration of falling objects. Einstein's General Relativity deepens the picture: gravity is the curvature of spacetime, explaining phenomena Newtonian gravity cannot — from GPS corrections to black holes and gravitational waves.
