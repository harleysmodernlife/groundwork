Two of the most practically important concepts in physics — friction and momentum — govern how objects interact through contact and collisions. Friction determines whether things slide or stay put. Momentum explains why collisions unfold the way they do and why stopping a moving object requires force applied over time.

---

## 1. Friction

**Friction** is a contact force that opposes relative motion (or the tendency of motion) between surfaces in contact. It arises from electromagnetic interactions between atoms at the surfaces.

### Types of Friction

- **Static friction (f_s)**: acts on objects that are not moving relative to each other. It adjusts to exactly counteract the applied force, up to a maximum value. This is why a gentle push doesn't move a heavy box — static friction perfectly matches your push. When the applied force exceeds the maximum static friction, the object starts to slide.
- **Kinetic friction (f_k)**: acts on objects already sliding against each other. It has a fixed value (for a given pair of surfaces and normal force) that is typically *less* than maximum static friction — which is why it's easier to keep sliding an object than to start it moving.
- **Rolling friction**: much smaller resistance on wheels or balls rolling across a surface. This is why wheels are so useful.

### The Friction Force Equation

**f = μN**

- μ (mu) = **coefficient of friction** — a dimensionless number describing how "grippy" the surfaces are. Different values for static (μ_s) and kinetic (μ_k) friction; typically μ_s > μ_k.
- N = **normal force** — the perpendicular contact force between surfaces (equal to weight on flat ground)

The coefficient depends entirely on the materials in contact. Steel on ice: μ ≈ 0.03. Rubber on dry concrete: μ ≈ 0.7. The contact area does not matter — doubling the area doubles the number of contact points but halves the pressure at each one; friction stays the same.

### Friction in Real Life

Friction makes walking possible (your foot would slide backward without it). It is the force that decelerates cars when braking (through tire-road friction) and through brake pads against rotors (sliding friction). Lubricants reduce kinetic friction by creating a thin film between surfaces, preventing direct contact.

## 2. Momentum

**Momentum** is the quantity of motion an object possesses — the product of its mass and velocity.

**p = mv**

- p = momentum (kg·m/s)
- m = mass (kg)
- v = velocity (m/s)

Momentum is a vector quantity — direction matters. A 1,000 kg car moving north at 20 m/s has the same *magnitude* of momentum as one moving south, but opposite momentum vectors.

### Why Momentum Matters: Impulse

Newton's Second Law can be rewritten in terms of momentum:

**F_net = Δp / Δt**   (net force equals change in momentum per unit time)

This leads to the concept of **impulse**:

**J = F·Δt = Δp**

Impulse is the product of force and time — it equals the change in momentum. This is why:
- Airbags save lives: by extending the time of impact, they reduce the force on a passenger (same Δp, larger Δt → smaller F)
- Catching a fast baseball with a stiff arm hurts more than following through and pulling the ball in (same Δp, shorter vs. longer Δt)
- Karate boards break because a fast, rigid strike delivers a large force in a very short time

---

## 3. Conservation of Momentum

One of the most powerful principles in physics: **in a closed system (no external forces), total momentum is conserved.**

**p_before = p_after**

This holds precisely whether the objects stick together or bounce apart.

### Elastic Collisions

Objects bounce off each other. Both momentum *and* kinetic energy are conserved. Billiard balls approximate elastic collisions.

### Inelastic Collisions

Objects collide and some kinetic energy is converted to heat, sound, or deformation. Momentum is still conserved; kinetic energy is not. Most real-world collisions are inelastic.

### Perfectly Inelastic Collisions

Objects stick together after impact and move as one. Maximum kinetic energy is lost (though momentum is still conserved). Car crashes are approximately perfectly inelastic.

### Worked Example

A 1,200 kg car moving at 15 m/s east collides with a stationary 800 kg car. They stick together. What is their combined velocity after the crash?

p_before = (1,200 kg)(15 m/s) + (800 kg)(0) = 18,000 kg·m/s east

p_after = (1,200 + 800) v = 2,000v

2,000v = 18,000
**v = 9 m/s east**

## 4. The Connection: Friction and Stopping Distance

Friction is what decelerates a sliding or braking vehicle. Combining kinematics, Newton's Second Law, and friction:

- Braking force = μ_k · m · g
- Deceleration = μ_k · g
- Using v² = v₀² + 2aΔx: stopping distance = v₀² / (2 · μ_k · g)

Doubling speed quadruples stopping distance. This is why highway speeds are so dangerous — it's not just the speed but the energy and stopping distance that scale with v².

---

## Summary

Friction is a contact force opposing motion, calculated as the coefficient of friction times the normal force. Static friction prevents motion up to a limit; kinetic friction acts during sliding. Momentum is mass times velocity, and impulse is the change in momentum caused by a force over time. In any closed system, total momentum is conserved in all collisions — even when kinetic energy is not.
