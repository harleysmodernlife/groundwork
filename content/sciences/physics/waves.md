Waves are one of the most fundamental phenomena in nature. Light, sound, ocean swells, seismic tremors, radio signals, and the quantum behavior of particles are all wave phenomena. Understanding waves — what they are, how they behave, and how they interact — is essential to physics, engineering, and modern technology.

---

## 1. What Is a Wave?

A **wave** is a disturbance that transfers energy through a medium (or through space) without transferring matter. When you drop a stone in a pond, the water molecules move up and down but don't travel across the pond — the *energy* does.

### Transverse vs. Longitudinal Waves

- **Transverse waves**: the medium moves *perpendicular* to the direction of wave travel. Example: a wave on a string, ocean waves (approximately), light waves. The oscillation is "side to side" while the wave moves "forward."
- **Longitudinal waves**: the medium moves *parallel* to the direction of wave travel, in alternating compressions and rarefactions. Example: sound waves, seismic P-waves.

---

## 2. Wave Properties

### Amplitude

**Amplitude** is the maximum displacement of the medium from its rest position. For a transverse wave on a string, it's how far the string moves up or down. Amplitude is related to the **energy** carried by the wave — larger amplitude means more energy.

### Wavelength

**Wavelength (λ)** is the distance between two consecutive identical points in phase — peak to peak, or trough to trough. Measured in meters.

### Frequency and Period

**Frequency (f)** is the number of complete wave cycles that pass a point per second. Measured in **hertz (Hz)**: 1 Hz = 1 cycle/second.

**Period (T)** is the time for one complete cycle: T = 1/f.

### Wave Speed

**v = fλ**

Wave speed equals frequency times wavelength. For a given medium, wave speed is fixed — so higher frequency means shorter wavelength, and lower frequency means longer wavelength.

- Speed of sound in air: ~343 m/s (at 20°C)
- Speed of light in vacuum: 3 × 10⁸ m/s (299,792,458 m/s exactly)

---

## 3. Sound Waves

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave — it requires a medium to travel and cannot propagate through a vacuum. It travels as pressure variations in air, water, or solid material.

**Human hearing range**: approximately 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.
- Below 20 Hz: **infrasound** (felt more than heard; produced by earthquakes, large animals)
- Above 20,000 Hz: **ultrasound** (used in medical imaging and sonar)

**Loudness (intensity)** relates to amplitude. **Pitch** relates to frequency. The **Doppler effect** describes how a wave's observed frequency changes when the source or observer is moving — the ambulance siren rises in pitch as it approaches and falls as it passes.

---

## 4. Electromagnetic Waves

Unlike sound, **electromagnetic waves** (EM waves) are transverse waves that require no medium — they can travel through vacuum. They consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields and always travel at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum.

The **electromagnetic spectrum** spans an enormous range of frequencies and wavelengths:

| Type | Wavelength | Applications |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| Radio waves | > 1 mm | Broadcasting, WiFi, MRI |
| Microwaves | 1 mm – 1 m | Cooking, radar, mobile phones |
| Infrared | 700 nm – 1 mm | Heat, remote controls, night vision |
| Visible light | 400–700 nm | Human vision |
| Ultraviolet | 10–400 nm | Sterilization, causes sunburn |
| X-rays | 0.01–10 nm | Medical imaging, security |
| Gamma rays | < 0.01 nm | Nuclear medicine, cancer treatment |

## 5. Wave Behavior

### Reflection

Waves bounce off surfaces. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. This is why mirrors work and why sound echoes off walls.

### Refraction

Waves change direction when they cross the boundary between two media at different speeds. Light bends when entering glass or water — this is why a straw appears bent in a glass of water and why lenses can focus light.

### Diffraction

Waves bend around obstacles and spread through openings. The effect is most pronounced when the obstacle or opening is similar in size to the wavelength. This is why you can hear someone around a corner (sound diffracts easily) but can't see them (light wavelengths are tiny compared to doorway openings).

### Interference

When two waves overlap, their amplitudes add:
- **Constructive interference**: peaks align with peaks → larger amplitude
- **Destructive interference**: peaks align with troughs → reduced or zero amplitude

Noise-canceling headphones use destructive interference — generating a sound wave exactly opposite to ambient noise, canceling it.

### Standing Waves

When a wave reflects back on itself, the incoming and reflected waves interfere to form a **standing wave** — a pattern of fixed **nodes** (zero amplitude) and **antinodes** (maximum amplitude). String instruments produce sound through standing waves on strings; wind instruments use standing waves in air columns.

---

## Summary

Waves transfer energy without transferring matter. Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to travel direction; longitudinal waves oscillate parallel to it. Key wave properties are amplitude, wavelength, frequency, and speed (v = fλ). Sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave; electromagnetic waves (including light) require no medium. All waves can reflect, refract, diffract, and interfere — behaviors that explain phenomena from lenses to noise-canceling headphones to musical instruments.
