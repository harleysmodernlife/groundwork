Chemical reactions can look wildly different from each other — a metal dissolving in acid, a fuel igniting, two solutions producing a precipitate, a compound splitting under heat. But beneath this variety, reactions fall into recognizable patterns. Understanding reaction types allows chemists to predict products, balance equations efficiently, and design syntheses.

---

## 1. What Is a Chemical Reaction?

In a **chemical reaction**, atoms are rearranged as existing bonds break and new bonds form. The starting materials are **reactants**; the ending materials are **products**. Atoms are conserved — they are neither created nor destroyed (consistent with the Law of Conservation of Mass).

Evidence that a reaction has occurred includes: color change, gas production, precipitate formation, temperature change, or light emission.

---

## 2. Synthesis (Combination) Reactions

Two or more substances combine to form a single product.

**A + B → AB**

Examples:
- 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl (sodium burning in chlorine gas)
- CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (quicklime reacting with water)
- N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ (Haber process for ammonia)

Often, at least one reactant is a highly reactive element (alkali metal, halogen), or the reaction requires high temperature or pressure.

---

## 3. Decomposition Reactions

A single compound breaks into two or more simpler substances.

**AB → A + B**

Examples:
- 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ (hydrogen peroxide decomposition)
- 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂ (mercuric oxide decomposition — Lavoisier's classic experiment)
- CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (limestone decomposition, requires high heat)

Decompositions often require an input of energy (heat, light, electricity) to proceed. **Electrolysis** is a special case — an electric current decomposes a compound (e.g., 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂).

---

## 4. Single Replacement (Single Displacement) Reactions

One element replaces another in a compound.

**A + BC → AC + B**

Whether the reaction occurs depends on the **activity series** — a ranking of elements by their tendency to displace others from compounds. More active elements displace less active ones; less active elements cannot displace more active ones.

**Activity series (partial, most to least active):**
Li, K, Ba, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Ni, Sn, Pb, H, Cu, Ag, Au

Examples:
- Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ (zinc is above hydrogen → displaces it from acid)
- Cu + 2AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag (copper displaces silver; you can watch silver crystals form)
- Au + HCl → no reaction (gold is below hydrogen in the activity series)

---

## 5. Double Replacement (Double Displacement / Metathesis) Reactions

The cations and anions of two ionic compounds exchange partners.

**AB + CD → AD + CB**

A reaction typically occurs if one of the products:
- Is **insoluble** (forms a precipitate — solid that comes out of solution)
- Is a **gas** that escapes
- Is a **molecular compound** (especially water — the driving force of neutralization)

### Precipitation Reactions

Two aqueous ionic compounds react to form an insoluble solid product.

AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)

Silver chloride (AgCl) is insoluble and precipitates out as a white solid. The **solubility rules** predict which products are insoluble.

**Key solubility rules (common ones):**
- All Group 1 salts and ammonium (NH₄⁺) salts are soluble
- All nitrates (NO₃⁻) are soluble
- Most chlorides are soluble, except AgCl, PbCl₂, Hg₂Cl₂
- Most sulfates are soluble, except BaSO₄, PbSO₄, CaSO₄
- Most carbonates, hydroxides, and phosphates are insoluble, except Group 1 and NH₄⁺

### Gas-Forming Reactions

Na₂S + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂S(g)
Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂(g)

Commonly produced gases: H₂S, CO₂, SO₂, NH₃.

---

## 6. Combustion Reactions

A substance rapidly reacts with **oxygen**, releasing heat and light (burning). The result is often **CO₂ and H₂O** for organic compounds.

**Complete combustion** (excess oxygen):
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (natural gas burning)
C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O (propane)

**Incomplete combustion** (limited oxygen):
Produces carbon monoxide (CO) and/or carbon (soot) instead of CO₂.
2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O
This is why a gas burner with insufficient airflow has a yellow, sooty flame instead of a clean blue one.

Metals also combust:
4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ (iron rusting is very slow combustion; iron burning in oxygen is fast)
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (brilliant white light)

---

## 7. Oxidation-Reduction (Redox) Reactions

Many of the above reaction types (particularly single replacement and combustion) involve electron transfer — **redox reactions**.

- **Oxidation**: loss of electrons (also: increase in oxidation state)
- **Reduction**: gain of electrons

Memory aid: **OIL RIG** — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain

The substance that is oxidized is the **reducing agent** (it gives electrons away). The substance that is reduced is the **oxidizing agent** (it takes electrons).

Redox reactions are central to:
- **Combustion**: fuel is oxidized, oxygen is reduced
- **Batteries and electrochemical cells**: spontaneous redox reactions generate electric current
- **Electrolysis**: applied electric current drives non-spontaneous redox reactions
- **Metabolism**: cellular respiration is a controlled redox reaction — glucose is oxidized, oxygen is reduced

---

## 8. Net Ionic Equations

Reactions in aqueous solution are often written as **net ionic equations** — showing only the species that actually change, omitting **spectator ions** (ions that are present but don't participate).

**Molecular equation:** AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)
**Full ionic equation:** Ag⁺ + NO₃⁻ + Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s) + Na⁺ + NO₃⁻
**Net ionic equation:** Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

The spectator ions (Na⁺ and NO₃⁻) appear on both sides and are removed. The net ionic equation shows what's actually happening chemically.

---

## Summary

The main reaction types are synthesis (A + B → AB), decomposition (AB → A + B), single replacement (A displaces B in a compound), double replacement (ionic partners exchange), and combustion (rapid oxidation releasing heat and light). Whether a double replacement occurs depends on whether a product is insoluble, gaseous, or molecular. Many reactions involve redox — transfer of electrons — which underpins combustion, batteries, and biological energy. Net ionic equations show only the species that actually change.
