Most atoms are stable — their nuclei persist indefinitely. But for many combinations of protons and neutrons, the nucleus is unstable and spontaneously transforms, releasing energy and particles in the process. This **radioactive decay** is not a chemical reaction; it's a nuclear event that changes the atom itself. Understanding radioactivity has given us nuclear power, medical imaging, cancer treatments, and the ability to date ancient materials.

---

## 1. Isotopes

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) that differ in their number of neutrons — and therefore in their mass number.

- Carbon-12 (¹²C): 6 protons, 6 neutrons — stable, 98.9% of natural carbon
- Carbon-13 (¹³C): 6 protons, 7 neutrons — stable, 1.1% of natural carbon
- Carbon-14 (¹⁴C): 6 protons, 8 neutrons — unstable (radioactive), trace amounts

The chemical symbol notation: ᴬZX where A = mass number, Z = atomic number, X = element symbol.

Isotopes of an element have virtually identical chemical properties (because chemistry depends on electron configuration, which depends on proton count, not neutrons). They differ in:
- Mass (affecting reaction rates slightly for light elements)
- Nuclear stability
- Radioactivity

The **atomic mass** on the periodic table is a weighted average of the masses of all naturally occurring isotopes.

---

## 2. Nuclear Stability

The stability of a nucleus depends on the ratio of neutrons to protons. For light elements, stable nuclei have roughly equal protons and neutrons (N/Z ≈ 1). As atomic number increases, more neutrons are needed to dilute proton-proton repulsion — the N/Z ratio in stable heavy nuclei approaches 1.5.

The **band of stability** on a plot of neutrons vs. protons encompasses all stable nuclei. Nuclei outside this band are radioactive and decay toward it.

Elements with Z > 83 (bismuth) have no stable isotopes — all are radioactive.

## 3. Types of Radioactive Decay

### Alpha Decay (α)

The nucleus emits an **alpha particle** — identical to a helium-4 nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons).

Result: atomic number decreases by 2; mass number decreases by 4.

Example: ²³⁸U → ²³⁴Th + ⁴He

Alpha particles are relatively large and slow. They are stopped by a sheet of paper or a few centimeters of air. However, if an alpha emitter is inhaled or ingested, the particles cause severe damage to nearby tissue.

### Beta Decay (β)

**Beta-minus decay**: a neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron (beta particle) and an antineutrino. Atomic number increases by 1; mass number unchanged.

¹⁴C → ¹⁴N + e⁻ + antineutrino (used in radiocarbon dating)

**Beta-plus decay (positron emission)**: a proton converts to a neutron, emitting a positron. Atomic number decreases by 1. Used in PET (positron emission tomography) scans.

Beta particles are stopped by a few millimeters of aluminum or a few meters of air.

### Gamma Decay (γ)

High-energy electromagnetic radiation emitted when an excited nucleus releases energy. No change in atomic number or mass number — only energy change.

Gamma rays are extremely penetrating — require several centimeters of lead or meters of concrete for shielding. Used in cancer radiation therapy and sterilizing medical equipment.

### Electron Capture

The nucleus captures an inner orbital electron. A proton + electron → neutron. Atomic number decreases by 1.

## 4. Half-Life

**Half-life (t₁/₂)** is the time required for half of the atoms in a radioactive sample to decay. Each radioactive isotope has a characteristic, fixed half-life ranging from fractions of a second to billions of years.

Half-lives of selected isotopes:
- Francium-223: 22 minutes
- Iodine-131: 8.02 days
- Strontium-90: 28.8 years
- Carbon-14: 5,730 years
- Uranium-238: 4.47 billion years

After n half-lives, the fraction remaining = (1/2)ⁿ.

After 3 half-lives: (1/2)³ = 1/8 (12.5%) of the original sample remains.

---

## 5. Radiocarbon Dating

**Radiocarbon dating** uses the known half-life of carbon-14 (5,730 years) to determine the age of organic materials up to about 50,000 years old.

While alive, organisms constantly exchange carbon with the environment, maintaining a constant ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio. At death, exchange stops and ¹⁴C decays while ¹²C does not. By measuring the current ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio and comparing to the atmospheric ratio, the time since death can be calculated.

Other radiometric dating methods use longer-lived isotopes for dating much older materials:
- **Potassium-40 / Argon-40** (half-life 1.25 billion years): dates rocks and volcanic ash
- **Uranium-238 / Lead-206** (half-life 4.47 billion years): dates the oldest rocks and meteorites; established Earth's age at ~4.54 billion years

## 6. Nuclear Reactions: Fission and Fusion

**Nuclear fission**: a heavy nucleus (e.g., ²³⁵U) is struck by a neutron and splits into lighter nuclei, releasing enormous energy and additional neutrons (which can trigger more fissions — a **chain reaction**). This is the principle behind nuclear power plants and atomic bombs.

**Nuclear fusion**: light nuclei (e.g., hydrogen isotopes deuterium and tritium) fuse to form helium, releasing far more energy per unit mass than fission. This powers the sun and all stars. Controlled fusion on Earth remains an active area of research — it promises abundant, clean energy with minimal waste.

**Why so much energy?** Einstein's E = mc² — when mass is converted in nuclear reactions, a small mass change (Δm) releases enormous energy (E = Δm·c²). The "mass defect" — the difference between the mass of the nucleus and the sum of its free constituent particles — becomes binding energy.

---

## 7. Medical and Industrial Applications

- **Medical imaging**: Tc-99m (technetium-99m, a gamma emitter with a 6-hour half-life) is the most widely used diagnostic radioisotope, used in bone scans, heart imaging, and organ studies
- **PET scans**: fluorine-18 or other positron emitters produce detectable gamma rays when positrons annihilate with electrons
- **Cancer treatment**: radiation therapy uses gamma rays or particle beams to destroy cancer cells; radioiodine therapy uses I-131 to destroy thyroid tissue
- **Industrial uses**: irradiation sterilizes food and medical equipment; thickness gauges in manufacturing use beta particles; smoke detectors use Am-241 (alpha emitter)

---

## Summary

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different neutron counts. Unstable nuclei undergo radioactive decay — alpha (loses helium nucleus), beta (neutron-proton conversion), or gamma (energy release). Each radioisotope has a fixed half-life. Radiocarbon dating uses ¹⁴C decay to date organic material; other isotope pairs date geological and cosmic events. Nuclear fission powers reactors; nuclear fusion powers stars. Medical applications of radioactivity include imaging, diagnosis, and cancer treatment.
