Everything you can see, touch, taste, or smell is made of atoms. The diversity of all matter — from air to water to living tissue to stars — arises from just over a hundred kinds of atoms combining in an enormous variety of ways. Understanding atoms and elements is the starting point for all of chemistry.

---

## 1. The Atom

An **atom** is the smallest unit of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element. Atoms are almost unimaginably small — a single human hair is about a million atoms wide.

Atoms are made of three fundamental particles:

| Particle | Charge | Mass (relative) | Location |
|----------|--------|-----------------|----------|
| Proton | +1 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Electron | −1 | ~1/1836 | Electron cloud |

The **nucleus** — containing protons and neutrons — is incredibly small relative to the atom as a whole. If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a marble at the center. The electrons occupy a diffuse cloud of probability around the nucleus.

---

## 2. Elements

An **element** is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom. Elements are defined by their **atomic number** — the number of protons in the nucleus. All atoms with 6 protons are carbon; all with 8 are oxygen; all with 79 are gold.

**Mass number** = number of protons + number of neutrons in the nucleus.

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (same atomic number) with different numbers of neutrons — and therefore different mass numbers. Carbon has three natural isotopes:
- Carbon-12 (⁶¹²C): 6 protons, 6 neutrons — 99% of natural carbon
- Carbon-13 (⁶¹³C): 6 protons, 7 neutrons
- Carbon-14 (⁶¹⁴C): 6 protons, 8 neutrons — radioactive; used in radiocarbon dating

## 3. The Periodic Table

The **periodic table** is the most important organizational tool in chemistry. It arranges all known elements by increasing atomic number in a grid that reveals patterns in chemical behavior.

### Periods and Groups

- **Periods** (rows): elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells
- **Groups** (columns): elements in the same group have the same number of **valence electrons** (outermost electrons) and therefore similar chemical properties

### Key Groups

- **Group 1 — Alkali metals**: 1 valence electron; highly reactive; react vigorously with water (lithium, sodium, potassium...)
- **Group 2 — Alkaline earth metals**: 2 valence electrons; reactive but less so than Group 1 (magnesium, calcium...)
- **Groups 3–12 — Transition metals**: variable valence electrons; generally less reactive; include iron, copper, gold, silver
- **Group 17 — Halogens**: 7 valence electrons; highly reactive non-metals; need just one more electron to complete their outer shell (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine)
- **Group 18 — Noble gases**: 8 valence electrons (or 2 for helium); full outer shell; nearly chemically inert (helium, neon, argon)

### Metals, Non-metals, and Metalloids

- **Metals** (left and center): shiny, conduct electricity and heat, malleable, ductile; lose electrons in reactions
- **Non-metals** (upper right): poor conductors, brittle in solid form; gain electrons in reactions
- **Metalloids** (staircase boundary): intermediate properties; semiconductors like silicon are metalloids

## 4. Atomic Mass and Moles

The **atomic mass** (also called atomic weight) listed on the periodic table is a weighted average of the masses of all naturally occurring isotopes of an element. Carbon's atomic mass of 12.011 reflects the mix of C-12 and C-13.

The **mole** is chemistry's counting unit, defined as 6.022 × 10²³ entities (Avogadro's number). One mole of any element has a mass in grams equal to its atomic mass. One mole of carbon atoms = 12.011 grams. This allows chemists to count atoms by weighing samples.

---

## 5. Electron Configuration and Chemical Behavior

Electrons occupy energy levels (shells) around the nucleus. The outermost electrons — **valence electrons** — determine an element's chemical behavior.

Atoms seek a full outermost shell (typically 8 electrons — the **octet rule**). They achieve this by:
- **Losing electrons** → become positively charged ions (**cations**) — common in metals
- **Gaining electrons** → become negatively charged ions (**anions**) — common in non-metals
- **Sharing electrons** → form covalent bonds

The number of valence electrons (equal to the group number for main-group elements) determines how an element bonds. Sodium (Group 1) has 1 valence electron and readily gives it up. Chlorine (Group 17) has 7 and readily gains one. When they meet, sodium gives its electron to chlorine, forming sodium chloride (NaCl) — table salt.

---

## Summary

Atoms are composed of protons, neutrons, and electrons. Elements are defined by their atomic number (proton count) and organized in the periodic table by increasing atomic number. Elements in the same group share the same number of valence electrons and similar chemical properties. Atoms interact chemically through their valence electrons, seeking a full outer shell by losing, gaining, or sharing electrons. The mole provides a practical unit for counting atoms in measurable quantities.
