The atom is not a solid billiard ball. Over a century of experiments has revealed that atoms have an internal structure of extraordinary complexity — a tiny, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons that exist not in fixed orbits but in probabilistic clouds. Understanding atomic structure explains the periodic table, chemical bonding, and why matter has the properties it does.

---

## 1. The Development of Atomic Models

Our understanding of atomic structure has evolved through successive models, each replacing its predecessor when experimental evidence demanded a better explanation.

### Dalton's Model (1803)

John Dalton proposed that matter is made of indivisible, identical atoms — solid, featureless spheres. Atoms of the same element are identical; atoms of different elements are different. Chemical reactions rearrange atoms but don't create or destroy them. Correct in essentials; wrong about indivisibility.

### Thomson's Plum Pudding Model (1897)

J.J. Thomson discovered the **electron** by observing cathode rays deflect in electric and magnetic fields. Since atoms are neutral and contain negative electrons, he proposed that atoms were spheres of positive charge with electrons embedded throughout — like raisins in a pudding.

### Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911)

Ernest Rutherford fired alpha particles (helium nuclei) at a thin gold foil. Most passed straight through; a few were deflected at large angles. This was impossible if positive charge were spread uniformly — the deflected particles had encountered something very small and very dense.

Conclusion: the atom is mostly empty space, with a tiny, dense, positively charged **nucleus** at the center and electrons somewhere in the surrounding space. Rutherford's model didn't explain electron arrangement — a negative electron orbiting a positive nucleus should spiral inward and emit radiation, collapsing in nanoseconds.

### Bohr's Model (1913)

Niels Bohr proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus only in specific, allowed energy levels — like rungs on a ladder — and don't radiate energy while in these orbits. Electrons can jump to higher levels by absorbing energy and release energy as light when they fall to lower levels. This explained the emission spectrum of hydrogen — the specific colors of light hydrogen emits.

Bohr's model works well for hydrogen but fails for multi-electron atoms.

### The Quantum Mechanical Model (Current)

The modern model, developed in the 1920s through quantum mechanics (de Broglie, Schrödinger, Heisenberg), treats electrons as waves rather than particles in defined orbits. The **Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle** states that we cannot simultaneously know an electron's exact position and momentum.

Instead of orbits, electrons occupy **orbitals** — regions of space where there is a high probability of finding the electron. The shape and energy of these orbitals are described mathematically by wave functions.

## 2. Quantum Numbers and Orbitals

Each electron in an atom is described by four **quantum numbers**:

- **Principal quantum number (n)**: energy level/shell. n = 1, 2, 3, ... Higher n = higher energy and greater average distance from nucleus.
- **Angular momentum quantum number (l)**: subshell shape. l = 0 to (n−1). l=0 → s orbital (spherical); l=1 → p orbital (dumbbell); l=2 → d orbital; l=3 → f orbital.
- **Magnetic quantum number (m_l)**: orientation of the orbital in space. s has 1 orientation; p has 3; d has 5; f has 7.
- **Spin quantum number (m_s)**: electron spin. Either +½ or −½ (up or down). Each orbital can hold at most 2 electrons (with opposite spins) — the **Pauli Exclusion Principle**.

Orbital capacity by subshell: s = 2; p = 6; d = 10; f = 14.

## 3. Electron Configuration

**Electron configuration** describes how electrons are distributed among an atom's orbitals. Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy (**Aufbau principle**), occupy orbitals singly before doubling up (**Hund's rule**), and no two electrons share all four quantum numbers (**Pauli Exclusion Principle**).

Energy order of subshells (approximate): 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p...

**Examples:**
- Hydrogen (Z=1): 1s¹
- Carbon (Z=6): 1s² 2s² 2p²
- Sodium (Z=11): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ (or [Ne] 3s¹ using noble gas core notation)

The **valence electrons** (in the outermost shell) determine chemical behavior. Sodium has one valence electron (3s¹), explaining its reactivity and tendency to form +1 ions.

## 4. The Nucleus in Detail

### Protons and Neutrons

Protons and neutrons are made of still smaller particles called **quarks**, held together by the **strong nuclear force** — the most powerful fundamental force, operating only at nuclear scales.

The strong force overcomes the electromagnetic repulsion between positively charged protons packed in the nucleus. The balance between these forces determines nuclear stability.

### Nuclear Stability and Radioactivity

Most combinations of protons and neutrons are unstable and undergo **radioactive decay**:
- **Alpha decay**: nucleus emits an alpha particle (2 protons + 2 neutrons); atomic number decreases by 2
- **Beta decay**: a neutron converts to a proton (or vice versa), emitting an electron or positron; atomic number changes by 1
- **Gamma decay**: high-energy photon emitted; composition unchanged; reduces energy

The ratio of neutrons to protons determines stability. Light elements are stable with roughly equal protons and neutrons; heavier elements need more neutrons than protons to dilute proton-proton repulsion.

## 5. Electromagnetic Spectrum and Atomic Spectra

When electrons transition between energy levels, they absorb or emit photons of specific energies:
- **Absorption spectrum**: electrons absorb photons and jump to higher energy levels; specific wavelengths are absorbed from white light, leaving dark lines
- **Emission spectrum**: electrons fall from excited states to lower levels; photons of specific colors are emitted

Each element has a unique spectral "fingerprint." This allows astronomers to identify elements in distant stars and nebulae from their light — one of the most powerful tools in astrophysics.

---

## Summary

Atomic models evolved from Dalton's solid sphere to Rutherford's nuclear model to the modern quantum mechanical model, where electrons exist in probabilistic orbitals described by quantum numbers. Electron configuration describes how electrons fill orbitals by energy. The nucleus contains protons and neutrons held by the strong force; unstable nuclei undergo radioactive decay. Electrons jumping between energy levels produce the unique emission and absorption spectra that identify elements.
