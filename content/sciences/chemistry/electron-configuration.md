Electron configuration is the map of how electrons are distributed among an atom's energy levels and subshells. It explains why elements in the same column of the periodic table have similar chemical properties, why some atoms readily form ions, and why the periodic table has the structure it does. Knowing how to write and interpret electron configurations is essential for understanding chemical bonding.

---

## 1. Energy Levels and Subshells

Electrons occupy **energy levels** (also called shells, labeled n = 1, 2, 3, ...). Within each energy level are **subshells**, each with a specific shape:

- **s subshell**: 1 orbital, spherical shape, holds up to 2 electrons
- **p subshell**: 3 orbitals, dumbbell shape, holds up to 6 electrons
- **d subshell**: 5 orbitals, holds up to 10 electrons
- **f subshell**: 7 orbitals, holds up to 14 electrons

Subshells are labeled by the energy level and letter: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f...

**Maximum electrons per energy level**: 2n²
- n=1: 2 electrons (just 1s)
- n=2: 8 electrons (2s + 2p)
- n=3: 18 electrons (3s + 3p + 3d)

---

## 2. Rules for Filling Orbitals

Three principles govern how electrons fill orbitals:

### The Aufbau Principle

Electrons fill orbitals starting from the lowest available energy. The order is not simply by energy level — subshells of different levels overlap:

**Energy fill order: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p**

The **diagonal rule** (or arrow diagram) is a memory aid: subshells are listed diagonally and filled following arrows from upper right to lower left, giving the order above.

Note the anomaly: 4s fills *before* 3d. This is because the 4s subshell is at lower energy than 3d in neutral atoms (though this reverses slightly in ions).

### The Pauli Exclusion Principle

No two electrons in the same atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers. In practice: each orbital holds a maximum of **2 electrons** with opposite spins (represented as ↑↓).

### Hund's Rule

When filling orbitals of the same energy (degenerate orbitals — i.e., the three 2p orbitals), electrons enter each orbital singly before any orbital receives a second electron. All singly occupied orbitals have the same spin direction.

This minimizes electron-electron repulsion. Think of it as "one person per seat before anyone sits in the middle."

## 3. Writing Electron Configurations

The notation lists each subshell with a superscript indicating the number of electrons:

**Format**: (energy level)(subshell)(number of electrons)

**Examples:**

- Hydrogen (H, Z=1): **1s¹**
- Helium (He, Z=2): **1s²** ← full first shell
- Lithium (Li, Z=3): **1s² 2s¹**
- Carbon (C, Z=6): **1s² 2s² 2p²**
- Nitrogen (N, Z=7): **1s² 2s² 2p³**
- Neon (Ne, Z=10): **1s² 2s² 2p⁶** ← full second shell
- Sodium (Na, Z=11): **1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹**
- Chlorine (Cl, Z=17): **1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵**
- Calcium (Ca, Z=20): **1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s²**

### Noble Gas Shorthand (Core Notation)

To save writing, the configuration of a noble gas core is written in brackets and the remaining electrons are listed:

- Sodium: [Ne] 3s¹
- Chlorine: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵
- Iron (Fe, Z=26): [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²

---

## 4. Orbital Box Diagrams

An **orbital box diagram** shows each orbital as a box (or line) and each electron as an arrow (↑ or ↓). This makes Hund's Rule and the Pauli Exclusion Principle visually clear.

For Carbon (1s² 2s² 2p²):
```
1s:  [↑↓]
2s:  [↑↓]
2p:  [↑] [↑] [ ]
```
The two 2p electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins — Hund's Rule.

## 5. Valence Electrons and Periodicity

**Valence electrons** are the electrons in the outermost occupied shell (the highest n value). They determine chemical behavior and bonding.

The number of valence electrons equals the group number for main-group (s and p block) elements:
- Group 1: 1 valence electron
- Group 2: 2 valence electrons
- Groups 13–18: 3–8 valence electrons

This is why elements in the same group behave similarly — they have the same number of valence electrons in equivalent configurations.

### Periods Explained

The periodic table's structure directly reflects electron configuration:
- **Period 1** (2 elements): fills the 1s subshell
- **Period 2** (8 elements): fills 2s and 2p
- **Period 3** (8 elements): fills 3s and 3p
- **Periods 4 and 5** (18 elements): fill 4s, 3d (or 4d), and 4p (or 5p)
- **Periods 6 and 7** (32 elements): include f-block elements (lanthanides and actinides)

---

## 6. Electron Configuration of Ions

When atoms form ions, electrons are added (anions) or removed (cations):
- **Cations** (metals losing electrons): remove electrons from the highest energy occupied subshell first. For iron: neutral Fe is [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²; Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶ (the 4s electrons are lost first); Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵.
- **Anions** (non-metals gaining electrons): add electrons to the next available orbital. Cl is [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵; Cl⁻ is [Ne] 3s² 3p⁶ (equivalent to argon's configuration).

Ions with complete outer shells (noble gas configuration) are especially stable — this is the driving force behind ionic bond formation.

---

## Summary

Electron configuration describes the distribution of electrons among an atom's energy levels and subshells. The Aufbau principle, Pauli Exclusion Principle, and Hund's Rule govern how electrons fill orbitals. Valence electrons — those in the outermost shell — determine chemical reactivity. The periodic table's structure directly reflects electron configurations: elements in the same group have the same valence electron count, and each period corresponds to filling a new energy level.
