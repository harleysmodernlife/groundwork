Stoichiometry is the quantitative heart of chemistry — the mathematics of chemical reactions. A balanced equation tells you the *ratio* in which reactants combine and products form. Stoichiometry uses that ratio and the mole concept to calculate exactly how much of anything is involved: how much product you can make, how much reactant you need, or what's left over when a reaction is complete.

---

## 1. The Mole: Chemistry's Counting Unit

Atoms and molecules are extraordinarily small — there are about 50 trillion atoms in a single grain of sand. To work with practical quantities of matter, chemists use the **mole** (mol).

**1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number)**

This number is chosen so that the **molar mass** of any element (the mass of one mole) in grams numerically equals its atomic mass in atomic mass units (amu):
- 1 mol of carbon (atomic mass 12.011 amu) = 12.011 grams
- 1 mol of iron (atomic mass 55.845 amu) = 55.845 grams

For compounds, the **molar mass** is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in the formula:
- Water (H₂O): 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.016 g/mol
- Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆): 6(12.011) + 12(1.008) + 6(16.00) = 180.16 g/mol

**Conversions:**
- Grams → Moles: divide by molar mass
- Moles → Grams: multiply by molar mass
- Moles → Particles: multiply by 6.022 × 10²³
- Particles → Moles: divide by 6.022 × 10²³

---

## 2. Mole Ratios from Balanced Equations

The coefficients in a balanced equation express the **mole ratios** of reactants and products.

**2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**

This means:
- 2 mol H₂ reacts with 1 mol O₂ to produce 2 mol H₂O
- If you have 4 mol H₂, you need 2 mol O₂ and produce 4 mol H₂O
- If you have 0.5 mol O₂, you need 1 mol H₂ and produce 1 mol H₂O

The ratio 2:1:2 applies at any scale — it's a ratio of moles, not grams.

---

## 3. The Stoichiometry Roadmap

Most stoichiometry problems follow this path:

**Grams of A → Moles of A → Moles of B → Grams of B**

1. Convert given grams to moles using molar mass of A
2. Convert moles of A to moles of B using the mole ratio from the balanced equation
3. Convert moles of B to grams using molar mass of B

**Example:** How many grams of water are produced when 36 grams of methane (CH₄) undergoes complete combustion?

Balanced equation: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Step 1: Moles of CH₄
36 g ÷ 16.05 g/mol = 2.24 mol CH₄

Step 2: Moles of H₂O (ratio 1:2 from equation)
2.24 mol CH₄ × (2 mol H₂O / 1 mol CH₄) = 4.48 mol H₂O

Step 3: Grams of H₂O
4.48 mol × 18.02 g/mol = **80.7 g H₂O**

## 4. Limiting Reactants

In most real reactions, reactants are not present in the exact stoichiometric ratio — one runs out first. The **limiting reactant** (limiting reagent) is the one that is completely consumed and determines how much product can form. The other reactant(s) are **in excess**.

**Finding the limiting reactant:**

Method 1 (compare mole ratios):
- Calculate moles of each reactant
- Divide by the coefficient from the balanced equation
- The reactant with the *smaller* result is limiting

Method 2 (calculate product from each):
- Calculate how much product each reactant would produce if fully used
- The reactant that produces *less* product is limiting

**Example:** 5.0 g of H₂ reacts with 5.0 g of N₂ to form NH₃. What is the limiting reactant?

Balanced equation: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Moles of H₂: 5.0 / 2.016 = 2.48 mol
Moles of N₂: 5.0 / 28.02 = 0.178 mol

Compare using ratio (coefficient: H₂ has 3, N₂ has 1):
- H₂: 2.48 / 3 = 0.827
- N₂: 0.178 / 1 = 0.178

N₂ gives the smaller value → **N₂ is the limiting reactant**

Maximum NH₃ produced:
0.178 mol N₂ × (2 mol NH₃ / 1 mol N₂) = 0.356 mol NH₃ = 0.356 × 17.03 = **6.06 g NH₃**

---

## 5. Percent Yield

Theoretical calculations assume perfect conditions. In practice, **side reactions**, incomplete reactions, and losses during separation mean you never get the theoretical maximum amount of product.

**Theoretical yield**: the calculated maximum amount of product from stoichiometry (assuming complete, ideal reaction)

**Actual yield**: the amount of product actually obtained in the laboratory

**Percent yield** = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%

If you calculated 6.06 g NH₃ (theoretical) but obtained 5.44 g (actual):
Percent yield = (5.44 / 6.06) × 100% = **89.8%**

A percent yield of 100% is virtually impossible. Yields above 90% are considered excellent; many industrial syntheses operate at much lower yields and use excess reactant to drive the reaction toward completion.

---

## 6. Solution Stoichiometry

When reactions occur in solution, **molarity (M)** — moles of solute per liter of solution — relates volume to moles:

**moles = molarity × volume (in liters)**
**M = mol / L**

This allows stoichiometry calculations from solution volumes and concentrations, which is essential for titrations and lab work.

**Example:** How many mL of 0.250 M HCl are needed to completely neutralize 25.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH?

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (1:1 mole ratio)

Moles of NaOH: 0.100 M × 0.0250 L = 0.00250 mol NaOH

Moles of HCl needed: 0.00250 mol (1:1 ratio)

Volume of HCl: 0.00250 mol ÷ 0.250 M = **0.0100 L = 10.0 mL**

---

## Summary

Stoichiometry uses the mole (6.022 × 10²³ particles) and molar mass to convert between grams and moles. Balanced equation coefficients give mole ratios that convert between moles of any reactant or product. The stoichiometry roadmap: grams → moles → moles → grams. The limiting reactant controls how much product forms; excess reactants are left over. Percent yield compares actual to theoretical product. In solution stoichiometry, molarity (M = mol/L) links volume to moles.
