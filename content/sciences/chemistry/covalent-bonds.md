Not all atoms transfer electrons to bond. When non-metals combine with each other, they *share* electron pairs — creating **covalent bonds** that hold molecules together. Covalent bonding explains the structure of water, DNA, proteins, and nearly all of the molecules in living systems.

---

## 1. How Covalent Bonds Form

Non-metal atoms have high electronegativity — they strongly attract electrons. When two non-metals approach each other, neither gives up electrons entirely. Instead, they share one or more pairs of electrons, with each shared pair constituting one **covalent bond**.

The shared electrons are attracted simultaneously to both nuclei, pulling the atoms together. Both atoms achieve a full outer shell (satisfying the octet rule) through sharing rather than transfer.

**Example: H₂ (hydrogen gas)**

Each hydrogen has 1 valence electron. By sharing, each effectively has 2 electrons — filling its first shell (which holds only 2). The result is a single covalent bond, represented as H–H or H₂.

**Example: O₂ (oxygen gas)**

Oxygen has 6 valence electrons; it needs 2 more for an octet. Each oxygen shares 2 electrons with the other, forming a **double bond** (two shared pairs): O=O.

**Example: N₂ (nitrogen gas)**

Nitrogen has 5 valence electrons; it needs 3 more. Each nitrogen shares 3 electrons with the other, forming a **triple bond**: N≡N. Nitrogen's triple bond is one of the strongest bonds in chemistry, which is why N₂ is so unreactive — and why breaking it requires enormous energy (as in the Haber process for making ammonia fertilizer).

## 2. Lewis Structures

A **Lewis structure** (electron dot structure) shows valence electrons as dots around atomic symbols and represents bonds as lines between atoms.

**Drawing Lewis structures:**
1. Count total valence electrons from all atoms
2. Arrange atoms (least electronegative atom is usually central)
3. Place single bonds between connected atoms
4. Fill outer atoms' octets with lone pairs
5. Use remaining electrons on the central atom
6. If the central atom lacks an octet, convert lone pairs on adjacent atoms to multiple bonds

**Example: Water (H₂O)**

O has 6 valence electrons; each H has 1. Total: 8. Oxygen forms 2 single bonds with hydrogen (satisfying H's shell of 2) and keeps 2 lone pairs. The central oxygen has 4 groups around it (2 bonds + 2 lone pairs).

## 3. Bond Properties

### Bond Order

- **Single bond**: 1 shared pair, written as a single line (—)
- **Double bond**: 2 shared pairs, written as two lines (=)
- **Triple bond**: 3 shared pairs, written as three lines (≡)

Higher bond order = stronger bond + shorter bond length + harder to break.

| Bond type | Bond length (approx.) | Bond energy (approx.) |
|-----------|----------------------|-----------------------|
| C–C single | 154 pm | 347 kJ/mol |
| C=C double | 134 pm | 614 kJ/mol |
| C≡C triple | 120 pm | 839 kJ/mol |

### Polarity

Electronegativity is an atom's tendency to attract shared electrons. If two bonded atoms have different electronegativities, the shared electrons spend more time near the more electronegative atom — creating a **polar covalent bond** with partial charges.

- Δelectronegativity < 0.4: nonpolar covalent (electrons shared equally)
- Δelectronegativity 0.4–1.7: polar covalent (unequal sharing)
- Δelectronegativity > 1.7: ionic (electron transfer, roughly)

In H₂O: oxygen (electronegativity 3.44) is much more electronegative than hydrogen (2.20). The O–H bonds are strongly polar, with partial negative charge on oxygen (δ−) and partial positive on hydrogen (δ+).

## 4. Molecular Geometry: VSEPR Theory

The shape of a molecule is determined by minimizing repulsion between all electron groups (bonding pairs and lone pairs) around the central atom — the **VSEPR model** (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion).

Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, which compresses bond angles.

| Electron groups | Lone pairs | Geometry | Bond angle | Example |
|-----------------|-----------|----------|-----------|---------|
| 2 | 0 | Linear | 180° | CO₂ |
| 3 | 0 | Trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃ |
| 3 | 1 | Bent | ~117° | SO₂ |
| 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄ |
| 4 | 1 | Trigonal pyramidal | ~107° | NH₃ |
| 4 | 2 | Bent | ~104.5° | H₂O |
| 5 | 0 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90°/120° | PCl₅ |
| 6 | 0 | Octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |

---

## 5. Molecular Polarity

A molecule is **polar** if it has polar bonds *and* its geometry does not cause the bond dipoles to cancel.

- CO₂: two polar C=O bonds, but linear geometry makes dipoles point in exactly opposite directions — they cancel. **Nonpolar molecule.**
- H₂O: two polar O–H bonds, bent geometry (104.5°) — dipoles do not cancel. **Polar molecule.** This is why water has such unusual properties (high boiling point, surface tension, ability to dissolve many substances).

**Like dissolves like**: polar solvents (water) dissolve polar and ionic compounds; nonpolar solvents (hexane) dissolve nonpolar compounds.

---

## 6. Intermolecular Forces

Covalent molecules are held together by **intramolecular** covalent bonds. Between molecules, weaker **intermolecular forces** operate:

- **London dispersion forces (LDF)**: temporary dipoles induced by electron fluctuations; present in all molecules; strength increases with molecular size and surface area
- **Dipole-dipole forces**: attraction between permanent dipoles in polar molecules
- **Hydrogen bonding**: especially strong dipole-dipole force when H is bonded to N, O, or F — highly electronegative atoms. Explains water's high boiling point, surface tension, and the base pairing in DNA.

---

## Summary

Covalent bonds form when non-metals share electron pairs, with each shared pair constituting one bond. Single, double, and triple bonds differ in strength and length. Lewis structures show bonding and lone pairs. Bond polarity depends on electronegativity differences. Molecular geometry is predicted by VSEPR theory based on minimizing electron pair repulsion. Molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and geometry. Intermolecular forces — especially hydrogen bonding — determine physical properties like boiling point and solubility.
