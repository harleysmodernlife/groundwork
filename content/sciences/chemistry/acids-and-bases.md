Acids and bases are among the most practically important concepts in chemistry. They govern digestion, blood pH, industrial processes, environmental chemistry, and the behavior of nearly every biological molecule. From the vinegar in salad dressing to the antacids that neutralize stomach acid, acid-base chemistry is everywhere.

---

## 1. Definitions of Acids and Bases

### Arrhenius Definition (1887)

The original, narrowest definition:
- **Acid**: produces H⁺ ions when dissolved in water. (HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻)
- **Base**: produces OH⁻ ions when dissolved in water. (NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻)

Limitation: only applies to aqueous solutions. Doesn't account for substances like ammonia (NH₃) that behave as bases without containing OH⁻.

### Brønsted-Lowry Definition (1923)

More general:
- **Acid**: a proton (H⁺) donor
- **Base**: a proton (H⁺) acceptor

When an acid donates a proton, it becomes its **conjugate base**. When a base accepts a proton, it becomes its **conjugate acid**. Every acid-base reaction involves conjugate acid-base pairs.

Example: HCl + H₂O → Cl⁻ + H₃O⁺
- HCl is the acid (donates H⁺); Cl⁻ is its conjugate base
- H₂O is the base (accepts H⁺); H₃O⁺ (hydronium) is its conjugate acid

### Lewis Definition (broadest)

- **Acid**: an electron pair acceptor
- **Base**: an electron pair donor

This extends acid-base chemistry to reactions not involving proton transfer at all. Useful in organic chemistry and coordination chemistry.

## 2. The pH Scale

**pH** measures the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺, or more precisely hydronium ions H₃O⁺) in solution:

**pH = −log[H⁺]**

In pure water at 25°C: [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷ mol/L → pH = 7 (neutral)

- **pH < 7**: acidic ([H⁺] > [OH⁻])
- **pH = 7**: neutral ([H⁺] = [OH⁻])
- **pH > 7**: basic/alkaline ([H⁺] < [OH⁻])

The pH scale is **logarithmic**: each unit represents a 10-fold change in [H⁺]. pH 4 is 10 times more acidic than pH 5, and 100 times more acidic than pH 6.

| Substance | Approximate pH |
|-----------|---------------|
| Battery acid | 0–1 |
| Stomach acid | 1.5–3.5 |
| Vinegar | 2.5–3 |
| Orange juice | 3.5 |
| Coffee | 5 |
| Pure water | 7 |
| Blood | 7.35–7.45 |
| Baking soda | 8.3 |
| Milk of magnesia | 10 |
| Ammonia | 11 |
| Bleach | 12–13 |
| Drain cleaner | 13–14 |

**pOH** = −log[OH⁻]; at 25°C, **pH + pOH = 14**

## 3. Strong vs. Weak Acids and Bases

### Strong Acids

Dissociate completely in water — essentially 100% ionized:
- HCl (hydrochloric acid)
- HBr, HI
- H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid) — first proton
- HNO₃ (nitric acid)
- HClO₄ (perchloric acid)

For a 0.1 M HCl solution: [H⁺] = 0.1 M; pH = 1.

### Weak Acids

Dissociate only partially — exist in equilibrium with their ions. The **acid dissociation constant (Ka)** quantifies this:

HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻

Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]

Larger Ka = stronger acid. Acetic acid (Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) is much weaker than HCl.

A 0.1 M acetic acid solution has pH ≈ 2.87 (not 1), because most molecules remain undissociated.

### Strong Bases

Dissociate completely: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂.

### Weak Bases

Partially accept protons: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

The **base dissociation constant (Kb)** describes this equilibrium. Ka × Kb = Kw = 10⁻¹⁴ (at 25°C) for a conjugate acid-base pair.

## 4. Neutralization Reactions

When an acid and base react, they neutralize each other, typically forming water and a salt:

**Acid + Base → Salt + Water**

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O

The net ionic equation for strong acid + strong base neutralization:

H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O

**Titration**: a procedure in which a solution of known concentration (the **titrant**) is slowly added to an unknown acid or base solution until the neutralization is complete (the **equivalence point**). An indicator (a substance that changes color at a specific pH) or a pH meter marks the endpoint. Titration is used to determine unknown concentrations precisely.

---

## 5. Buffers

A **buffer** resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. It consists of a **weak acid and its conjugate base** (or weak base and conjugate acid) in similar concentrations.

**How it works:**
- When acid (H⁺) is added, the conjugate base reacts with it: A⁻ + H⁺ → HA (removes the added H⁺)
- When base (OH⁻) is added, the weak acid reacts with it: HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O (removes the added OH⁻)

**Human blood** is buffered at pH 7.35–7.45 primarily by the bicarbonate buffer system (H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻). A blood pH below 7.35 is **acidosis**; above 7.45 is **alkalosis** — both are medically serious.

---

## 6. Acid Rain

Industrial emissions of SO₂ and NOₓ react with water vapor in the atmosphere to form sulfuric and nitric acids, which fall as **acid rain** (pH 4–5, sometimes below 3). Effects: acidification of lakes (killing aquatic life), leaching of soil nutrients, damage to forests, and corrosion of stone and metal structures.

The problem has been substantially reduced in developed countries through emissions controls since the 1970s–90s.

---

## Summary

Acids donate protons (Brønsted-Lowry) or accept electron pairs (Lewis); bases accept protons or donate electron pairs. pH is the negative log of H⁺ concentration — below 7 is acidic, above 7 is basic. Strong acids and bases dissociate completely; weak ones establish equilibria described by Ka and Kb. Neutralization combines acid and base to form salt and water. Buffers maintain pH by using weak acid/conjugate base pairs to absorb added H⁺ or OH⁻. Blood pH is tightly buffered at 7.35–7.45.
