When a metal meets a non-metal, electrons transfer from one atom to the other — and the resulting charged particles are held together by powerful electrostatic attraction. This is **ionic bonding**, the force that holds table salt together, gives minerals their hardness, and makes many important compounds possible.

---

## 1. How Ionic Bonds Form

Atoms are most stable when their outermost shell is full — typically 8 electrons (the **octet rule**). Metals in Groups 1 and 2 have 1–2 valence electrons and reach a full outer shell most easily by *losing* those electrons. Non-metals in Groups 16–17 have 6–7 valence electrons and reach a full outer shell most easily by *gaining* 1–2 electrons.

When a metal and non-metal interact, the metal transfers its valence electrons to the non-metal. The result:
- The metal atom becomes a positively charged **cation** (fewer electrons than protons)
- The non-metal atom becomes a negatively charged **anion** (more electrons than protons)
- Opposite charges attract: the cation and anion are held together by **electrostatic force** — an ionic bond

**Example: Formation of NaCl (table salt)**

Sodium (Na): [Ne] 3s¹ — 1 valence electron
Chlorine (Cl): [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵ — 7 valence electrons

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ (sodium loses 1 electron; becomes isoelectronic with neon)
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (chlorine gains 1 electron; becomes isoelectronic with argon)

Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract each other → NaCl (sodium chloride)

## 2. Predicting Ion Charges

The charge of an ion can be predicted from its group:

| Group | Valence e⁻ | Typical Ion |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| 1 (alkali metals) | 1 | +1 (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺) |
| 2 (alkaline earth metals) | 2 | +2 (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺) |
| 13 | 3 | +3 (Al³⁺) |
| 15 | 5 | −3 (N³⁻, P³⁻) |
| 16 | 6 | −2 (O²⁻, S²⁻) |
| 17 (halogens) | 7 | −1 (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) |

Transition metals can form multiple ion charges (e.g., Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺) — designated with Roman numerals: iron(II) = Fe²⁺, iron(III) = Fe³⁺.

---

## 3. Polyatomic Ions

Many ionic compounds contain **polyatomic ions** — groups of atoms covalently bonded to each other but carrying an overall charge:

- **Sulfate**: SO₄²⁻
- **Nitrate**: NO₃⁻
- **Carbonate**: CO₃²⁻
- **Phosphate**: PO₄³⁻
- **Hydroxide**: OH⁻
- **Ammonium**: NH₄⁺

These ions act as a single unit in chemical formulas. Ammonium sulfate is (NH₄)₂SO₄ — two ammonium ions for every one sulfate ion.

---

## 4. Writing Chemical Formulas for Ionic Compounds

In ionic compounds, the overall charge must be zero. The formula is determined by balancing the charges of the cation and anion:

**Method (criss-cross rule)**:
- Take the magnitude of each ion's charge and use it as the subscript for the other ion, then simplify if possible

- Calcium chloride: Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ → CaCl₂ (1 calcium, 2 chloride, total charge = +2 − 2 = 0)
- Aluminum oxide: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ → Al₂O₃ (2 aluminum × +3 = +6; 3 oxygen × −2 = −6; balanced)
- Magnesium nitrate: Mg²⁺ and NO₃⁻ → Mg(NO₃)₂ (parentheses used for polyatomic ion with subscript > 1)

---

## 5. Crystal Lattice Structure

Ionic compounds don't exist as individual pairs of ions — they form **crystal lattices**: three-dimensional arrays in which each cation is surrounded by anions and each anion is surrounded by cations.

In NaCl, each Na⁺ is surrounded by 6 Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ by 6 Na⁺ ions. The lattice extends in all directions. The **lattice energy** — the energy holding the lattice together — is very high, which is why ionic compounds tend to be:
- **Solid at room temperature** (strong lattice forces)
- **High melting and boiling points** (requires much energy to break the lattice)
- **Hard but brittle** (rigid lattice; when stressed, like-charged planes meet and repel, causing cleavage along crystal planes)

---

## 6. Properties of Ionic Compounds

| Property | Explanation |
|----------|-------------|
| High melting/boiling point | Strong electrostatic forces in the lattice |
| Hard, brittle crystals | Rigid lattice structure; brittle along cleavage planes |
| Conduct electricity when melted or dissolved | Free ions can move and carry charge; solid lattice locks ions in place |
| Soluble in water (usually) | Polar water molecules surround and separate ions (**solvation**) |
| Do not conduct electricity as solids | Ions are fixed in the lattice |

**Electrolytes**: ionic compounds that conduct electricity when dissolved in water are **electrolytes** — the ions in solution carry the current. Sports drinks contain electrolytes (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) because these ions are essential to nerve and muscle function.

---

## 7. Naming Ionic Compounds

**Binary ionic compounds** (one metal, one non-metal):
- Name the cation first (metal name unchanged)
- Name the anion second: change the non-metal's name to end in -ide
- Example: NaCl = sodium chloride; MgO = magnesium oxide; CaBr₂ = calcium bromide

**Compounds with transition metal cations** (which can have multiple charges):
- Include a Roman numeral in parentheses to indicate the charge
- FeCl₂ = iron(II) chloride; FeCl₃ = iron(III) chloride

**Compounds with polyatomic ions**:
- Use the polyatomic ion's name as-is
- Ca(NO₃)₂ = calcium nitrate; K₂SO₄ = potassium sulfate

---

## Summary

Ionic bonds form when metals transfer electrons to non-metals, creating oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction. Ion charges are predictable from group number; transition metals have variable charges. Ionic compounds form crystalline lattice structures with high melting points, hardness, and brittleness. They conduct electricity only when dissolved or melted, as the lattice must break for ions to move freely. Formulas are written by balancing charges to achieve electrical neutrality.
