A chemical equation is a symbolic representation of a chemical reaction. Balancing it — ensuring the same number of each type of atom appears on both sides — is not a mathematical trick. It is a direct expression of the Law of Conservation of Mass: atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

---

## 1. Why Equations Must Be Balanced

In any chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed. The total mass of reactants must equal the total mass of products. An unbalanced equation violates this law and doesn't accurately represent the reaction.

**Unbalanced:** H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Count atoms: Left: 2H, 2O. Right: 2H, 1O. Oxygen is not balanced — this equation implies an oxygen atom disappears, which is impossible.

**Balanced:** 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Count atoms: Left: 4H, 2O. Right: 4H, 2O. ✓

---

## 2. Reading Chemical Equations

**Symbols used:**
- **→** : "yields" or "produces"
- **⇌** : reversible reaction (equilibrium)
- **(s)** : solid
- **(l)** : liquid
- **(g)** : gas
- **(aq)** : aqueous (dissolved in water)
- **Δ** (above arrow): heat applied
- **Numbers in front of formulas** = coefficients (number of formula units; these change)
- **Subscript numbers** = atoms within a molecule (these do NOT change; changing subscripts changes the substance)

The coefficient applies to all atoms in the formula that follows it.

---

## 3. The Balancing Process

**Rules:**
1. Never change subscripts (that would change the identity of the compound)
2. Change only **coefficients** (numbers in front of formulas)
3. A coefficient of 1 is implied and not written
4. The balanced equation should have the simplest whole-number coefficients possible (reduce if all coefficients have a common factor)

### Step-by-Step Method

**Example: Balance the combustion of propane**

Step 1: Write the unbalanced equation.
C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Step 2: Count atoms on each side.
Left: 3C, 8H, 2O
Right: 1C, 2H, 3O

Step 3: Balance the most complex molecule first (the one with the most elements). Start with carbon.

3 carbons on left → put coefficient 3 in front of CO₂:
C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3CO₂ + H₂O
Left: 3C, 8H, 2O
Right: 3C, 2H, 7O

Step 4: Balance hydrogen. 8H on left → need 4 H₂O:
C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O
Left: 3C, 8H, 2O
Right: 3C, 8H, 10O ✓ C and H balanced.

Step 5: Balance oxygen. 10O on right; 2O in O₂ → need 5 O₂:
C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Step 6: Verify all atoms.
Left: 3C, 8H, 10O
Right: 3C, 8H, 10O ✓

Step 7: Check that coefficients are in the simplest whole-number ratio. 1:5:3:4 — no common factor. Done.

---

## 4. Balancing Tips and Strategies

### Save the Simplest for Last

When a diatomic element (H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂, etc.) appears by itself as a reactant or product, balance it last — it's easy to adjust.

### Polyatomic Ions

If a polyatomic ion (such as NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, PO₄³⁻) appears unchanged on both sides of the equation, treat it as a unit when counting rather than counting individual atoms.

Ca(OH)₂ + H₃PO₄ → Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + H₂O

Count PO₄ groups: 1 on left, 2 on right → put 2 in front of H₃PO₄.
Then balance Ca: 3 Ca on right → put 3 in front of Ca(OH)₂.
Then balance H and O.

### Fractional Coefficients (Then Multiply)

Sometimes it's easier to use a fractional coefficient temporarily, then multiply through to get whole numbers.

C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Balance C: 2CO₂. Balance H: 3H₂O.
O on right: 4 + 3 = 7. Need 7/2 O₂.

C₂H₆ + 7/2 O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

Multiply all by 2: **2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O** ✓

## 5. Types of Balancing Problems

### Combination and Decomposition

Relatively straightforward — usually one or two elements to balance.

### Combustion

Organic compound + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (if complete). Standard approach: balance C, then H, then O last.

### Redox by Inspection

For simple cases, trial-and-error balancing works. For complex redox reactions (especially in acidic or basic solution), the **half-reaction method** is required.

---

## 6. Half-Reaction Method for Redox Equations

For complex reactions involving electron transfer, balance oxidation and reduction separately:

1. Separate the reaction into two **half-reactions** (one oxidation, one reduction)
2. Balance atoms in each half-reaction (other than O and H first, then O by adding H₂O, then H by adding H⁺)
3. Balance charge in each half-reaction by adding electrons (e⁻)
4. Multiply half-reactions by factors to make electrons equal in both
5. Add the half-reactions; cancel electrons and anything that appears on both sides

Example: MnO₄⁻ + Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + Fe³⁺ (in acid)

Oxidation half: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻
Reduction half: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O

Multiply oxidation by 5: 5Fe²⁺ → 5Fe³⁺ + 5e⁻

Add: MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O ✓

---

## 7. Stoichiometry Preview

A balanced equation is the key to **stoichiometry** — using the mole ratios from coefficients to calculate how much product forms from given amounts of reactants, or how much reactant is needed to produce a desired amount of product.

In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O:
The coefficients 2:1:2 are mole ratios. If you start with 4 moles of H₂, you need 2 moles of O₂ to fully react, and you'll produce 4 moles of H₂O.

---

## Summary

Balancing chemical equations expresses the Law of Conservation of Mass: atoms are rearranged but never created or destroyed. Balancing is done by adjusting coefficients only — never subscripts. A systematic approach: balance the most complex species first, save diatomic elements for last, verify all atoms on both sides, and simplify to the lowest whole-number ratio. For complex redox reactions, the half-reaction method separates oxidation and reduction and balances each individually before combining.
