Every rock on Earth has a story written in its composition and texture. Understanding rocks and minerals — what they're made of, how they form, and how they change over time — is the foundation of geology and essential to understanding Earth's history.

---

## 1. Minerals

A **mineral** is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and a crystalline atomic structure. There are over 4,000 known minerals, but fewer than 20 make up the vast majority of Earth's crust.

### Mineral Properties

Minerals are identified by their physical properties:

- **Hardness**: resistance to scratching, measured on the **Mohs scale** (1 = talc, 10 = diamond). A mineral can scratch anything softer than itself.
- **Luster**: how light reflects off the surface (metallic, glassy/vitreous, pearly, silky)
- **Color**: useful but unreliable — the same mineral can appear in many colors due to impurities
- **Streak**: the color of the mineral's powder (more consistent than surface color)
- **Cleavage**: tendency to break along flat planes determined by atomic structure
- **Fracture**: irregular breaking pattern when cleavage is absent
- **Crystal form**: the geometric shape crystals grow into under ideal conditions
- **Specific gravity**: density relative to water

### Common Rock-Forming Minerals

- **Quartz** (SiO₂): very hard, glassy; found in granite, sandstone, and sand
- **Feldspar**: most abundant mineral group in the crust; found in granite and many igneous rocks
- **Mica**: perfect sheet cleavage; found in granite, schist
- **Calcite** (CaCO₃): fizzes in acid; makes up limestone and marble
- **Olivine**: dense, green; common in the mantle and basalt

## 2. The Three Rock Types

Rocks are classified by how they form.

### Igneous Rocks

**Igneous rocks** form from the cooling and solidification of magma or lava.

- **Intrusive (plutonic)**: magma cools slowly underground, allowing large crystals to form. Example: **granite** — visible crystals of quartz, feldspar, and mica.
- **Extrusive (volcanic)**: lava cools quickly at the surface, forming small or no visible crystals. Example: **basalt** — fine-grained, dark. Obsidian (volcanic glass) forms when cooling is nearly instantaneous.

Crystal size is the key indicator: slow cooling = large crystals; fast cooling = small or no crystals.

### Sedimentary Rocks

**Sedimentary rocks** form from accumulated particles (sediment) that are compressed and cemented together over time (**lithification**).

- **Clastic**: made of fragments of other rocks. **Sandstone** (sand), **shale** (clay), **conglomerate** (pebbles and gravel).
- **Chemical**: precipitated from dissolved minerals in water. **Limestone** (often from calcite), **rock salt**, **gypsum**.
- **Organic**: formed from accumulated biological material. **Coal** (compressed plant matter), some **limestone** (from shells and coral).

Sedimentary rocks often contain **fossils** and are deposited in horizontal layers called **strata**. Older layers lie beneath newer ones (the **law of superposition**).

### Metamorphic Rocks

**Metamorphic rocks** form when existing rocks are transformed by intense heat, pressure, or chemically active fluids — but without fully melting. The mineral composition and crystal structure change.

- **Foliated**: minerals are aligned in bands or layers under directed pressure. **Slate** (from shale) → **phyllite** → **schist** → **gneiss** (increasingly metamorphosed).
- **Non-foliated**: pressure from all directions; minerals grow but don't align. **Marble** (from limestone), **quartzite** (from sandstone).

## 3. The Rock Cycle

Rocks are not permanent. The **rock cycle** describes how any rock type can be transformed into any other through geological processes:

- Igneous rock is **weathered and eroded** into sediment → compacted into **sedimentary rock**
- Any rock buried deeply enough is **metamorphosed** by heat and pressure
- Any rock heated enough **melts** into magma, which cools into **igneous rock**
- Uplift brings deep rocks back to the surface, where the cycle continues

This cycle operates over millions to billions of years, driven by Earth's internal heat and the energy of the sun (which drives erosion through weather and water).

---

## Summary

Minerals are the building blocks of rocks — defined by composition and crystalline structure. Igneous rocks crystallize from magma; sedimentary rocks form from compressed sediment; metamorphic rocks result from heat and pressure transforming existing rocks. The rock cycle continuously transforms rocks from one type into another over geologic time.
