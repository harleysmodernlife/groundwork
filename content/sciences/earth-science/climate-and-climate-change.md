**Weather** is what happens outside today. **Climate** is what you expect the weather to be like based on decades of data. While weather is chaotic and short-term, climate is the long-term pattern of temperature, precipitation, and other conditions in a region — and it is changing at a rate unprecedented in recorded human history.

---

## 1. What Determines Climate

Several factors shape regional climates:

- **Latitude**: regions near the equator receive more direct sunlight year-round; polar regions receive less and experience dramatic seasonal variation.
- **Altitude**: temperature drops with elevation at about 6.5°C per kilometer.
- **Proximity to large bodies of water**: oceans moderate temperature — coastal areas have milder winters and cooler summers than inland areas at the same latitude. Water heats and cools slowly.
- **Ocean currents**: warm currents (like the Gulf Stream) carry tropical heat toward higher latitudes, warming coastal areas. Cold currents cool adjacent coastlines.
- **Prevailing winds**: wind patterns determine whether a region receives moist maritime air or dry continental air.
- **Topography**: mountain ranges force air upward (cooling and precipitating) on the windward side and create rain shadows on the leeward side.

---

## 2. Climate Zones

Earth's surface is broadly divided into climate zones:

- **Tropical**: hot year-round, high precipitation, near the equator
- **Arid/Desert**: very low precipitation, either hot (Sahara) or cold (Gobi)
- **Temperate**: moderate temperatures with distinct seasons; most of Europe, the U.S., and China
- **Continental**: large seasonal temperature swings, away from ocean moderating effects
- **Polar**: extreme cold year-round; tundra (permafrost but brief warm season) and ice cap
- **Mediterranean**: warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters

## 3. Natural Climate Variability

Earth's climate has never been static. Natural factors drive long-term climate cycles:

- **Milankovitch cycles**: periodic changes in Earth's orbital shape (eccentricity), axial tilt, and wobble (precession) alter how much solar energy reaches Earth and when. These cycles, operating over tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of years, are the primary driver of ice ages and interglacial periods.
- **Volcanic eruptions**: large eruptions inject sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, forming reflective aerosols that temporarily cool the planet. The 1991 Pinatubo eruption reduced global temperatures by about 0.5°C for 1–2 years.
- **Solar variability**: slight changes in the sun's energy output over 11-year cycles and longer periods affect climate, though the effect is small compared to greenhouse forcing.
- **Ocean circulation**: changes in deep ocean circulation patterns, such as the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), redistribute heat globally and can trigger abrupt regional climate shifts.

## 4. Human-Caused Climate Change

Since the Industrial Revolution (~1750), human activities have added large quantities of greenhouse gases to the atmosphere — primarily through burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas), deforestation, agriculture, and industrial processes.

**Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** has increased from about 280 ppm pre-industrial to over 420 ppm today — higher than at any point in at least 800,000 years, as measured from Antarctic ice cores. **Methane (CH₄)** has more than doubled. Both are potent greenhouse gases.

The result is the **enhanced greenhouse effect**: more infrared radiation is trapped, warming the surface. The global average temperature has risen approximately 1.2°C above pre-industrial levels and is rising faster than at any point in the geological record associated with human civilization.

### Observed Effects

- Rising global average temperatures, with recent decades each being the hottest on record
- Melting ice sheets (Greenland, Antarctica) and glaciers worldwide
- Rising sea levels (from both thermal expansion of water and melting ice)
- Ocean acidification from absorbed CO₂ (CO₂ + H₂O → carbonic acid)
- More intense heat waves, droughts, wildfires, and heavy precipitation events
- Shifts in species ranges and timing of seasonal events (phenology)
- Coral bleaching from warmer, more acidic oceans

### Scientific Consensus

The conclusion that human activities are the dominant cause of observed warming since the mid-20th century represents the consensus of virtually every major scientific organization and over 97% of actively publishing climate scientists. This is among the most thoroughly examined questions in the history of science.

---

## 5. Climate Projections and Pathways

Future warming depends on future emissions. The **Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)** projects scenarios ranging from limiting warming to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels (requiring rapid, deep cuts in emissions) to 4°C or more by 2100 under high-emissions trajectories.

The 1.5°C and 2°C thresholds from the **Paris Agreement** represent levels beyond which risks of severe and irreversible impacts rise sharply — including loss of most tropical coral reefs, accelerated sea level rise threatening coastal cities, and worsening food and water insecurity.

---

## Summary

Climate is the long-term pattern of weather in a region, shaped by latitude, proximity to water, terrain, and ocean currents. Earth's climate has always varied naturally due to orbital cycles, volcanic activity, and solar changes. Human greenhouse gas emissions have now become the dominant driver of climate change, causing warming, ice loss, sea level rise, and shifts in weather extremes at a pace faster than natural cycles can explain.
