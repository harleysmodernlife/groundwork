Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a specific place and time — temperature, humidity, precipitation, wind, and cloud cover. These conditions are not random; they are driven by predictable physical processes involving the unequal heating of Earth's surface and the circulation of air and moisture through the atmosphere.

---

## 1. What Drives Weather

The engine of all weather is the **unequal heating of Earth's surface** by the sun. The equator receives more direct solar energy than the poles. This temperature difference creates pressure gradients that drive the global circulation of air.

Warm air is less dense than cool air. It rises, creating **low-pressure zones**. As it rises and cools, moisture condenses and precipitation often forms. Cool, dense air sinks, creating **high-pressure zones**. Air moves horizontally from high pressure to low pressure — this movement is **wind**.

The **Coriolis effect** — caused by Earth's rotation — deflects moving air to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. This deflection creates the curved wind patterns and rotating storm systems we observe.

---

## 2. Air Masses

An **air mass** is a large body of air with relatively uniform temperature and humidity throughout. Air masses form when air stagnates over a region long enough to take on that region's characteristics.

Classification:
- **Continental (c)**: dry, formed over land
- **Maritime (m)**: moist, formed over ocean
- **Polar (P)**: cold, formed at high latitudes
- **Tropical (T)**: warm, formed at low latitudes

Examples: **cP** (continental polar) — cold and dry, the air mass that brings Arctic blasts to the central U.S. **mT** (maritime tropical) — warm and humid, the source of Gulf moisture that fuels thunderstorms.

## 3. Fronts

A **front** is the boundary between two air masses with different temperatures and densities. Most significant weather occurs at fronts.

### Cold Front

A cold, dense air mass advances and slides under a warmer air mass, forcing it rapidly upward. This produces a narrow band of intense precipitation — often thunderstorms — followed by clearing skies and cooler temperatures. Cold fronts move faster than warm fronts and are represented on weather maps by a blue line with triangles.

### Warm Front

A warm air mass advances and rides up gently over a retreating cold air mass. The gradual lifting produces widespread, steady precipitation over a large area — typically rain or snow lasting many hours. Warm fronts are represented by a red line with semicircles.

### Stationary Front

Two air masses meet but neither advances. Prolonged cloudy weather and precipitation can persist for days. Represented by alternating cold- and warm-front symbols.

### Occluded Front

A fast-moving cold front catches up with and overtakes a warm front, lifting the warm air off the surface. Complex precipitation patterns result.

## 4. Storm Systems

### Thunderstorms

Thunderstorms form when warm, moist air rises rapidly. As water vapor condenses, it releases heat (**latent heat**), which accelerates the updraft further. The towering **cumulonimbus** cloud can extend 15 km into the atmosphere. Lightning results from the separation of electrical charge within the cloud; thunder is the shockwave from the rapid expansion of heated air along the lightning channel.

Severe thunderstorms can produce **tornadoes** — violently rotating columns of air extending from a thunderstorm to the ground, formed when horizontal wind shear (winds at different altitudes moving in different directions at different speeds) is tilted to vertical by a powerful updraft.

### Hurricanes (Tropical Cyclones)

Hurricanes are large, organized storm systems that form over warm tropical ocean water (above ~26°C). Warm, moist air rises, creating a deep low-pressure center. The Coriolis effect causes the inflow to rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere. They weaken over land (cut off from warm ocean moisture) and at higher latitudes.

### Mid-Latitude Cyclones

The large low-pressure systems that produce most of the week-to-week weather in temperate regions. They develop along fronts, grow as warm and cold air circulate around the low-pressure center, and produce days of cloud and precipitation.

---

## 5. Reading Weather Maps

Weather maps show:
- **Isobars**: lines connecting points of equal pressure. Closely spaced isobars = strong pressure gradient = strong winds.
- **H and L symbols**: centers of high and low pressure
- **Front symbols**: cold, warm, stationary, occluded
- **Station models**: circles showing observed conditions (temperature, dew point, wind, pressure, cloud cover) at specific locations

---

## Summary

Weather is driven by unequal solar heating, which creates pressure differences that move air. Air masses bring regional temperature and humidity characteristics; fronts mark their boundaries and generate most significant weather events. Thunderstorms, tornadoes, and hurricanes result from specific thermodynamic conditions. Understanding these systems allows for weather forecasting and explains the patterns we experience day to day.
