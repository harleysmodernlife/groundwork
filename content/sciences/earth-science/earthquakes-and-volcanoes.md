Earthquakes and volcanoes are among the most powerful forces on Earth's surface — and they are not random. Both are direct expressions of plate tectonics, concentrated where plates meet, separate, or grind past one another.

---

## 1. Earthquakes

### What Causes an Earthquake

The Earth's crust is under constant stress from plate motion. Rock doesn't flow — it deforms elastically under stress until it reaches its breaking point, then suddenly ruptures. This sudden release of stored elastic energy is an **earthquake**.

The point underground where the rupture begins is the **focus** (or hypocenter). The point on the surface directly above the focus is the **epicenter**. Energy radiates outward from the focus as **seismic waves**.

### Types of Seismic Waves

- **P-waves** (primary): compressional waves that travel through solids, liquids, and gases. Fastest; arrive first.
- **S-waves** (secondary): shear waves that travel only through solids. Arrive second.
- **Surface waves**: travel along the Earth's surface. Slowest but most destructive — responsible for most building damage.

The difference in arrival time between P-waves and S-waves at a seismograph station reveals how far away the earthquake occurred. Data from three or more stations allows triangulation of the epicenter's location.

### Measuring Earthquakes

The **Richter scale** (more precisely, the **moment magnitude scale**, or Mw) measures the energy released. Each whole-number step represents roughly 32 times more energy. A magnitude 7.0 releases about 1,000 times more energy than a magnitude 5.0.

The **Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale** measures observed shaking and damage at a specific location, ranging from I (imperceptible) to XII (total destruction).

### Fault Types

Earthquakes occur along **faults** — fractures in the crust where movement has occurred:
- **Normal faults**: crust is pulled apart; one side drops relative to the other (divergent settings)
- **Reverse/thrust faults**: crust is compressed; one side is pushed up over the other (convergent settings)
- **Strike-slip faults**: blocks slide horizontally past each other (transform settings; e.g., San Andreas Fault)

## 2. Volcanoes

### What Causes Volcanic Eruptions

**Magma** — molten rock beneath the surface — is less dense than surrounding solid rock. It rises through the crust along pathways of weakness. When it reaches the surface, it is called **lava**.

Eruptions are driven by the buildup of dissolved gases in magma (water vapor, CO₂, sulfur dioxide). As magma rises and pressure decreases, gases come out of solution — like CO₂ escaping from a shaken soda bottle. The violence of the eruption depends on the magma's **viscosity** (thickness) and gas content.

- **Low-viscosity magma** (basaltic): gas escapes easily; produces relatively gentle, effusive eruptions with lava flows. Example: Hawaiian volcanoes.
- **High-viscosity magma** (rhyolitic): gas is trapped; pressure builds until explosive eruption. Example: Mount St. Helens, Krakatoa.

### Types of Volcanoes

- **Shield volcanoes**: broad, gently sloping; built from many thin lava flows. Example: Mauna Loa, Hawaii.
- **Stratovolcanoes (composite volcanoes)**: steep-sided, layered with lava and pyroclastic material; most explosive. Example: Mount Rainier, Fuji.
- **Cinder cones**: small, steep cones built from pyroclastic fragments; typically short-lived eruptions.
- **Calderas**: large depressions formed when a volcano collapses after emptying its magma chamber. Example: Yellowstone caldera.

### Where Volcanoes Form

Most volcanoes occur at plate boundaries:
- **Subduction zones**: as oceanic crust subducts and melts, magma rises through the overriding plate. This creates the **Ring of Fire** — the chain of volcanoes encircling the Pacific Ocean.
- **Divergent boundaries**: magma wells up at mid-ocean ridges (mostly underwater) and rift zones.
- **Hot spots**: plumes of exceptionally hot mantle material that burn through the overlying plate regardless of boundary location. Example: the Hawaiian Islands, Yellowstone.

---

## 3. Hazards and Monitoring

Volcanic hazards include lava flows, pyroclastic flows (fast-moving clouds of hot gas and rock), ash fall, lahars (volcanic mudflows), and volcanic gases. Earthquakes can trigger tsunamis when seafloor displacement displaces large volumes of water.

Scientists monitor volcanoes using seismographs (earthquake swarms often precede eruptions), GPS (ground deformation), gas sensors, and thermal cameras. Earthquake prediction remains one of science's unsolved problems; short-term forecasting is not yet reliable.

---

## Summary

Earthquakes occur when stressed rock suddenly ruptures along a fault, releasing seismic waves. Volcanoes form where magma reaches the surface — primarily at subduction zones, divergent boundaries, and hot spots. Both are concentrated at plate boundaries and are expressions of the same tectonic forces reshaping the planet's surface.
