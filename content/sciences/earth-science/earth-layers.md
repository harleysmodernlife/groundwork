The Earth beneath your feet is not uniform rock all the way down. It is layered like an onion, each layer with its own composition, temperature, and physical state — and understanding those layers explains everything from why volcanoes erupt to why the continents move.

---

## 1. How We Know What's Inside the Earth

No drill or probe has ever reached the Earth's core. The deepest borehole ever drilled — the Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia — reached only 12 km, barely scratching the surface of a planet 6,371 km in radius.

What we know about Earth's interior comes from **seismic waves** — vibrations produced by earthquakes that travel through the planet. Different materials transmit these waves at different speeds, and some wave types can't travel through liquid at all. By analyzing how seismic waves change as they move through the Earth, scientists have mapped the planet's internal structure with surprising precision.

---

## 2. The Four Main Layers

### The Crust

The **crust** is the outermost layer — the thin, solid shell we live on. It ranges from about 5–10 km thick under the oceans (**oceanic crust**, made of dense basalt) to 25–70 km thick under continents (**continental crust**, made of lighter granite).

The crust is rigid and brittle. It breaks under stress rather than flowing.

### The Mantle

Beneath the crust lies the **mantle**, extending about 2,900 km deep. It makes up roughly 84% of Earth's volume. The mantle is composed of silicate rock and is solid in the mechanical sense — but at the extreme temperatures and pressures present there (up to ~3,700°C), it behaves plastically over geological timescales, flowing like an extremely thick fluid.

The upper portion of the mantle, along with the crust above it, forms the **lithosphere** — a rigid layer that is broken into tectonic plates. Beneath the lithosphere is the **asthenosphere**, a partially molten layer of softer rock on which the plates float and move.

### The Outer Core

At 2,900–5,150 km depth, the **outer core** is composed primarily of liquid iron and nickel. The flow of this electrically conductive liquid generates Earth's **magnetic field** through a dynamo effect. The magnetic field shields the planet from harmful solar radiation and is essential to life.

### The Inner Core

The **inner core** extends from 5,150 km to the Earth's center at ~6,371 km. Despite temperatures exceeding 5,000°C — hotter than the surface of the Sun — the inner core is solid iron-nickel alloy. The immense pressure from the weight of the entire planet above it prevents it from melting.

---

## 3. The Moho Boundary

The boundary between the crust and mantle is called the **Mohorovičić discontinuity** (Moho), named after the Croatian seismologist who discovered it in 1909 by noticing that earthquake waves sped up sharply at a certain depth. The Moho is a genuine compositional boundary — the rocks change — not just a temperature or pressure transition.

---

## 4. Heat and Convection

The Earth's interior is hot for two reasons: residual heat from planetary formation 4.5 billion years ago, and ongoing heat produced by the **radioactive decay** of elements like uranium, thorium, and potassium in the mantle and crust.

This internal heat drives **convection currents** in the mantle — hot rock rises, spreads laterally, cools, and sinks back down. These slow, massive circulation cells are the engine behind plate tectonics, pushing the lithospheric plates across the surface of the planet.

---

## Summary

Earth has four main layers: the thin, brittle crust; the thick, flowing mantle; the liquid outer core that generates our magnetic field; and the solid inner core under enormous pressure. We know this from seismic wave analysis. Heat from radioactive decay drives mantle convection, which in turn drives the movement of tectonic plates at the surface.
