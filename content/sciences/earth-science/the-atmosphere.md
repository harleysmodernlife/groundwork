The atmosphere is the thin envelope of gas surrounding Earth — thin relative to the planet's size, yet complex enough to regulate temperature, filter radiation, drive weather, and make life possible. Understanding its structure and composition is the foundation of meteorology and climate science.

---

## 1. Composition of the Atmosphere

Earth's atmosphere is a mixture of gases:

- **Nitrogen (N₂)**: ~78% — chemically stable, acts as a diluting buffer
- **Oxygen (O₂)**: ~21% — essential for respiration and combustion
- **Argon (Ar)**: ~0.93% — inert noble gas
- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)**: ~0.04% — critical greenhouse gas and input to photosynthesis
- **Water vapor (H₂O)**: 0–4% — varies by location and altitude; the most powerful greenhouse gas
- **Trace gases**: ozone, methane, nitrous oxide, and others — small in quantity but significant in effect

Oxygen did not exist in significant quantities in Earth's early atmosphere. It accumulated over billions of years as a byproduct of photosynthesis by early cyanobacteria — one of the most consequential biological events in Earth's history.

## 2. Layers of the Atmosphere

The atmosphere is divided into layers based on how temperature changes with altitude.

### Troposphere

**0–12 km** (average). This is where all weather occurs and where nearly all water vapor exists. Temperature decreases with altitude at about 6.5°C per kilometer — the **environmental lapse rate**. The boundary at the top is the **tropopause**.

### Stratosphere

**12–50 km**. Temperature increases with altitude here because the **ozone layer** (concentrated O₃) absorbs ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun. This UV absorption warms the stratosphere and blocks harmful UV from reaching the surface. Jet aircraft cruise in the lower stratosphere to avoid turbulence.

### Mesosphere

**50–85 km**. Temperature falls again with altitude. The coldest temperatures in the atmosphere occur at the top of the mesosphere (~−90°C). This is where most meteors burn up.

### Thermosphere

**85–600 km**. Temperature rises dramatically as gas molecules absorb high-energy solar radiation — though the gas is so thin that it would feel freezing. The **International Space Station** orbits here. This layer contains the **ionosphere**, a region of charged particles that reflects radio waves and produces the **aurora borealis** and **aurora australis**.

### Exosphere

**600 km and beyond**. The outermost layer, where the atmosphere gradually merges with space. Gas molecules are so sparse that they rarely collide.

## 3. The Greenhouse Effect

The **greenhouse effect** is not inherently harmful — it is the natural process that keeps Earth about 33°C warmer than it would be without an atmosphere.

Here's how it works:
1. Solar radiation (mostly visible light) passes through the atmosphere and warms the surface.
2. The warmed surface radiates energy back upward as infrared (heat) radiation.
3. **Greenhouse gases** (CO₂, water vapor, methane, nitrous oxide) absorb this infrared radiation rather than letting it escape to space.
4. They re-radiate the energy in all directions, including back toward the surface, warming it further.

The concern with modern climate change is the **enhanced greenhouse effect** — the amplification of this natural process by the addition of greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and agriculture.

---

## 4. Air Pressure

The atmosphere has weight. **Air pressure** is the force per unit area exerted by the weight of the air above a point. At sea level, air pressure is about 101,325 Pa (pascals), or **1 atmosphere (atm)**. Pressure decreases with altitude because there is less air above.

Differences in air pressure drive wind: air moves from high pressure to low pressure, creating the winds that circulate heat around the globe.

---

## 5. The Ozone Layer

Concentrated in the stratosphere (15–35 km), the **ozone layer** absorbs 95–99% of the sun's ultraviolet radiation. UV radiation damages DNA and causes skin cancer, cataracts, and immune suppression. Without the ozone layer, life on land would be nearly impossible.

Human-produced chemicals called **chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)** — once widely used in refrigerants and aerosols — catalytically destroy ozone. The resulting "ozone hole" over Antarctica was discovered in the 1980s. The 1987 **Montreal Protocol** phased out CFCs globally, and the ozone layer is gradually recovering.

---

## Summary

Earth's atmosphere is layered — troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, exosphere — with temperature changes defining each boundary. It is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, with trace greenhouse gases that warm the surface through the greenhouse effect. The stratospheric ozone layer shields life from ultraviolet radiation. Air pressure differences drive wind, linking atmosphere to weather.
