The surface of the Earth is not a single solid shell. It is broken into about a dozen major pieces — **tectonic plates** — that float on the partially molten rock of the mantle and move continuously, dragging continents and ocean floors along with them. This movement, operating over millions of years, has shaped every mountain range, ocean basin, and continent on the planet.

---

## 1. The Theory of Plate Tectonics

The idea that continents move was proposed by Alfred Wegener in 1912, who noticed that South America and Africa fit together like puzzle pieces and that the same fossil species appeared on both continents despite thousands of miles of ocean between them. He called his hypothesis **continental drift**, but he couldn't explain the mechanism — and the scientific community largely rejected it.

The mechanism became clear in the 1950s and 60s when oceanographers discovered **seafloor spreading**: new oceanic crust forms at mid-ocean ridges where magma rises from the mantle, pushing the seafloor outward in both directions. This confirmed that the crust itself is in motion, and **plate tectonics** became accepted as the unifying theory of geology.

---

## 2. Types of Plate Boundaries

The interactions between plates happen at their edges — **boundaries** — and the type of interaction depends on how the plates move relative to each other.

### Divergent Boundaries

Plates move **apart**. Magma wells up to fill the gap, creating new crust. On the ocean floor, this forms **mid-ocean ridges** (like the Mid-Atlantic Ridge). On land, it forms rift valleys — the East African Rift is a continent in the process of splitting apart.

### Convergent Boundaries

Plates move **toward each other**. What happens depends on what's colliding:

- **Oceanic + continental**: the denser oceanic plate is forced beneath the continental plate in a process called **subduction**. The subducting plate melts into the mantle, and the overriding plate is pushed up into mountain ranges. Volcanoes and deep ocean trenches form here. Example: the Andes Mountains, the Cascades.
- **Oceanic + oceanic**: one plate subducts beneath the other, forming deep trenches and volcanic island arcs. Example: Japan, the Aleutian Islands.
- **Continental + continental**: neither plate subducts (both are too light), so the crust crumples and is pushed upward, forming massive mountain ranges with no volcanic activity. Example: the Himalayas.

### Transform Boundaries

Plates slide **horizontally past each other**. Crust is neither created nor destroyed. The grinding motion produces significant earthquakes. Example: the San Andreas Fault in California.

---

## 3. What Drives Plate Motion

The plates are moved by **convection currents** in the mantle. Hot rock rises, spreads outward beneath the plates, cools, and sinks — dragging the plates along. Additionally, the weight of cold, dense oceanic crust sinking at subduction zones (**slab pull**) is now considered the dominant driving force. The ridge pushing new crust apart (**ridge push**) also contributes.

Plate movement is slow — roughly 2–10 cm per year, about the rate a fingernail grows. Over hundreds of millions of years, that is enough to completely rearrange the continents.

---

## 4. Evidence for Plate Tectonics

- **Fossil distribution**: identical species found on continents now separated by oceans
- **Rock type matching**: geological formations that line up when continents are reassembled
- **Seafloor spreading**: symmetrical magnetic stripe patterns on either side of mid-ocean ridges (as the seafloor spreads, iron minerals lock in the polarity of Earth's magnetic field, which reverses periodically — creating a record of past plate motion)
- **GPS measurements**: modern satellite technology directly measures plate movement in real time

---

## Summary

Tectonic plates are massive sections of Earth's lithosphere that move because of mantle convection and slab pull. At divergent boundaries, plates spread apart and new crust forms. At convergent boundaries, crust is either subducted or crumpled into mountains. At transform boundaries, plates grind past each other. Over geological time, this process has shaped every major feature of Earth's surface.
