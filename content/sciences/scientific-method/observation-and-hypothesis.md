Science begins with paying attention. Before any experiment is designed or any data collected, a scientist must first observe the world carefully and ask a precise question about what they see. This process — moving from raw observation to a testable hypothesis — is the foundation of the entire scientific method.

---

## 1. Observation: More Than Just Looking

**Observation** is the act of gathering information about the world using the senses or instruments that extend them. A good scientific observation is specific, measurable, and recorded.

There are two types:

- **Qualitative observations** describe characteristics without numbers: *The solution turned blue. The leaf has serrated edges.*
- **Quantitative observations** use measurements and numbers: *The temperature rose 4°C in 10 minutes. The sample weighed 2.3 grams.*

Quantitative observations are generally more useful because they can be compared precisely across experiments and researchers. Qualitative observations often come first — they're what prompts a scientist to ask "why?" in the first place.

### The Role of Background Knowledge

Observation doesn't happen in a vacuum. A scientist with domain knowledge notices things a novice would miss. A biologist looking at a leaf doesn't just see green — they notice the arrangement of veins, signs of disease, and insect damage. **Background knowledge shapes what you observe and what questions you think to ask.**

---

## 2. The Research Question

Once an observation sparks curiosity, the next step is forming a **research question** — a focused, specific question the investigation will attempt to answer.

A weak question: *Why do plants grow?*
A strong question: *Does the amount of daily light exposure affect the height of bean seedlings after two weeks?*

The strong version specifies what's being measured (height), what's being changed (light), what organism is involved (bean seedlings), and over what time period (two weeks). **A vague question produces vague science.**

---

## 3. The Hypothesis

A **hypothesis** is a proposed explanation for an observation — a testable, falsifiable answer to the research question. It is not a random guess. A good hypothesis is grounded in existing knowledge and makes a specific, checkable prediction.

### The If-Then Format

The most reliable format is **if-then**:

*If [independent variable changes in a specific way], then [dependent variable will respond predictably], because [brief reasoning].*

**Example:** *If bean seedlings receive 12 hours of light per day instead of 6, then they will grow taller after two weeks, because light provides the energy plants use for photosynthesis and growth.*

This format forces you to identify:
- The **independent variable** — what you deliberately change
- The **dependent variable** — what you measure as a result
- The **reasoning** — why you expect this relationship

### Falsifiability

The most important property of a scientific hypothesis is **falsifiability** — it must be possible for evidence to prove it wrong. A claim that can't be tested or disproved isn't a scientific hypothesis; it's an assumption.

*"Plants grow because they want to"* is not falsifiable. *"Plants exposed to more light grow taller"* is — you can design an experiment where that prediction fails.

---

## 4. Hypothesis vs. Theory vs. Law

These three terms are routinely confused.

- A **hypothesis** is an untested explanation — a starting point.
- A **theory** is a well-tested explanation backed by substantial evidence from many independent lines of research.
- A **scientific law** describes *what* happens, often mathematically, without explaining *why*. Newton's Law of Gravitation describes how gravity behaves; it doesn't explain what gravity is.

A hypothesis that survives repeated testing doesn't automatically become a theory. It takes an enormous body of converging evidence. Evolution, cell theory, and germ theory are all called "theories" — meaning they are among the most strongly supported explanations in all of science.

---

## 5. A Worked Example

**Observation:** A farmer notices crops near the fence line are consistently shorter than crops in the center of the field.

**Research question:** Does proximity to the fence affect crop height?

**Background research:** The fence runs east to west. Trees grow along the fence and may block morning sunlight.

**Hypothesis:** If crops grow in the shadow of fence-line trees, then they will be shorter than crops in full sunlight, because reduced light limits photosynthesis and slows growth.

This hypothesis is specific, grounded in biology, and falsifiable. A controlled experiment would measure light levels and crop heights at various distances from the fence while holding all other variables — water, soil type, seed variety — constant.

---

## Summary

Good science starts with careful observation, a focused question, and a falsifiable hypothesis. These three steps don't guarantee a correct answer, but they guarantee a meaningful one — a result that actually tells you something about the world.
