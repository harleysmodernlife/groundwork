Collecting data is only half the work. The other half is making sense of it — organizing raw numbers and observations into patterns, testing whether those patterns are real or coincidental, and drawing conclusions that are honest about what the evidence actually shows.

---

## 1. Types of Data

Scientific data comes in two forms:

- **Quantitative data**: numerical measurements. *The seedling grew 4.2 cm in seven days. The sample had a pH of 6.8.* Quantitative data can be compared, averaged, graphed, and analyzed statistically.
- **Qualitative data**: descriptive observations without numbers. *The solution turned cloudy. The leaves showed yellowing at the edges.* Qualitative data is useful for generating hypotheses and capturing things that resist measurement.

Most experiments rely primarily on quantitative data because it supports more rigorous analysis.

---

## 2. Organizing and Displaying Data

Raw data is nearly impossible to interpret at a glance. Scientists organize it into:

- **Data tables**: rows and columns that make individual values easy to compare
- **Graphs**: visual representations that reveal trends, distributions, and relationships
  - **Bar graphs**: compare values across discrete categories
  - **Line graphs**: show change over time or across a continuous variable
  - **Scatter plots**: reveal correlations between two variables

A well-constructed graph should have labeled axes with units, a clear title, and a scale that doesn't distort the data. Manipulating a graph's scale to exaggerate or minimize differences is a common form of misleading presentation.

---

## 3. Analyzing Data

### Measures of Central Tendency

- **Mean**: the arithmetic average. Useful but sensitive to outliers.
- **Median**: the middle value when data is sorted. More robust when extreme values are present.
- **Mode**: the most frequently occurring value. Used most often with categorical data.

### Variability

Understanding spread matters as much as understanding the center. **Standard deviation** measures how much individual values deviate from the mean on average. A small standard deviation means data clusters tightly around the mean; a large one means wide variation.

### Statistical Significance

When two groups produce different averages, the question is whether the difference is real or just noise from random variation. **Statistical significance** (expressed as a p-value) estimates the probability of seeing a difference at least this large by chance alone if there were no real effect.

A p-value below 0.05 is the conventional threshold — it means there's less than a 5% chance the result is random. This is a threshold, not a guarantee. A statistically significant result can still be practically meaningless if the effect size is tiny.

---

## 4. Drawing Conclusions

Conclusions should directly address the original hypothesis: is it supported, not supported, or inconclusive?

### Supported vs. Proven

A hypothesis is **supported** by data — never definitively **proven**. Science works by accumulating evidence, and future experiments might reveal conditions under which the hypothesis fails. Saying a hypothesis is "proven" overstates certainty.

### Not Supported ≠ Worthless

A hypothesis that is not supported by the data is still a valuable result. It eliminates an explanation, narrows the possibilities, and — if published — prevents other researchers from wasting time on the same dead end. Negative results matter.

### Sources of Error

Honest scientific conclusions acknowledge potential errors:

- **Random error**: unpredictable variation from measurement precision, environmental fluctuations, or biological variability. Minimized by larger sample sizes.
- **Systematic error**: consistent bias in one direction — a miscalibrated scale, a flawed survey question, or a non-representative sample. Cannot be fixed by increasing sample size; requires redesigning the method.

---

## 5. Correlation and Causation

One of the most common errors in interpreting data is conflating **correlation** (two variables moving together) with **causation** (one causing the other).

Ice cream sales and drowning rates both rise in summer. They are correlated — but ice cream doesn't cause drowning. Both are caused by a third variable: hot weather driving people to pools and beaches. This third factor is called a **confounding variable**.

Establishing causation requires more than correlation. It typically requires a controlled experiment in which all confounding variables are held constant and only the independent variable is changed.

---

## Summary

Data analysis transforms raw measurements into understanding. Organizing data reveals patterns; statistics test whether those patterns are real; conclusions assess what the evidence actually supports. The best scientific conclusions are precise about what was tested, honest about uncertainty, and careful never to claim more than the data shows.
