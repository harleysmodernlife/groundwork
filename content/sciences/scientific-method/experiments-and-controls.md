A hypothesis is only as good as the experiment designed to test it. Controlled experiments are the core tool of empirical science — they allow researchers to isolate a single variable and observe its effect while everything else stays constant.

---

## 1. The Logic of a Controlled Experiment

The goal of an experiment is to answer one question: does changing **X** cause a change in **Y**? To answer that cleanly, only X can vary. If multiple things change at once, you can't tell which one caused the result.

A **controlled experiment** holds all conditions constant except one — the variable being tested. This design lets the experimenter attribute any observed change directly to that variable.

---

## 2. Variables

Every experiment involves three categories of variables:

- **Independent variable** — what the experimenter deliberately changes. This is the cause being tested. *Example: the amount of fertilizer added to plants.*
- **Dependent variable** — what is measured to see if a change occurred. This is the effect being observed. *Example: plant height after four weeks.*
- **Controlled variables** — everything else that is held constant: temperature, light, water, pot size, soil type, seed variety. If any of these vary unexpectedly, they become **confounding variables** that muddy the results.

Identifying and controlling confounding variables is one of the hardest parts of real experimental design.

---

## 3. The Control Group

An experiment needs a **control group** — a group that receives no treatment. It serves as the baseline for comparison. Without a control, you have nothing to compare your treatment group against.

- **Experimental group**: receives the independent variable (e.g., plants given fertilizer)
- **Control group**: receives no treatment or a neutral treatment (e.g., plants given plain water)

The difference between the two groups — if any — is the effect of the independent variable.

### Placebos

In experiments involving humans, the control group often receives a **placebo** — a treatment that looks identical to the real one but has no active ingredient. This controls for the **placebo effect**, the documented tendency for people to improve simply because they believe they're being treated.

---

## 4. Sample Size and Replication

One plant or one trial proves nothing. Experiments require:

- **Adequate sample size**: testing many subjects reduces the influence of random variation. One unusual plant won't skew the average when you're measuring 50.
- **Replication**: repeating the entire experiment. If a different researcher, in a different lab, gets the same results, confidence in the finding increases dramatically.

A result from a sample of three is anecdote. A result replicated across hundreds is evidence.

---

## 5. Blind and Double-Blind Designs

Human bias can corrupt data even when researchers try to be objective. Two designs address this:

- **Single-blind**: participants don't know which group they're in (treatment or control), but researchers do.
- **Double-blind**: neither participants nor researchers administering the treatment know who is in which group. A third party manages the group assignments.

Double-blind designs are the gold standard for clinical trials because they eliminate both participant expectation effects and unconscious researcher bias in measurement.

---

## 6. A Worked Example

**Hypothesis:** If students study with background music, they will score lower on comprehension tests than students who study in silence, because auditory distraction competes for cognitive resources.

**Experimental design:**
- **Independent variable**: presence of background music (music vs. silence)
- **Dependent variable**: score on a standardized reading comprehension test
- **Controlled variables**: same reading passage, same test, same study duration (20 minutes), same time of day, same room temperature
- **Control group**: studies in silence
- **Experimental group**: studies with music playing at 65 dB
- **Sample size**: 60 students per group (120 total)

At the end, scores are compared. If the music group scores significantly lower, the hypothesis is supported. If there's no difference, it is not.

---

## Summary

Controlled experiments work by changing one variable, measuring its effect on another, and holding everything else constant. Control groups, adequate sample sizes, and blind designs all exist for the same reason: to make sure the data reflects reality rather than researcher expectations or random chance.
