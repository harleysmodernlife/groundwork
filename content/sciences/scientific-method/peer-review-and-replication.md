Science is not a collection of facts — it is a process that continuously checks itself. Two mechanisms make this self-correction possible: **peer review**, which vets findings before they are published, and **replication**, which tests whether findings hold up when others repeat the work.

---

## 1. Why Science Needs Verification

Any single scientist can make mistakes — in experimental design, data collection, statistical analysis, or interpretation. Bias, both conscious and unconscious, can skew results. Equipment malfunctions. Random chance occasionally produces misleading outcomes. No individual result, however carefully obtained, should be accepted without scrutiny.

The scientific community addresses this with a structured verification process built on the assumption that **every finding should be treated as provisional until confirmed by independent evidence**.

---

## 2. Peer Review

Before a study is published in a scientific journal, it undergoes **peer review** — evaluation by other experts in the same field who were not involved in the research.

### How It Works

1. Researchers submit a manuscript to a journal.
2. The journal editor sends the manuscript to two to four independent reviewers with relevant expertise.
3. Reviewers assess the methodology, data, statistical analysis, and conclusions. They may approve the study, request revisions, or reject it.
4. The authors revise and resubmit if needed.
5. Only studies that survive this process are published.

### What Peer Review Catches

- Flawed experimental designs
- Incorrect statistical methods
- Conclusions that go beyond what the data supports
- Missing context or contradicting prior literature

### What Peer Review Does Not Guarantee

Peer review is powerful but imperfect. Reviewers cannot catch fraud if data is fabricated, and they may miss subtle errors. **Publication in a peer-reviewed journal means a study passed initial expert scrutiny — not that it is definitely correct.** This is why replication matters.

---

## 3. Replication

**Replication** is the process of repeating an experiment to see if the same results occur. It is the ultimate test of a scientific finding.

- **Direct replication**: the same methods are followed as closely as possible in a new lab or with a new sample.
- **Conceptual replication**: the underlying hypothesis is tested using a different method or population to see if the effect generalizes.

If an independent group of researchers runs the same experiment and gets the same result, confidence in the finding rises substantially. If they get a different result, the original finding is called into question.

### The Replication Crisis

In the 2010s, researchers across psychology, medicine, and nutrition attempted to replicate hundreds of published studies and found that a significant portion could not be replicated. This **replication crisis** forced a reckoning with publication bias (journals favor positive results), small sample sizes, and statistical practices that make chance findings look significant.

The response has included pre-registration of studies (researchers publicly declare their hypothesis and methods before collecting data), larger sample requirements, and open data standards.

---

## 4. The Role of Independent Confirmation

Replication by independent researchers carries far more weight than replication by the original team. An independent group has no stake in the original result being correct. They bring different equipment, different samples, and different potential biases — and if they still get the same answer, the finding is on much firmer ground.

This is why scientific consensus develops slowly. A single landmark study doesn't change the consensus; a body of independently replicated evidence does.

---

## 5. Science vs. Pseudoscience

One marker of pseudoscience is the **unfalsifiable finding** — a claim that can't be tested, or results that can't be replicated by anyone except the original proponent. Legitimate science welcomes scrutiny. Claims that resist independent verification or that only "work" for one researcher in one lab should be viewed skeptically.

---

## Summary

Peer review and replication are the immune system of science. Peer review checks a study before it reaches the public. Replication tests whether the finding survives contact with reality under different hands and conditions. Together, they transform a single observation into established knowledge — slowly, carefully, and with healthy skepticism at every step.
