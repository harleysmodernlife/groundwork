Standard economic models are built on a powerful assumption: people are rational. They have consistent preferences, process information accurately, and make choices that maximize their well-being. This assumption is mathematically convenient and generates useful predictions — but decades of research in psychology and economics have revealed that it is systematically wrong in specific, predictable ways.

**Behavioral economics** studies how people actually make decisions, and why those decisions often deviate from the predictions of classical theory.

---

## 1. The Standard Rational Actor Model

Classical economic theory assumes individuals:
- Have **stable, consistent preferences** — if you prefer A to B and B to C, you prefer A to C
- **Maximize expected utility** — when facing uncertain outcomes, they weigh probabilities correctly
- **Process information accurately** — without bias or selective attention
- **Are unaffected by irrelevant factors** — like how a choice is framed or what reference point they start from

This is the **homo economicus** — economic man — a fictional creature of pure rationality. No one fully meets this standard, but the question is whether deviations are random (canceling out in aggregate) or systematic (clustering in predictable directions).

Behavioral economics shows they are systematic.

---

## 2. Loss Aversion and Prospect Theory

**Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory** (1979) is the cornerstone of behavioral economics. Key finding: losses hurt roughly **twice as much** as equivalent gains feel good.

Rational theory predicts that gaining $100 and losing $100 should feel equal and opposite. They don't. Losing $100 generates about twice the emotional impact of gaining $100.

This **loss aversion** explains many puzzles:
- People hold losing investments too long (to avoid "locking in" a loss) and sell winning ones too soon
- Workers resist wage cuts more than they welcome equivalent raises
- Insurance is purchased even when expected value says otherwise
- Negotiators fight hardest not to lose what they already have

**Reference points** matter enormously. Whether an outcome feels like a gain or a loss depends on what you compare it to — your expectations, the status quo, or an arbitrary anchor.

---

## 3. The Endowment Effect

People value things more once they own them. Give someone a mug, then offer to trade it for something of equivalent value — most people refuse.

Standard theory says ownership shouldn't affect value. Behavioral theory says it does: possession shifts the reference point, and trading away the mug feels like a loss.

The endowment effect has implications for contract negotiation, property disputes, and any situation where people must give something up to gain something else.

---

## 4. Heuristics and Cognitive Biases

People use mental shortcuts (**heuristics**) to simplify decisions. These work reasonably well most of the time but generate systematic errors:

**Availability heuristic** — Events that come to mind easily feel more probable. Plane crashes, shark attacks, and lottery wins are vivid and memorable; they feel more likely than statistics warrant.

**Representativeness heuristic** — People judge probability by similarity to a prototype, ignoring base rates. If someone sounds like a librarian, they're judged more likely to be a librarian than a salesperson — even though salespeople vastly outnumber librarians.

**Anchoring** — People start with an initial number (even an arbitrary one) and adjust insufficiently. Tell someone a wheel of fortune landed on 65 before asking the percentage of African nations in the UN — they'll guess higher than if it landed on 10.

**Overconfidence** — People consistently overestimate their knowledge, skill, and the accuracy of their predictions. Most people think they're above-average drivers.

**Confirmation bias** — People seek information that confirms their existing beliefs and discount information that challenges them.

---

## 5. Present Bias and Time Inconsistency

People heavily discount the future relative to the present — more than rational models predict. This **hyperbolic discounting** means:

- We prefer $100 today over $110 next week (but would rationally accept the wait)
- We chronically underinvest in retirement savings, health, and education
- We make plans for the future that we don't follow through on

A related concept: **time inconsistency**. On Monday, you plan to exercise tomorrow. Tuesday arrives and you don't feel like it. The future self and present self have different preferences — and the present self usually wins.

This explains why commitment devices (automatic enrollment in savings plans, gym contracts with cancellation penalties) can help people achieve their own stated goals.

---

## 6. Framing Effects

Identical information presented differently generates different choices. Surgeries are more popular when described as having a "90% survival rate" than a "10% mortality rate." Ground beef sells better labeled "80% lean" than "20% fat." People choose differently from menus organized differently.

Rational theory says framing shouldn't matter — only the underlying facts do. Behavioral theory shows framing is often decisive.

---

## 7. Social Preferences

People aren't purely self-interested. They care about fairness, reciprocity, and others' outcomes:

- **Ultimatum game**: If offered an unfair split of $10 (you get $1, I get $9), most people reject it — giving up real money to punish perceived unfairness
- **Dictator game**: People voluntarily give money away even when anonymously assigned all the power
- Workers who feel underpaid reduce their effort even when the wage is objectively acceptable

---

## Summary

Behavioral economics demonstrates that human decision-making deviates from rationality in systematic, predictable ways: loss aversion, heuristics, anchoring, present bias, framing effects, and social preferences all shape choices. These deviations aren't random noise — they follow patterns that behavioral economists can model and predict. This research has reshaped how economists think about markets, policy design, and human motivation, and has given rise to the field of nudge theory and libertarian paternalism as approaches to improving decisions without restricting freedom.
