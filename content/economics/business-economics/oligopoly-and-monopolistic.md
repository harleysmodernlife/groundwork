Most real markets fall between the extremes of perfect competition and pure monopoly. Two intermediate structures — **monopolistic competition** and **oligopoly** — describe the majority of industries consumers actually encounter.

---

## 1. Monopolistic Competition

**Monopolistic competition** combines elements of both monopoly and competition:

- **Many sellers** — like perfect competition
- **Differentiated products** — unlike perfect competition, each firm's product is unique in some way (brand, quality, location, style)
- **Free entry and exit** — no significant barriers

Examples: restaurants, clothing brands, hair salons, bookstores. Each restaurant has its own menu and atmosphere; none can ignore competitors, but none is directly substitutable for any other.

### Pricing in Monopolistic Competition

Because products are differentiated, each firm faces a **downward-sloping demand curve** — a small amount of market power. Like a monopolist, it sets MR = MC and prices above marginal cost.

But this power is limited. Close substitutes exist. Raise prices too much and customers go elsewhere.

### Long-Run Equilibrium

Like perfect competition, free entry erodes profits. If restaurants in a neighborhood are profitable, new restaurants open. Demand for each existing restaurant shifts left until **economic profit = 0**.

In long-run equilibrium, price exceeds marginal cost (unlike perfect competition), and firms produce below their minimum average total cost — **excess capacity**. The variety offered to consumers may justify this inefficiency, but each firm operates with unused capacity.

### Product Differentiation and Advertising

Firms in monopolistic competition invest in differentiation: branding, quality improvements, unique features, and advertising. Advertising shifts demand curves and builds loyalty — but also raises costs. Whether advertising is primarily informative (increasing efficiency) or persuasive (creating artificial loyalty) is debated.

## 2. Oligopoly

**Oligopoly** is a market dominated by a small number of large firms. Each firm's decisions significantly affect the others.

Examples: airlines, major automakers, smartphone manufacturers, wireless carriers, beer (in many countries), major streaming services.

Key features:
- **Few sellers** — typically 2–10 significant players
- **High barriers to entry** — capital requirements, economies of scale, patents, brand loyalty
- **Interdependence** — what one firm does affects all others; strategic behavior is essential

### Interdependence and Strategy

An oligopolist can't make pricing or production decisions without considering rivals' responses. If American Airlines cuts fares, United must respond. If Ford raises quality, GM must react. This interdependence makes oligopoly the domain of **game theory** — the study of strategic decision-making.

### The Prisoner's Dilemma

A classic framework for understanding oligopoly behavior. If two competing firms can choose to compete aggressively or cooperate (tacitly), the logic of self-interest often pushes both toward aggressive competition — even though both would be better off cooperating. Each fears the other will defect, so both end up in the worse equilibrium.

### Collusion and Cartels

Firms may try to escape the prisoner's dilemma by colluding: agreeing to fix prices, limit output, or divide markets. A **cartel** (like OPEC among oil-producing nations) acts like a collective monopolist, maximizing joint profits.

In most countries, explicit price-fixing agreements among competitors are **illegal** under antitrust law. The temptation is strong — each firm earns more if all restrict output — but each firm also has an incentive to cheat (produce more than its agreed share while others hold back). Cartels often break down for this reason.

**Tacit collusion** — coordinating behavior without explicit agreement — is legal but blurs into antitrust grey areas. Firms may use "price signaling" (announcing prices publicly) or follow a price leader's moves.

### The Kinked Demand Curve

One model of oligopoly pricing suggests prices are relatively stable: if one firm raises its price, rivals don't follow (they gain customers), so demand for the price-raiser collapses. If one firm cuts its price, rivals match the cut immediately to protect market share. This "kink" in the demand curve discourages price changes — firms prefer stability.

## 3. Comparing Market Structures

| Structure | Sellers | Product | Entry | Price vs. MC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition | Many | Identical | Free | P = MC |
| Monopolistic competition | Many | Differentiated | Free | P > MC |
| Oligopoly | Few | Similar or differentiated | Barriers | P > MC |
| Monopoly | One | No substitutes | Blocked | P >> MC |

---

## Summary

Monopolistic competition features many sellers with differentiated products and free entry — yielding zero long-run profit but persistent excess capacity. Oligopoly features few firms with significant interdependence; strategic behavior and game theory explain pricing and competition. Both structures produce prices above marginal cost and some deadweight loss, but they also generate product variety, innovation, and the kinds of markets that make up most of the real economy.
