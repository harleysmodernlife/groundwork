Before a business can decide how much to produce or what price to charge, it must understand its costs. Economic cost analysis goes deeper than accounting — it distinguishes between costs that change with output and costs that don't, and it includes costs that never appear on a balance sheet.

---

## 1. Explicit vs. Implicit Costs

**Explicit costs** are direct, out-of-pocket payments: wages, rent, raw materials, utilities. These appear on income statements.

**Implicit costs** are the opportunity costs of using resources you already own. If you use your own building for your business, you forgo rental income you could have earned. If you manage the business yourself, you forgo the salary you could earn elsewhere.

**Economic profit** subtracts both explicit and implicit costs from revenue. **Accounting profit** only subtracts explicit costs. A business can be profitable by accounting standards but be destroying value economically — if the owner's time and capital could earn more elsewhere.

---

## 2. Fixed Costs

**Fixed costs** don't change with the quantity of output produced. Whether you make 1 widget or 10,000, you pay the same:

- Rent on a factory or office
- Equipment lease payments
- Insurance premiums
- Salaries of salaried employees

Fixed costs exist in the short run. In the long run, all costs become variable — a firm can move to a smaller building or exit the market entirely.

**Average fixed cost (AFC)** = Fixed costs ÷ Quantity produced. As output rises, AFC falls — the fixed cost is spread over more units. This is why scale matters.

---

## 3. Variable Costs

**Variable costs** change directly with output. More production means more:

- Raw materials
- Hourly labor
- Energy consumption
- Packaging and shipping

**Average variable cost (AVC)** = Variable costs ÷ Quantity. AVC typically falls at first (as efficiency improves with scale) and then rises (as diminishing returns set in).

---

## 4. Total Cost and Average Total Cost

**Total cost (TC)** = Fixed costs + Variable costs

**Average total cost (ATC)** = Total cost ÷ Quantity = AFC + AVC

The ATC curve is U-shaped: it falls initially as fixed costs are spread over more units and efficiency gains accumulate, then rises as diminishing returns push variable costs up faster than output increases.

The **minimum point of the ATC curve** is economically significant — it represents the most efficient scale of production.

---

## 5. Marginal Cost

**Marginal cost (MC)** is the cost of producing one additional unit. It is the most important cost concept for production decisions.

MC = Change in total cost ÷ Change in quantity

MC typically falls at first (as efficiency improves) and then rises (diminishing returns). The MC curve intersects the ATC curve at its minimum — a mathematical relationship that holds for all cost curves of this shape.

**The key rule**: A profit-maximizing firm produces up to the point where MC = MR (marginal cost equals marginal revenue). Beyond that point, each additional unit costs more to make than it earns.

---

## 6. Sunk Costs

**Sunk costs** are costs that have already been paid and cannot be recovered. They should have no influence on future decisions — but psychologically, they often do.

If you've spent $500 on a concert ticket and the day arrives but you're sick, the rational decision is based on how you feel today — not on the $500 you already spent. That money is gone either way.

In business: a company that has invested $50 million in a failing project should abandon it if future prospects are poor — not continue throwing good money after bad to "recover" the sunk cost. **Ignore sunk costs. Look only at future costs and benefits.**

---

## 7. Short Run vs. Long Run

In the **short run**, at least one input is fixed. A restaurant can't instantly expand its kitchen, so it manages costs within existing capacity.

In the **long run**, all inputs are variable. The firm can build a larger plant, adopt new technology, or change its entire cost structure. Long-run cost analysis shapes strategic investment decisions.

---

## Summary

Understanding costs is the foundation of business decision-making. Fixed costs don't change with output; variable costs do. Average total cost is U-shaped. Marginal cost drives production decisions — produce until MC equals marginal revenue. Implicit costs and opportunity costs matter even when they don't appear on the books. And sunk costs, no matter how painful, should be ignored when deciding what to do next.
