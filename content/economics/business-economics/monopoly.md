A monopoly exists when a single firm is the only seller of a product with no close substitutes. Unlike competitive firms, a monopolist isn't a price taker — it faces the entire market demand curve and can choose its price. This market power allows profits that would be competed away in a competitive market, but it comes with significant efficiency costs.

---

## 1. Sources of Monopoly Power

How does a monopoly arise? Several conditions can create and sustain one:

**Natural monopoly** — Some industries have economies of scale so significant that one firm can serve the entire market more cheaply than multiple competitors. Utilities (water, electricity) are classic examples: it would be wasteful to build competing pipe networks. Average costs fall continuously as output grows.

**Patents and copyrights** — Intellectual property law grants exclusive rights for a limited period. A pharmaceutical company that develops a new drug holds a patent monopoly — no competitor can sell the identical product for years.

**Control of essential resources** — De Beers historically controlled much of the world's diamond supply. Alcoa once controlled most U.S. aluminum production.

**Government franchise** — A government may grant exclusive rights to a single provider (a cable company serving a particular city, the postal service for first-class mail).

**Network effects** — Some products become more valuable as more people use them. This can create winner-take-all dynamics (operating systems, social networks).

---

## 2. Monopoly Pricing: The Price Maker

Unlike competitive firms, a monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve. To sell more, it must lower its price. This means:

**Marginal revenue < Price.** When a monopolist sells one more unit by lowering price, it earns the price of that unit but loses revenue on all previous units (which now sell at a lower price too). MR falls faster than the demand curve.

The monopolist still maximizes profit by setting **MR = MC**, but the resulting price is **above** marginal cost — consumers pay more than the cost of the last unit produced. In perfect competition, P = MC; in monopoly, P > MC.

---

## 3. Deadweight Loss

The monopolist produces less and charges more than a competitive market would. This creates **deadweight loss** — transactions that would benefit both buyer and seller but don't happen because the price is too high.

Some consumers who value the product above its marginal cost of production — and would have bought it in a competitive market — don't buy in the monopoly. Value is destroyed. Resources that could be used productively sit idle. This is the core efficiency argument against monopoly.

---

## 4. Profit in the Long Run

In competitive markets, economic profit attracts entry and is competed away. In monopoly, barriers to entry prevent competitors from entering. A monopolist can sustain **positive economic profit in the long run** — which is why monopoly power is so valuable to firms and so costly to consumers.

---

## 5. Price Discrimination

A monopolist with market power can increase profit by charging different prices to different customers based on their willingness to pay — a practice called **price discrimination**.

- **First-degree** (perfect): charge each customer their maximum willingness to pay. Eliminates consumer surplus entirely. Theoretically efficient (no deadweight loss) but rarely achievable.
- **Second-degree**: charge different prices based on quantity (bulk discounts).
- **Third-degree**: charge different prices to different groups (student discounts, senior prices, business vs. economy airline tickets).

Price discrimination is common and often welfare-increasing compared to a single monopoly price — more customers are served, though the distribution of surplus shifts from consumers to the firm.

---

## 6. Regulation of Monopoly

Because natural monopolies are often unavoidable, governments regulate rather than prohibit them:

**Rate-of-return regulation** — Set prices to cover costs plus a "fair" return on investment. Problem: reduces incentive to minimize costs.

**Price cap regulation** — Set a ceiling price, letting the firm keep efficiency gains. Better incentives, but may lead to underinvestment if the cap is too low.

**Public ownership** — Government runs the monopoly directly. Common in Europe for utilities.

**Antitrust action** — If a monopoly was acquired through anticompetitive behavior (not just superior efficiency), the government can break it up or restrict its practices. The 1911 Standard Oil breakup and the 1984 AT&T breakup are historical examples.

---

## Summary

Monopoly arises when one firm controls a market through natural advantages, intellectual property, resource control, or government franchise. Monopolists price above marginal cost, produce less than socially optimal quantities, and sustain long-run economic profits. The result is deadweight loss — value destroyed by transactions that don't happen. Governments respond through regulation or antitrust enforcement. Price discrimination is a strategy to extract more consumer surplus while potentially serving more customers than single-price monopoly.
