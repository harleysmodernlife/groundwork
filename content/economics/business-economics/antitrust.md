Markets generate enormous wealth and efficiency — but left unchecked, powerful firms can eliminate competition, fix prices, and harm consumers. **Antitrust law** (called **competition law** outside the U.S.) is the body of rules designed to preserve competitive markets and prevent the abuse of market power.

---

## 1. Why Antitrust?

Competition benefits consumers through lower prices, more choices, and greater innovation. When firms dominate markets through anticompetitive means, these benefits erode:

- Prices rise above competitive levels
- Quality improvements slow
- Barriers prevent new entrants from challenging incumbents
- Smaller suppliers and buyers are squeezed

Antitrust law exists to prevent this — not by ensuring equal outcomes for all firms, but by preserving the competitive process itself.

---

## 2. The Core U.S. Antitrust Laws

**The Sherman Antitrust Act (1890)** — The foundation. Two key provisions:
- *Section 1*: Prohibits contracts, combinations, or conspiracies in restraint of trade. Price-fixing agreements between competitors are illegal per se — no economic justification makes them legal.
- *Section 2*: Prohibits monopolization or attempts to monopolize. Not having a monopoly (which may arise legitimately from superior competition), but acquiring or maintaining one through anticompetitive conduct.

**The Clayton Act (1914)** — Addresses specific practices:
- Price discrimination that harms competition
- Exclusive dealing contracts and tying arrangements (under certain conditions)
- Mergers and acquisitions that substantially lessen competition

**The Federal Trade Commission Act (1914)** — Created the FTC. Prohibits "unfair methods of competition" and "unfair or deceptive acts or practices."

---

## 3. Prohibited Conduct

**Per se illegal** (no justification accepted):
- **Price fixing** — Competitors agreeing to set prices at a certain level
- **Bid rigging** — Competitors coordinating who wins contracts
- **Market allocation** — Competitors dividing territories or customers
- **Group boycotts** — Competitors agreeing to refuse to deal with a third party

**Rule of reason** (evaluated case by case, weighing competitive effects):
- Vertical price restraints (a manufacturer setting minimum resale prices)
- Exclusive dealing arrangements
- Non-compete clauses
- Tying arrangements (requiring customers to buy product B to get product A)

The rule of reason asks: does this practice harm competition overall, or does it have legitimate procompetitive justifications?

---

## 4. Merger Review

Before completing large mergers, companies must notify the FTC and Department of Justice (DOJ). Regulators evaluate whether the merger would "substantially lessen competition."

Key analytical tools:
- **Market definition** — What market are we in? A narrow definition (cola drinks) vs. a broad one (all beverages) dramatically changes concentration assessments.
- **Market concentration** — The **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)** measures market concentration by summing squared market shares. High HHI + significant increase from merger → likely challenge.
- **Competitive effects** — Will the combined firm gain power to raise prices? Will it eliminate a direct competitive threat?

Remedies:
- **Block the merger** outright
- **Require divestitures** — sell off parts of the business to a competitor
- **Impose behavioral conditions** — require certain conduct (access to rivals, non-discrimination)

Historical cases: the blocked AT&T/T-Mobile merger (2011), the DOJ's challenge to the UnitedHealth/Change Healthcare merger (2022).

---

## 5. Major Historical Cases

**Standard Oil (1911)** — Rockefeller's Standard Oil controlled ~90% of U.S. refining capacity through predatory pricing and exclusive railroad deals. The Supreme Court ordered it broken into 34 independent companies.

**AT&T (1984)** — The Bell System's monopoly on long-distance and local telephone was broken up into regional "Baby Bells" and a separate long-distance company.

**Microsoft (2000)** — Found to have illegally maintained its Windows monopoly by bundling Internet Explorer and pressuring PC makers. The remedy (a proposed breakup) was overturned on appeal; Microsoft accepted behavioral remedies.

**Current tech cases** — DOJ and FTC have filed antitrust suits against Google (search advertising), Apple (App Store), Amazon (e-commerce), and Meta (social media acquisitions). These cases are ongoing.

---

## 6. Criticism and Debate

Antitrust is contested:
- **Chicago School critics** argue that many "anticompetitive" practices actually benefit consumers (efficiency gains, lower costs) and that markets self-correct. They prefer high evidentiary bars.
- **Neo-Brandeisian critics** argue that modern antitrust is too narrow (focused only on consumer prices) and misses concentration's effects on workers, suppliers, democracy, and innovation. They call for more aggressive enforcement.

The question of whether Big Tech requires new regulatory approaches — or can be handled with existing antitrust law — is one of the central policy debates of the era.

---

## Summary

Antitrust law preserves competition by prohibiting price-fixing, monopolization through anticompetitive conduct, and mergers that significantly reduce competition. Per se violations (price-fixing, bid-rigging) are automatically illegal; other practices are evaluated under the rule of reason. Merger review assesses market concentration and competitive effects. Historical cases broke up Standard Oil and AT&T; current enforcement focuses on Big Tech. Antitrust remains contested: how much market concentration is acceptable, and what tools best preserve competitive markets, are live debates.
