Economies don't grow in a straight line. They expand, slow down, contract, and recover — then do it again. This repeating pattern is called the **business cycle**, and understanding it helps explain why unemployment rises and falls, why recessions happen, and what governments and central banks do in response.

---

## 1. The Four Phases

The business cycle has four recognizable phases:

**Expansion** — Output grows, employment rises, consumer spending increases, and businesses invest. This is the "good times" phase. Incomes climb, confidence is high, and credit flows freely.

**Peak** — The economy reaches its highest point before a downturn. Growth may slow, inflation may rise, and imbalances start to build. The peak is only identifiable in hindsight.

**Contraction (Recession)** — GDP falls for at least two consecutive quarters. Unemployment rises, businesses cut investment, consumers pull back, and credit tightens. A severe, prolonged contraction is called a **depression**.

**Trough** — The lowest point of the cycle. Growth halts, unemployment peaks, and conditions stabilize before recovery begins.

The cycle then repeats — though no two cycles are identical in length, depth, or cause.

---

## 2. What Causes Business Cycles?

Economists disagree on the precise causes, but several forces consistently shape cycles:

**Demand shocks** — Sudden changes in spending. A surge in consumer confidence can trigger an expansion; a financial crisis can trigger a contraction by freezing credit and collapsing demand.

**Supply shocks** — Unexpected changes on the production side. A spike in oil prices raises costs across the economy and can stall growth (as happened in the 1970s). A technological breakthrough can do the opposite.

**Credit and investment cycles** — When credit is cheap and optimism is high, businesses over-invest. When the bubble bursts — as in 2008 — the resulting pullback is severe.

**Inventory cycles** — Businesses build up inventories during expansions and draw them down during contractions, amplifying the underlying cycle.

**External events** — Wars, pandemics, natural disasters, and geopolitical crises can all shift the trajectory of an economy.

---

## 3. Measuring the Cycle

The **National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER)** officially dates U.S. recessions. A recession is commonly defined as two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth, though NBER uses a broader set of indicators — employment, income, industrial production, and sales — to make the call.

Since World War II, U.S. expansions have averaged about five years; recessions have averaged about eleven months. But outliers exist: the expansion from 2009 to 2020 lasted over a decade; the COVID recession of 2020 lasted only two months.

**Leading indicators** — signals that tend to move before the economy does. Examples: new housing permits, stock prices, consumer confidence surveys. Economists watch these to anticipate turning points.

**Lagging indicators** — signals that confirm what has already happened. Examples: unemployment rate, business loan rates. These lag because employers are slow to hire and fire.

---

## 4. Policy Responses

Governments and central banks use two main tools to smooth the cycle:

**Fiscal policy** — The government increases spending or cuts taxes during recessions to stimulate demand (expansionary fiscal policy). During booms, it may do the opposite to prevent overheating. The problem: fiscal decisions move slowly through the political process.

**Monetary policy** — The central bank (in the U.S., the Federal Reserve) lowers interest rates during recessions to encourage borrowing and investment, and raises them during expansions to cool inflation. Monetary policy can be adjusted quickly.

**Automatic stabilizers** — Programs that respond automatically without new legislation. Unemployment insurance pays out more during recessions, cushioning the blow to income. Tax receipts fall automatically when incomes drop, leaving more money in private hands.

---

## 5. Why the Cycle Matters for Real People

Business cycles aren't abstract. When a recession hits:

- Workers lose jobs, sometimes permanently in shrinking industries
- Small businesses close
- Retirement savings shrink if they're tied to stocks
- Government tax revenue falls while spending on safety nets rises, widening deficits

When expansions run long:
- Wages rise and workers have more bargaining power
- Businesses invest in new equipment and hire more
- Tax revenues grow, potentially reducing deficits

The cycle affects everyone, but it affects lower-income workers and those without savings most severely — they have the least cushion when things turn bad.

---

## Summary

The business cycle describes the recurring pattern of expansion, peak, contraction, and trough in economic activity. Causes range from demand shocks to credit cycles to external events. Policymakers use fiscal and monetary tools to smooth the ride, but cycles cannot be fully eliminated — only managed. Understanding the cycle helps make sense of economic news, job markets, and the logic behind government responses to downturns.
