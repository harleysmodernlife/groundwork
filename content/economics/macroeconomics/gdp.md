Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the most widely used measure of an economy's size — the total value of all goods and services produced in a country in a given period. Understanding what GDP measures, how it's calculated, and what it misses is foundational to reading economic news and evaluating economic policy.

---

## 1. What GDP Measures

**GDP** = the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given time period (usually a quarter or a year).

Key terms:
- **Market value**: GDP counts goods at their prices — a $50,000 car counts for more than a $5 haircut
- **Final goods and services**: Only the end product, not intermediate inputs (the steel in the car isn't counted separately from the car itself — to do so would double-count)
- **Within a country's borders**: GDP measures production in a geographic location, regardless of who owns the firm. A Japanese-owned factory in Ohio contributes to U.S. GDP.

---

## 2. How GDP Is Calculated

### The Expenditure Approach (most common)

**GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)**

- **C (Consumption)**: Household spending on goods and services — the largest component in most economies (about 70% in the U.S.)
- **I (Investment)**: Business spending on capital (equipment, buildings), residential construction, and inventory changes — not financial investment like buying stocks
- **G (Government Spending)**: Federal, state, and local government purchases of goods and services — *not* transfer payments (Social Security, welfare) which redistribute existing income
- **X - M (Net Exports)**: Exports minus imports; positive when a country exports more than it imports (trade surplus), negative when it imports more (trade deficit)

### Other Approaches
The **Income Approach** adds all income earned in producing output; the **Production Approach** sums value added at each stage of production. All three give the same result in theory.

---

## 3. Nominal vs. Real GDP

**Nominal GDP** is measured in current prices — it rises when either production increases or prices rise.

**Real GDP** adjusts for inflation, measuring actual production. Only real GDP tells you whether the economy is actually producing more.

**GDP Deflator** (and the related **CPI**) convert nominal to real GDP. If nominal GDP grew 5% but inflation was 3%, real GDP grew only 2%.

---

## 4. GDP Per Capita

Total GDP tells you the economy's size; **GDP per capita** (GDP divided by population) is a better measure of living standards.

A country of 1 billion people with a GDP of $2 trillion produces less per person than a country of 10 million people with a GDP of $300 billion.

GDP per capita is widely used to compare living standards across countries and over time — though it's an average that conceals inequality.

---

## 5. What GDP Doesn't Measure

GDP is useful but limited. It doesn't measure:

**Distribution**: GDP can rise while most people get poorer if gains are concentrated at the top. The median income may stagnate even as the average rises.

**Informal economy**: Unpaid work — childcare, caregiving, household labor — doesn't enter GDP, even though it's economically essential.

**Quality of life factors**: Health, life expectancy, education, leisure time, and safety are not captured in GDP.

**Environmental costs**: Cutting down a forest raises GDP (timber production) but doesn't subtract the loss of ecosystem services.

**Sustainability**: GDP doesn't distinguish between consuming capital (depleting resources) and productive growth.

**Negative activities**: Military spending, incarceration, and disaster recovery all raise GDP.

Alternative measures have been proposed: the **Human Development Index (HDI)** combines income with life expectancy and education; **Genuine Progress Indicator** adjusts for inequality and environmental costs; **Gross National Happiness** (Bhutan) attempts to measure wellbeing directly.

---

## 6. Interpreting GDP Growth

**GDP growth rates** (typically reported quarterly, annualized) tell you whether the economy is expanding or contracting.

- **Positive growth**: The economy is producing more
- **Negative growth** (two consecutive quarters): Recession
- **3–4% annual growth**: Historically strong for developed economies
- **7–10% growth**: Characteristic of rapidly developing economies like China in its high-growth phase

GDP growth is significant for employment — growing economies generally create jobs; contracting economies generally lose them.

---

## Summary

GDP measures the total value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders. It's calculated primarily as C + I + G + (X-M) — consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports. Real GDP (adjusted for inflation) is more meaningful than nominal GDP for tracking actual production. GDP per capita is a rough measure of average living standards. GDP is enormously useful for tracking economic size and cycles, but it misses distribution, unpaid work, environmental costs, and quality of life — metrics that require supplementary measures.
