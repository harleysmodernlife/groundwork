When the economy is struggling, what should the government do? One major answer is **fiscal policy** — the use of government spending and taxation to influence economic activity. Fiscal policy is one of the two main tools of macroeconomic stabilization (the other is monetary policy, handled by central banks).

---

## 1. What Is Fiscal Policy?

Fiscal policy involves decisions about:

- **Government spending** — how much the government spends on defense, infrastructure, social programs, education, and more
- **Taxation** — what tax rates apply to individuals and businesses, and what deductions or credits exist

These decisions affect aggregate demand — the total amount of spending in the economy. More government spending or lower taxes puts more money into the economy; less spending or higher taxes pulls money out.

---

## 2. Expansionary vs. Contractionary Fiscal Policy

**Expansionary fiscal policy** — used to stimulate a sluggish economy:
- Increase government spending (build roads, expand programs, send stimulus checks)
- Cut taxes (leave more money with consumers and businesses)
- Result: aggregate demand rises, output increases, unemployment may fall

**Contractionary fiscal policy** — used to cool an overheating economy or reduce debt:
- Decrease government spending
- Raise taxes
- Result: aggregate demand falls, inflation may ease

The choice depends on where the economy is in the business cycle and what problem policymakers are trying to solve.

---

## 3. The Multiplier Effect

A key Keynesian insight: government spending doesn't just affect the initial recipient — it ripples through the economy.

If the government hires workers to build a bridge, those workers spend their wages at local businesses, which hire more staff, who spend more wages, and so on. The total increase in economic activity is **larger** than the initial spending. This amplification is the **fiscal multiplier**.

The multiplier's size depends on how much people save vs. spend (the **marginal propensity to consume**). If people spend 80 cents of every new dollar, the multiplier is higher than if they save half.

In practice, multipliers vary by context. Spending on food stamps or unemployment benefits tends to have high multipliers (recipients spend immediately). Tax cuts for high earners have lower multipliers (more of it is saved).

---

## 4. Automatic Stabilizers

Not all fiscal policy requires active decisions. Some programs respond automatically to economic conditions:

- **Unemployment insurance** — payments rise when unemployment rises, supporting incomes during recessions without requiring new legislation
- **Progressive income taxes** — tax revenue falls automatically when incomes drop (because lower incomes face lower rates), leaving more money in the private economy
- **Food assistance programs** — enrollment expands automatically during hard times

These **automatic stabilizers** smooth the business cycle without the delays of the legislative process.

---

## 5. Deficits, Surpluses, and Debt

When the government spends more than it collects in taxes, it runs a **budget deficit** and must borrow — issuing bonds — to cover the gap. When it collects more than it spends, it runs a **surplus**.

Keynesian economists generally favor deficit spending during recessions (stimulus is worth borrowing for) and argue for surpluses or balance during booms. Critics worry that persistent deficits add to the national debt and impose costs on future generations.

The **national debt** is the cumulative total of past deficits (minus surpluses). For the U.S., this is measured in trillions of dollars. Its sustainability depends on interest rates, economic growth, and whether creditors continue to trust U.S. bonds.

---

## 6. Limitations of Fiscal Policy

**Lags** — Fiscal policy takes time. Congress must debate and pass legislation; spending must be appropriated and executed. By the time stimulus arrives, the recession may be easing.

**Crowding out** — Government borrowing can push up interest rates, making private borrowing more expensive and potentially offsetting some stimulus.

**Political constraints** — Tax cuts are popular; spending cuts are not. This asymmetry leads some to worry about a ratchet effect: deficits grow in downturns but don't shrink as much in expansions.

**Ricardian equivalence** — Some economists argue that rational consumers anticipate future tax increases to pay for current borrowing, and save the stimulus money rather than spending it — neutralizing the effect. Most economists see this as a theoretical concern, not a practical rule.

---

## Summary

Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxes to manage aggregate demand. Expansionary policy stimulates growth during recessions; contractionary policy cools inflation during booms. Automatic stabilizers work without legislation. Deficits allow governments to spend beyond their immediate revenues, but persistent borrowing raises long-term concerns. Fiscal policy is powerful but slow — which is why monetary policy often carries more of the short-run stabilization load.
