Inflation is the general, sustained rise in prices across the economy over time. Moderate inflation is considered normal and even healthy in modern economies; high or unpredictable inflation can be deeply disruptive. Understanding what causes inflation, how it's measured, and how central banks respond is essential for understanding macroeconomic policy.

---

## 1. What Inflation Is

**Inflation** is measured as the percentage change in a price index over time. The most common is the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** — which tracks the prices of a representative "basket" of goods and services that households typically buy.

*If the CPI was 100 in January and 103 in January the following year, inflation was 3%.*

**Deflation** (prices falling) sounds appealing but can be economically destructive — if prices are falling, consumers delay purchases (waiting for lower prices), businesses reduce production and lay off workers, and a downward spiral can ensue. Economists generally prefer low, stable inflation to deflation.

**Hyperinflation** — extremely rapid price increases — destroys an economy. Germany's Weimar Republic in the early 1920s and Zimbabwe in the 2000s are classic cases: prices doubled daily or weekly; savings became worthless; the currency lost all meaning.

---

## 2. Measuring Inflation

**Consumer Price Index (CPI)**: Tracks a basket of goods purchased by typical urban households. The **Core CPI** excludes food and energy prices (which are volatile) to give a cleaner picture of underlying inflation.

**Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE)**: The Federal Reserve's preferred measure; uses a different basket and methodology than the CPI. Generally runs slightly lower than CPI.

**Producer Price Index (PPI)**: Tracks prices at the wholesale level; can foreshadow consumer inflation.

## 3. Causes of Inflation

### Demand-Pull Inflation

"Too much money chasing too few goods." When aggregate demand exceeds the economy's productive capacity, prices rise. Causes:
- Rapid economic growth (consumers spending freely)
- Government stimulus spending
- Very loose monetary policy (low interest rates, high money supply growth)

### Cost-Push Inflation

Rising production costs push up prices. Causes:
- Rising wages (labor costs up → prices up)
- Rising commodity prices (especially oil — energy costs affect everything)
- Supply chain disruptions
- The 2021–2023 inflation was substantially cost-push, driven by supply chain disruptions, energy price increases (partly from Russia's invasion of Ukraine), and pandemic-related supply constraints.

### Built-In (Wage-Price Spiral)

Workers expect inflation and demand higher wages; businesses raise prices to cover higher wages; workers expect more inflation and demand higher wages. Self-reinforcing expectations can sustain inflation even after the initial cause has passed.

---

## 4. Effects of Inflation

**On borrowers**: Inflation helps borrowers — they repay loans in dollars worth less than when they borrowed. This is why moderate inflation is good for indebted households and governments.

**On savers/creditors**: Inflation hurts people holding fixed amounts of money or lending at fixed interest rates — the real value of their holdings declines.

**On workers**: Depends on whether wages keep up with inflation. If real wages (wages adjusted for inflation) fall, purchasing power declines.

**On uncertainty**: High and unpredictable inflation makes long-term planning difficult, discourages investment, and distorts economic signals — this is why even moderate inflation above 3-4% raises concerns.

**On fixed incomes**: Retirees on fixed pensions see purchasing power erode. (Social Security is indexed to inflation, mitigating this for Social Security recipients.)

---

## 5. The Federal Reserve and Inflation

The U.S. Federal Reserve has a **dual mandate**: maximum employment and stable prices (targeting ~2% inflation).

To combat high inflation, the Fed raises the **federal funds rate** — the interest rate at which banks lend to each other overnight. Higher rates:
- Make borrowing more expensive (consumer loans, mortgages, business credit)
- Reduce spending and investment
- Cool down demand
- Eventually reduce inflation — but at the cost of slower growth and potentially higher unemployment

To combat low inflation or recession, the Fed lowers rates — stimulating borrowing and spending.

The 2022–2023 inflation episode saw the Fed raise rates from near-zero to over 5% — the most aggressive tightening in decades — to bring inflation down from over 9% to near target.

---

## Summary

Inflation is the sustained general rise in prices, measured by indexes like the CPI. It is caused by demand exceeding supply (demand-pull), rising production costs (cost-push), or self-reinforcing expectations. Moderate inflation (around 2%) is considered normal and manageable; high or unpredictable inflation distorts economic decisions and erodes purchasing power, particularly for savers and fixed-income earners. Central banks use interest rate policy primarily to control inflation, accepting slower growth as the cost of reducing it.
