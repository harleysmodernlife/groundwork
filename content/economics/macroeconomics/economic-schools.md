How does an economy work? What causes recessions — and what should governments do about them? These questions have been debated for centuries, and different schools of economic thought have produced different, often competing answers. Understanding the major schools helps make sense of policy debates that otherwise seem like pure ideology.

---

## 1. Classical Economics

Classical economics emerged in the late 18th century with Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) and was refined by David Ricardo, John Stuart Mill, and others. Its core ideas:

- **Markets self-correct.** Supply and demand naturally find equilibrium. If wages are too high, unemployment rises; workers accept lower wages; employment recovers.
- **Say's Law**: "Supply creates its own demand." Producing goods generates the income needed to buy them; gluts don't persist.
- **Minimal government interference.** The invisible hand of the market allocates resources better than central planners.

Classical economics dominated until the Great Depression, when its predictions failed spectacularly. Mass unemployment persisted for years — markets weren't correcting.

---

## 2. Keynesian Economics

John Maynard Keynes responded to the Depression with his *General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936). His argument:

- **Aggregate demand drives output.** If consumers and businesses stop spending, the economy contracts — and may stay contracted. Markets don't automatically recover.
- **Government must step in.** During recessions, governments should increase spending (or cut taxes) to replace collapsed private demand. This is **fiscal stimulus**.
- **The paradox of thrift.** If everyone saves at once, total spending falls, output drops, and everyone is worse off — even though saving is rational for individuals.

Keynesian ideas shaped policy during and after World War II and the mid-20th century. The phrase "we are all Keynesians now" (often attributed to Nixon) captured how mainstream the approach became.

---

## 3. Monetarism

Milton Friedman and the Chicago School pushed back on Keynesianism in the 1960s and 70s:

- **Money supply is what matters.** Inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." If the central bank prints too much money, prices rise.
- **Government spending doesn't fix recessions.** It just crowds out private investment and distorts markets.
- **Rules, not discretion.** Central banks should follow a fixed rule for money growth rather than reacting to short-term conditions.

Monetarism gained influence when Keynesian policies couldn't explain stagflation (inflation + unemployment simultaneously) in the 1970s. Fed Chairman Paul Volcker applied monetarist ideas to break inflation in the early 1980s — at the cost of a severe recession.

---

## 4. Supply-Side Economics

Rising in the 1980s under Reagan and Thatcher, supply-side economics argued:

- **Cut taxes, especially on high earners and businesses.** This encourages investment, risk-taking, and growth that benefits everyone ("trickle-down").
- **Deregulate.** Government rules reduce efficiency and stifle entrepreneurship.
- **The Laffer Curve.** At very high tax rates, cutting taxes can actually increase revenue by expanding the economy.

Supply-side ideas remain politically influential, though many economists dispute the claim that tax cuts at current rates significantly boost growth or pay for themselves.

---

## 5. New Keynesian Economics

Modern mainstream macroeconomics blends Keynes with classical insights:

- **Prices and wages are "sticky."** They don't adjust instantly, so markets can stay out of equilibrium — justifying active monetary and sometimes fiscal policy.
- **Monetary policy is the first tool.** Central banks adjust interest rates to stabilize inflation and employment.
- **Microeconomic foundations matter.** Models are built on how rational individuals and firms behave.

Most central banks today — including the Federal Reserve — operate within a broadly New Keynesian framework.

---

## 6. Austrian Economics

The Austrian School (Ludwig von Mises, Friedrich Hayek) emphasizes:

- **Spontaneous order.** Markets coordinate information better than any planner can.
- **Business cycles are caused by credit expansion.** Artificially low interest rates cause "malinvestment" that must eventually be corrected.
- **Intervention makes things worse.** Government stimulus distorts price signals and delays necessary adjustment.

Austrian ideas enjoy popularity among libertarians and some conservatives but remain outside the mainstream of academic economics.

---

## 7. Behavioral Economics

A newer field (covered in more depth elsewhere) that imports psychology into economics:

- **People aren't fully rational.** They have biases, make predictable errors, and are influenced by how choices are framed.
- **Standard models fail to explain real behavior.** Insights from this school have influenced policy design, retirement savings programs, and nudge-based regulation.

---

## Summary

No single school has a monopoly on truth. Classical economics explains long-run efficiency; Keynesianism explains why recessions persist; monetarism focuses on price stability; behavioral economics explains irrational choices. Real policymakers draw on all of them. Knowing the schools helps you recognize which framework someone is using when they argue about taxes, deficits, or what the Fed should do next.
