Two institutions born from the 1944 Bretton Woods conference have shaped the global economy for eight decades: the **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** and the **World Bank**. They are often confused, but they serve distinct purposes, operate differently, and have generated very different debates about their impact on developing countries.

---

## 1. Origins: The Bretton Woods System

In July 1944, as World War II neared its end, representatives of 44 Allied nations met at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, to design the postwar international monetary order.

The goals: prevent a repeat of the interwar catastrophe — the Great Depression, competitive currency devaluations, trade wars, and economic nationalism that contributed to political extremism. The solution was international cooperation backed by institutions.

The Bretton Woods system established:
- Fixed exchange rates pegged to the U.S. dollar (itself pegged to gold at $35/ounce)
- The IMF to monitor exchange rates and provide emergency financing
- The World Bank (originally the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development) to fund postwar reconstruction and later development

The fixed exchange rate system collapsed in 1971 when Nixon ended dollar-gold convertibility. But the institutions remained and adapted.

---

## 2. The IMF: Lender of Last Resort

**Mission**: Promote international monetary cooperation, facilitate balanced trade, support financial stability, and provide resources to members in balance of payments difficulties.

**What the IMF does:**

*Surveillance* — Monitors global and national economies; publishes economic outlooks and risk assessments; advises governments on economic policy.

*Emergency lending* — When countries face balance of payments crises (can't finance imports or service external debt), the IMF provides loans. Recent major programs: Greece (2010), Argentina (multiple), Ukraine (2014, 2022), Pakistan, Sri Lanka.

*Technical assistance* — Helps developing countries strengthen tax administration, financial regulation, and economic statistics.

**How IMF loans work:**
- Countries with financial crises borrow from the IMF's pool of member contributions
- Loans come with **conditionality** — requirements to implement economic reforms (fiscal adjustment, monetary tightening, structural reforms)
- Voting power is proportional to quota contributions — the U.S. has the largest share and effective veto over major decisions

---

## 3. The World Bank: Development Finance

**Mission**: Reduce poverty and promote shared prosperity in developing countries.

**What the World Bank does:**

*Investment lending* — Finances infrastructure (roads, dams, power plants), health systems, education, agriculture, and urban development in developing countries.

*Policy-based lending* — Provides budget support conditional on policy reforms.

*Knowledge and research* — Publishes influential economic research and development data (including the World Development Indicators).

**World Bank Group structure:**
- *IBRD* — Lends to middle-income countries at favorable rates
- *IDA* — Provides grants and near-zero interest loans to the world's poorest countries
- *IFC* — Invests in private sector projects in developing countries
- *MIGA* — Political risk insurance for foreign investors

## 4. Controversies and Criticisms

Both institutions have been heavily criticized — often from opposite directions.

**Structural adjustment programs (SAPs)** — From the 1980s through the 1990s, the IMF and World Bank required countries receiving loans to implement the "Washington Consensus": privatization, deregulation, trade liberalization, fiscal austerity, and market-friendly reforms.

Critics (including Nobel laureate Joseph Stiglitz, former World Bank chief economist) argue SAPs:
- Required harsh austerity that hurt the poor (cutting health and education spending)
- Liberalized capital markets prematurely, creating vulnerability to crises
- Applied one-size-fits-all solutions without accounting for local conditions
- Prioritized creditor repayment over human welfare

Defenders argue SAPs addressed genuine economic imbalances and that countries resisting reforms often fared worse.

**Governance concerns** — Voting power weighted toward wealthy countries means the U.S. effectively controls the IMF (it's the only country with enough votes to block major decisions). Developing countries argue they have insufficient voice in institutions that primarily operate in the developing world.

**Environmental and social impacts** — World Bank projects (large dams, infrastructure) have been criticized for displacing communities, damaging ecosystems, and benefiting elites over the poor. The Bank has responded with increasingly rigorous environmental and social safeguards.

**Debt sustainability** — IMF loans must be repaid. Critics argue that emergency lending to countries in crisis can extend unsustainable debt burdens rather than restructuring them.

---

## 5. Reform and Evolution

Both institutions have adapted:
- Increased developing-country voice in governance (though reforms remain partial)
- Shifted away from rigid conditionality toward more flexible programs
- Expanded focus on climate change, gender equity, and inequality
- The World Bank has made climate finance a central priority
- The IMF created a Resilience and Sustainability Trust to help low-income countries facing climate and pandemic risks

---

## 6. Alternatives and Rivals

New institutions have emerged alongside the Bretton Woods twins:

- **New Development Bank** (BRICS bank) — Founded by Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa
- **Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB)** — China-led, financing Asian infrastructure
- **China's Belt and Road Initiative** — Direct bilateral lending for infrastructure globally

These alternatives have reduced the IMF/World Bank's monopoly on development finance and provided options for countries seeking financing without Washington Consensus conditions.

---

## Summary

The IMF provides emergency liquidity to countries in balance of payments crises, with conditions attached. The World Bank finances development investment in poorer countries. Both were created at Bretton Woods to support international economic stability and development. Both have been criticized — the IMF for imposing harsh austerity conditions, the World Bank for project impacts and governance — and both have evolved in response. The rise of alternative institutions has reshaped the global development finance landscape.
