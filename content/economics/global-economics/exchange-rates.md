When you travel abroad, you exchange your money for a foreign currency. The rate at which that exchange happens — how many euros your dollar buys, or how many yen your pound fetches — is the **exchange rate**. Exchange rates affect not just travelers but trade, investment, inflation, and the competitiveness of entire economies.

---

## 1. What Is an Exchange Rate?

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. If 1 U.S. dollar buys 0.92 euros, the dollar-euro exchange rate is 1.00/0.92 ≈ 1.09 (dollars per euro) or expressed the other way, 0.92 euros per dollar.

Exchange rates fluctuate constantly in foreign exchange markets (**forex markets**), which handle over $7 trillion in daily transactions — the largest financial market in the world.

---

## 2. Appreciation and Depreciation

When a currency **appreciates** (strengthens), it buys more of foreign currencies. A stronger dollar means:
- Imports are cheaper (foreign goods cost fewer dollars)
- Exports are more expensive for foreigners (they need more of their currency to buy dollars)
- U.S. goods become less competitive abroad

When a currency **depreciates** (weakens), it buys less of foreign currencies. A weaker dollar means:
- Imports cost more
- Exports are cheaper for foreign buyers
- U.S. goods become more competitive internationally

A country running a trade deficit might welcome currency depreciation because it makes exports more competitive and imports pricier, narrowing the gap.

---

## 3. What Determines Exchange Rates?

In floating exchange rate systems (like the U.S., EU, UK, Japan), rates are set by supply and demand in currency markets. Key drivers:

**Interest rates** — Higher interest rates attract foreign capital seeking returns. To invest in U.S. bonds, foreigners must buy dollars, increasing demand and raising the dollar's value. This is why Fed rate hikes typically strengthen the dollar.

**Inflation** — A country with higher inflation sees its purchasing power erode relative to trading partners. Over time, exchange rates adjust so that the same basket of goods costs the same across countries (**purchasing power parity**).

**Economic growth and investment attractiveness** — A strong economy with good investment prospects attracts foreign capital, increasing demand for its currency.

**Trade flows** — Countries with trade surpluses receive more foreign currency than they pay out, tending to push their currency up. (Though capital flows often swamp trade flows in modern markets.)

**Market expectations and speculation** — Traders anticipate future conditions. Expected changes in interest rates or growth can move exchange rates immediately.

**Political stability and confidence** — Currency crises often reflect lost confidence. Capital flees unstable countries; their currencies collapse.

---

## 4. Fixed, Floating, and Managed Exchange Rates

**Floating exchange rates** — Set by market supply and demand. Most major currencies (dollar, euro, yen, pound) float freely. Flexibility allows automatic adjustment to economic shocks.

**Fixed exchange rates** — A government pegs its currency to another (usually the dollar or euro). This provides stability for trade and reduces uncertainty, but requires the central bank to maintain the peg by buying or selling currency — and constrains monetary policy. Currency boards (like Hong Kong's) operate this way.

**Managed float** — Most currencies fall between the extremes: they float but central banks intervene occasionally to smooth volatility or resist excessive appreciation or depreciation. China historically managed the renminbi closely.

**Currency union** — Members share a single currency (the eurozone). No exchange rate risk within the union, but each member gives up independent monetary policy entirely.

---

## 5. Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)

**Purchasing power parity** is the idea that exchange rates should, in the long run, equalize the purchasing power of currencies. The **Big Mac Index** (from *The Economist*) is a playful illustration: if a Big Mac costs $6 in the U.S. and the equivalent of $4 in another country at current exchange rates, that currency is undervalued by 33% relative to PPP.

PPP exchange rates are used to compare incomes across countries. GDP at market exchange rates can be misleading: a dollar of income goes much further in India than in Norway. PPP-adjusted GDP accounts for these differences.

---

## 6. Currency Crises

Sometimes exchange rates collapse suddenly — a **currency crisis**. This typically happens when:

- A country has large external debts in foreign currency
- Reserves fall below the level needed to defend a peg
- Investors lose confidence and capital flees rapidly

Famous crises: Mexico (1994 "tequila crisis"), Asia (1997-98), Russia (1998), Argentina (2001), Turkey (2018). Each involved a combination of debt, fixed exchange rates, and sudden capital outflows.

The IMF often intervenes during currency crises with emergency loans, conditional on economic reforms — a controversial arrangement discussed elsewhere.

---

## 7. Exchange Rates and Everyday Life

Exchange rates affect people beyond currency exchange at airports:

- **Importers and exporters** must manage exchange rate risk. A U.S. exporter paid in euros faces uncertainty if the euro falls before payment.
- **Inflation**: Currency depreciation raises import prices, feeding through to consumer price levels.
- **Multinational earnings**: A U.S. company's foreign profits are worth less when converted to a stronger dollar.
- **Tourism**: Exchange rates affect where people travel and how much they spend.

---

## Summary

Exchange rates are prices of currencies determined by supply and demand in global forex markets. Appreciation strengthens a currency; depreciation weakens it. Interest rates, inflation, growth expectations, and capital flows drive exchange rate movements. Currencies can float freely, be fixed to another currency, or be managed. Purchasing power parity provides a long-run benchmark for exchange rate valuation. Currency crises occur when confidence collapses and capital flees. Exchange rates permeate everyday economic life — from import prices to tourism to corporate earnings.
